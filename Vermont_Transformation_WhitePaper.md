# Transforming Vermont's Health System

### A Six-Pillar White Paper on Vermont's Healthcare Transformation — Past, Present, and a Roadmap to 2040

*Prepared for Vermont policymakers, the Agency of Human Services, the Green Mountain Care Board, the Health Care Delivery Advisory Committee, hospital leaders, and the legislators who will hold this work accountable.*

*Companion to* Transforming American Healthcare *and the Health Transformation Review platform. April 2026.*

---

## Executive Summary

Vermont is attempting the most comprehensive state-level healthcare transformation in recent American history. It is doing so not by choice but by necessity: nine of fourteen hospitals reported operating losses in FY2023, the worst at −8.9%; absent structural change, Oliver Wyman projects thirteen of fourteen in losses by 2028 and a cumulative five-year deficit of $700 million to $2.4 billion. Average individual-market premiums rose 108% from 2018 to 2024 (from $456 to $948 per month) while median household income grew 22%. The population over 65 will exceed 30% by 2040 while the working-age, commercially insured base — the cross-subsidy that keeps rural hospitals solvent — shrinks 13%. Vermont is not an outlier. It is a preview: what Vermont must solve now, most other states will face on a ten-to-twenty-year lag.

This white paper makes three arguments.

**First, the crisis is structural, and structural crises require structural responses.** Vermont's decade of voluntary payment reform (the All-Payer ACO Model, OneCare Vermont, voluntary VITL connectivity) produced modest results because the highest-cost actors could opt out. Acts 167, 51, and 68 replaced voluntary reform with mandatory architecture: reference-based pricing (RBP) for commercial payers beginning FY2027, global hospital budgets beginning FY2028, and a binding December 2028 Statewide Health Care Delivery Strategic Plan.

**Second, transformation must be understood and managed through six interdependent pillars** — Policy, Technology, Economics, Clinical, Equity, and Operations. The dominant failure mode in American healthcare reform is single-pillar thinking: payment reform without clinical redesign, technology without operational capacity, equity as an afterthought. Vermont's reform agenda is unusual precisely because it touches all six pillars at once. But the pillars are sequenced, not simultaneous-and-equal: Policy and Technology must lead, Economics and Clinical follow, Equity constrains every decision, and Operations determines whether any of it is executed. Vermont's progress is uneven across the six — strong in Policy and Clinical, improving in Technology, mid-build in Economics, and most at risk in Equity and Operations.

**Third, the next thirty-six months are decisive, and the binding constraint is execution, not analysis.** The policy is largely correct and the capital is flowing — $195 million in federal Rural Health Transformation (RHT) Program funds received in December 2025, plus up to ~$150 million per year in AHEAD EAST Fund Medicare investment. What is not yet built is the organizational machinery: a restructured AHS operating as a population-health system operator, a joint AHS–GMCB analytics capability, a Clinically Integrated Network (CIN) for shared services, and the project-management infrastructure to coordinate a 19-component transformation portfolio against a single December 2028 deadline. The federal environment has also turned hostile: H.R. 1 (the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, signed July 4, 2025) imposes $911 billion in Medicaid cuts over ten years, front-loading a temporary rural fund against back-loaded permanent revenue reductions.

The roadmap in Section 4 sets out, pillar by pillar, what Vermont must do between 2026 and 2030 to reach a sustainable steady state by 2035 and to be positioned for the demographic peak of 2040. The central recommendation is simple to state and hard to do: **build the execution capacity — the restructured agency, the analytics, the CIN, the PMO — fast enough to meet the statutory deadlines, and make the December 2028 plan a binding commitment to a quantified future state rather than a descriptive document.**

## 1. The Transformation Imperative: Why Vermont, Why Now

On September 18, 2024, the Green Mountain Care Board convened a public meeting at which Oliver Wyman presented a year-long, $1 million statewide hospital-systems analysis commissioned under Act 167. The findings were not surprising to anyone paying attention, but they were stated with a precision that made them impossible to dismiss. Vermont's hospital system is on a trajectory toward financial failure, driven by four converging forces:

- **Demographic aging.** The 65-and-older share of Vermont's population will exceed 30% by 2040, up from 21.7% in 2020 — a 57% increase in the elderly population. The working-age population falls 13%. An older population means more Medicare and Medicaid (both of which reimburse below cost) and fewer commercially insured patients to subsidize the difference.
- **Hospital financial fragility.** Nine of fourteen hospitals lost money in FY2023; the Critical Access Hospitals (CAHs) — the smallest, most rural, least substitutable facilities — are the most distressed, with operating profit of $938 per adjusted discharge against a $2,784 benchmark.
- **The unsustainability of commercial cross-subsidy.** Vermont hospitals charge commercial insurers 250–300% of Medicare on average (UVMMC reached ~417% of Medicare for outpatient services in 2022). Those prices flow directly into premiums — a 108% rise over six years — and the shrinking commercial base can no longer carry the load.
- **Administrative complexity and workforce exhaustion.** UVMMC's administrative cost per adjusted discharge was $3,826 against a $1,427 national benchmark. A projected 370-FTE primary-care shortage by 2030 compounds a housing market (3% rental vacancy; second-highest per-capita homelessness in the country) that makes recruitment and retention difficult.

Oliver Wyman distilled the response into three imperatives: **(1) build housing and fix transportation; (2) pay PPS hospitals with reference-based pricing and move to global budgets; and (3) move all care possible out of hospitals.** These are not incremental recommendations. They are structural prescriptions for a system that, absent structural change, is heading toward collapse — not through orderly redesign but through unplanned closures.

Vermont has three assets that make a structural response feasible where it would not be in most states: a small, transparent, high-capacity state government; a hospital sector that is non-profit and community-governed (no investor-owned chains, no shareholder-value obligations); and the Green Mountain Care Board — a five-member independent regulator created in 2011 with the authority not merely to review hospital budgets but to *set* them. That regulatory authority is the structural condition that lets Vermont make mandatory what other states can only encourage.

## 2. The Six-Pillar Framework

Every serious healthcare-transformation framework of the past thirty years has named the same necessary conditions: pay differently, redesign care, build data infrastructure, address social determinants, ensure equity, execute operationally. The list is correct. The problem is treating it as a list. A checklist implies independent items that can be addressed in any order, where partial completion is partial progress. The six pillars are not independent items — they are a system of interdependent components in which each pillar's effectiveness depends on the others, the failure of any one cascades through the rest, and the *order* of intervention matters as much as the content.

| Pillar | Diagnostic question | Structural role | Vermont's instruments |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Policy** | Is it permissible? | The mandatory architecture. Converts aspirational reform into binding requirements so the highest-cost actors cannot opt out. | Acts 167/51/68; AHEAD State Agreement; the Statewide Strategic Plan mandate. |
| **Technology** | Is it possible? | The data substrate. Makes population-health management, VBC execution, equity measurement, and planning analytically feasible. | VHCURES (all-payer claims); VITL/VHIE; the joint AHS–GMCB analytics vendor; the CIN; AI-scribe grants. |
| **Economics** | Is it sustainable? | The incentive architecture. Determines whether organizations have a financial reason to behave differently. | Reference-based pricing (FY2027); global budgets (FY2028–2030); AHEAD total-cost-of-care accountability; the EAST Fund. |
| **Clinical** | Is it effective? | The mechanism of change. Where payment reform becomes patient outcomes — or fails to. | Blueprint for Health (PCMH); Collaborative Care Model; CCBHC expansion; primary-care investment. |
| **Equity** | Is it just? | The cross-cutting constraint applied to every other pillar. Transformation that improves averages while widening disparities has failed. | Geographic equity across 14 HSAs; SDOH investment; rural-access protection in RBP/budget design; Act 167/68 equity reporting. |
| **Operations** | Is it executable? | The execution layer. Translates mandates, payment models, data, and clinical programs into organizational reality. | 14-hospital transformation planning; AHS restructuring; the HSA-Coordinator model; the Division of Planning and Effectiveness; the PMO. |

### The dependency logic that makes sequencing non-optional

Five dependencies form the structural core, and they explain why Vermont sequences its reforms as it does:

1. **Policy → Economics (the foundational dependency).** Payment reform that is voluntary produces voluntary results. Vermont spent a decade under OneCare's voluntary ACO and produced modest results among participants and none among those who opted out. Act 68's *mandatory* RBP and global budgets eliminate the opt-out. Without the Policy pillar's mandatory authority, the Economics pillar stays aspirational.
2. **Economics → Clinical.** Payment reform changes the financial incentive; clinical redesign changes the care model that responds to it. A hospital under a global budget has an incentive to prevent hospitalizations — but can only act on it if it has the primary-care network, care coordination, and behavioral-health integration to keep patients out of the hospital.
3. **Technology → everything.** You cannot attribute patients for global budgets without accurate matching; cannot identify care gaps without population data; cannot model service-line changes without analytics; cannot monitor equity without stratified data. Technology is the substrate on which every other pillar's management functions depend.
4. **Equity as constraint.** Every payment, clinical, and technology decision must be evaluated for its equity impact *before* it is finalized — does it reduce disparities or widen them? Equity is not the last pillar in the sequence; it is the lens applied throughout.
5. **Operations as translation.** A strategy that is analytically sound, financially compelling, technically feasible, and clinically validated still fails if the implementing organization lacks the project-management infrastructure, change-management capability, and sustained leadership attention to execute it.

The practical implication for Vermont: **Technology must precede Economics-as-management** (you cannot manage global budgets you cannot measure), **Policy must precede Economics-as-mandate** (voluntary reform fails), **Equity must be designed in from the start** (retrofitting equity into a finished RBP methodology is too late), and **Operations is the binding constraint** that determines whether the other five produce change or merely documents.

## 3. Vermont's Transformation Across the Six Pillars: Past, Present, and Trajectory

This section assesses where Vermont stands on each pillar — what was true in 2022 (before Act 167), what is true in early 2026, and what the trajectory and risks are. The pattern is consistent: Vermont's *policy* and *clinical* foundations are among the strongest in the country, its *technology* and *economics* are mid-build, and its *equity* and *operations* pillars carry the most risk.

### 3.1 Policy — Strongest pillar; the mandatory architecture is built

**Past (2022):** Payment reform was voluntary. The Vermont All-Payer ACO Model and OneCare Vermont allowed the highest-cost providers to decline participation; the "all-payer" system did not include all payers. There was no comprehensive system diagnostic, no statewide strategic plan, no all-payer federal agreement, and no dedicated transformation capital.

**Present (2026):** Vermont has assembled the most complete state-level reform mandate in the country through a deliberate **reform cascade** — Act 167 (2022) established the diagnosis (commissioning the Oliver Wyman analysis through a process of 230+ meetings and 3,100+ participants), Act 51 (2023) built institutional capacity, and Act 68 (2025) deployed the mandatory requirements: RBP for commercial payers beginning FY2027, global budgets for non-CAH hospitals beginning FY2028 and all hospitals by FY2030, and a binding Statewide Strategic Plan due December 1, 2028. The AHEAD State Agreement (signed January 17, 2025) commits Vermont to a nine-year all-payer total-cost-of-care model beginning January 1, 2027, with EAST Fund capital and explicit preservation of GMCB's rate-setting authority.

**Trajectory and risk:** The architecture is durable — federal agreements require CMS consent to unwind, and the reform cascade has created institutions (the Health Care Delivery Advisory Committee, expanded GMCB authority) that are constituencies for their own continuation. The exposure is not repeal but **erosion through the regulatory process**: an RBP methodology set so close to current commercial rates that little repricing occurs, or global budgets set at levels requiring no behavioral change. Hospital lobbying will target the methodology, not the mandate. The 2026 governor's race and the December 2028 plan's scope are the watch items.

### 3.2 Technology — Improving fast, but a two-year data lag limits real-time management

**Past (2022):** VHCURES, Vermont's all-payer claims database, was operational but ran on a two-year data lag. VITL, the health information exchange, was operational but voluntary and incompletely adopted. FHIR compliance was minimal. There was no AI-governance framework and incomplete race/ethnicity data in VHCURES.

**Present (2026):** VHCURES remains solid and already enables analyses most states cannot conduct, but the lag persists — the November 2025 AHS report acknowledged that most available measures reflect 2023 or earlier data. The **joint AHS–GMCB analytics-vendor procurement** (awarded to McKinsey, 2025–2026) is the most urgent near-term investment: it must produce a platform capable of modeling transformation scenarios, evaluating hospital proposals, and generating the outcome metrics the Strategic Plan requires. The **Vermont CIN** is in design, intended to give all 14 hospitals shared analytics, IT, and AI-governance capability that small CAHs (with limited IT staff) cannot individually afford. RHT IT-advance grants are funding FHIR-capable upgrades at smaller facilities, and AI-scribe grants are expanding effective provider capacity.

**Trajectory and risk:** Technology is the fastest-improving pillar nationally, and Vermont's CIN-based shared-services model is a sound answer to the small-hospital capacity gap. The risks are concrete: the analytics vendor must be **operational by January 2027** or hospitals enter the AHEAD performance year without financial-management capability (this is the portfolio's highest-scored risk); VITL connectivity gaps must close or AHEAD population-health management is incomplete; race/ethnicity data quality must improve or the Equity pillar cannot see the disparities it is meant to close; and AI deployment is outpacing governance, creating patient-safety and equity risk if tools trained on national commercial data perform differently for the Northeast Kingdom's elderly, rural, Medicaid population.

### 3.3 Economics — Mid-build; mandatory reform enacted, mechanics being designed

**Past (2022):** Hospitals were rewarded for volume under fee-for-service. Commercial-to-Medicare price ratios ran 250–300%+. Nine of fourteen hospitals were heading into losses, with a projected $700M–$2.4B five-year deficit. There were no global budgets and minimal commercial VBC.

**Present (2026):** RBP is enacted (FY2027) with methodology under design; global budgets are mandated (FY2028 non-CAH, FY2030 all). The AHEAD Medicaid global budget is already operational (January 2026), GMCB has begun applying a −1% commercial-rate benchmark ($94.58M in FY2026 reductions ordered), and Act 55's drug-pricing cap produced $104.3M in FY2026 savings. The EAST Fund (up to ~$150M/year in additional Medicare funds) is the capital that directs investment toward the primary care, behavioral health, home health, and long-term care that make global budgets produce population-health improvement rather than just cost reduction.

**Trajectory and risk:** This pillar's success depends entirely on the methodology and the levels — the substance GMCB sets, not the mandates already enacted. Two design questions are decisive: **social risk adjustment** in global budgets (without it, hospitals serving high-SDOH populations like Essex County are penalized) and **the RBP glide path** (too aggressive risks destabilizing fragile CAHs; too gentle produces no repricing). The federal overlay is the EAST Fund's exposure to H.R. 1 Medicaid cuts: a 30%+ EAST Fund reduction would constrain the Clinical-pillar capacity that makes global budgets viable, and HTR assesses this as a moderate-to-high probability.

### 3.4 Clinical — A national-leading foundation; behavioral health is the swing variable

**Past (2022):** Vermont entered transformation with a genuine clinical asset: the Blueprint for Health, an all-payer PCMH program with ~90% of primary-care providers participating and a documented 5.8:1 return on investment ($5.8M in reduced medical expenditure per $1M invested in primary care) — among the most robust ROI findings in the VBC literature. Behavioral-health follow-up and CCBHC infrastructure were weaker: 76% 30-day follow-up after mental-health ED visits, 68% after SUD ED visits, and zero certified CCBHCs.

**Present (2026):** Blueprint participation remains at 90%+ and is transitioning to Primary Care AHEAD (enhanced Medicare primary-care payments replacing the expiring demonstration). The Collaborative Care Model (CoCM) and the Mental Health Integration (MHI) pilot are effective but not yet permanently funded. CCBHC certification is in progress with five new entities targeted for July 2026. The PACE/long-term-care gap and primary-care productivity constraints persist.

**Trajectory and risk:** Vermont's Blueprint-plus-AHEAD primary-care investment is the strongest in the country, and the clinical logic is proven by Vermont's own data. The critical variable is **behavioral-health integration sustainability** — the MHI pilot needs permanent EAST Fund financing, and the CoCM Medicare billing codes (CPT 99492/99493/99494) are the mechanism that can make it durable. The workforce constraint (370-FTE primary-care gap) is best addressed not purely by headcount but by team-based care models that let providers work at the top of their licensure with minimized administrative burden.

### 3.5 Equity — Highest-risk pillar; gaps documented but not yet closing

**Past (2022):** Vermont's aggregate numbers were strong — 91% statewide primary-care access (4 points above the national benchmark), 97% insurance coverage — but the aggregates hid the disparities: an 11-point white/BIPOC primary-care access gap (79–81% for BIPOC adults), 8% uninsurance in Essex County against 3% statewide, and the Northeast Kingdom's elevated chronic-disease and behavioral-health-crisis burden. There was no systematic HSA-level equity measurement and minimal SDOH screening.

**Present (2026):** The disparities are now documented and largely unchanged. The HEROI equity-scoring framework has been developed and scoring is underway; Blueprint HRSN (health-related social needs) screening reaches 60%+ of practices; and social-risk-adjustment methodology for global budgets is in design as an active policy question. The GLP-1 access gap is the most consequential new equity issue: Vermont's Medicaid population has exactly the obesity, diabetes, and cardiovascular profile where GLP-1 efficacy is best demonstrated, but near-term budget pressure makes coverage difficult; the BALANCE Model (CMS, December 2025) and IRA price negotiation (semaglutide to ~$274/month effective 2027) are the levers.

**Trajectory and risk:** This is the pillar most at risk of moving backward. H.R. 1's Medicaid cuts fall disproportionately on the low-income and rural populations whose gaps are already widest; federal equity-program funding has been reduced (VDH reported $7M retracted). Without explicit GLP-1 coverage expansion, sustained SDOH investment, and deliberate Northeast Kingdom access protection built into the RBP and global-budget design, Vermont's equity gaps will widen as coverage contracts for the populations with the greatest need. **Equity must be a co-equal design input to the Economics pillar, not a downstream review.**

### 3.6 Operations — The binding constraint; the agency is not yet built for the job

**Past (2022):** AHS operated as a program-silo organization — structured around funding streams and program types (Medicaid, mental health, substance use, aging, developmental services, Blueprint, health care reform) rather than around populations or the geographic communities they live in. There was no systematic hospital-transformation process, minimal transformation-PMO capacity, no Vermont CIN, and fragmented EMS (12+ separate rural agencies).

**Present (2026):** All 14 hospitals are engaged in transformation planning (with the RHRC engagement and a successor function), and the January 2026 plans document 148 operational initiatives with named owners and timelines. PMO establishment is underway, the HSA-Coordinator model is being deployed, the CIN is in development, and AHS has received $195M in RHT capital. Two reports to the legislature (August and November 2025) have been delivered under Act 68's monthly reporting requirement.

**Trajectory and risk:** Oliver Wyman identified four structural gaps that the restructuring must close — the program-silo misalignment with Hospital Service Areas, the absence of a Planning and Effectiveness analytical function, the under-resourcing of the transformation-management function, and fragmented SDOH integration. AHS's own November 2025 report acknowledged it "lacks a dedicated analytical unit" and that "transformation activities are sequenced by capacity rather than by strategic priority." **This is the pillar that will determine whether the other five produce a transformed system or a partial one.** The single most likely cause of plan failure is inadequate project-management infrastructure for a 19-component portfolio converging on one December 2028 deadline.

### Where Vermont stands — pillar scorecard at a glance

| Pillar | Foundation | Build status (2026) | Primary risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Policy | National-leading | Mandatory architecture enacted | Regulatory-process erosion; 2026 election |
| Technology | Solid base (VHCURES) | Mid-build; analytics vendor + CIN in progress | Vendor not operational by Jan 2027; AI governance gap |
| Economics | Distressed → reforming | Mandates enacted; mechanics in design | Methodology/levels; EAST Fund exposure to Medicaid cuts |

## 4. The Future State: A Roadmap to 2040

Oliver Wyman provided the *diagnostic* scenario — without transformation, 13 of 14 hospitals in losses by 2028, unsustainable premiums, and contraction through unplanned closures. The defining gap in Vermont's current transformation discourse is the absence of an equally specific *alternative* scenario. The December 2028 Strategic Plan must close that gap: it must commit to a quantified trajectory for what Vermont's system will look like in 2031, 2035, and 2040, with the financial modeling to show it is achievable. This section provides the framework for that commitment — first the target steady state, then the phased roadmap, then the pillar-by-pillar actions.

### 4.1 The target steady state (2035)

By 2035, a successfully transformed Vermont health system should exhibit the following characteristics:

- **A rationalized, sustainable hospital network** of roughly 10–12 financially sustainable facilities plus 2–4 facilities converted to Rural Emergency Hospital (REH) or comparable focused-scope models, organized in a three-tier structure (Regional Specialty Centers, Community Hospitals, and converted/focused-scope facilities) with Centers of Excellence concentrating specialty volume where it can sustain expertise.
- **All-payer global budgets in steady-state operation**, with 13 of 14 hospitals within a sustainable operating-margin range, the Oliver Wyman deficit trajectory reversed, and per-capita cost growth below GDP growth.
- **Commercial premium growth below 5% annually**, with RBP and global-budget savings flowing through to premiums under Act 68's passthrough requirement — easing the affordability crisis that is the defining economic anxiety of working Vermont families.
- **Primary care as a true population-health function**, with 100% PCMH participation, Primary Care AHEAD fully deployed, behavioral-health integration (CoCM) at the great majority of practices, and the primary-care FTE gap reduced from 370 to under 200 through team-based care and telehealth.
- **Equity gaps measurably narrowing** — the white/BIPOC primary-care access gap reduced from 11 points to under 6, Northeast Kingdom uninsurance below 5%, and disparities tracked transparently by HSA through HEROI scoring and social-risk-adjusted budgets.
- **An AHS that operates as a population-health system operator** — organized geographically around Hospital Service Areas, equipped with an internal analytics and planning capability, and managing transformation as a coordinated portfolio rather than a collection of projects.

### 4.2 The phased roadmap

**Phase 1 — Build the machinery (2026–2027).** The defining task of this phase is execution capacity. Stand up the analytics vendor (operational by January 2027), the CIN, the AHS PMO and Portfolio Manager, the HSA-Coordinator model, and the Division of Planning and Effectiveness. Finalize the RBP methodology and the global-budget design — including social risk adjustment — with equity as a co-equal design input. Launch AHEAD Cohort 2 and Primary Care AHEAD (January 2027). Certify five CCBHCs (July 2026). Make the BALANCE/GLP-1 coverage decision. *Success in this phase is infrastructural: the question is whether the agency and its data capability exist in time to manage what FY2027–FY2028 demands.*

**Phase 2 — Operate and prove (2028–2030).** Bring non-CAH global budgets online (FY2028) and complete coverage including CAHs (FY2030). Deliver the December 2028 Strategic Plan as a *binding* commitment with a quantified 2031/2035/2040 trajectory. Demonstrate the first concrete results — slowing premium growth, stabilizing hospital margins, reduced avoidable hospitalizations, narrowing equity gaps — and document them in the monthly reports that protect the architecture politically. Deploy the rationalized hospital tier structure with REH/CACC conversions paired with RHT-funded EMS and telehealth so that consolidation does not become inaccessibility. *Success in this phase is evidentiary: Vermont must show the mandatory model works before the political window narrows and before the back-loaded Medicaid cuts arrive.*

**Phase 3 — Absorb the federal shock and reach steady state (2030–2035).** Use the efficiency gains built in Phases 1–2 to absorb the post-2030 Medicaid revenue reductions through productivity rather than service cuts. Complete the workforce build-out. Reach the 2035 steady state. *Success in this phase is resilience: whether the structural changes are deep enough that Vermont weathers the expiration of the RHT fund and the acceleration of the Medicaid cuts.*

**Phase 4 — Meet the demographic peak (2035–2040).** Scale dementia-care infrastructure, PACE programs, and HCBS capacity for the frailty/multi-morbidity phase of the Baby Boom curve. Update the Strategic Plan on its three-year cycle. Serve as the documented national template other states adopt as they reach Vermont's current condition. *Success in this phase is durability and transferability.*

## 5. Making It Executable: Govern the Transformation as a Portfolio

The roadmap above will not execute itself, and the historical record of healthcare reform is a record of correct plans that produced documents rather than change. Vermont's transformation is — in the precise sense of the discipline — a **portfolio**: a structured collection of 19 interdependent programs and projects across the six pillars, converging on a single December 2028 deadline, with competing resource demands and shared dependencies. It must be managed as one.

**The core recommendation:** establish, by Q1 2027, a transformation Project Management Office within AHS, led by a dedicated **Portfolio Manager** (PMI Portfolio Management Professional credential), reporting to the AHS Secretary with a GMCB co-reporting relationship and budget authority over the PMO. Below the Portfolio Manager, designate a Program Manager for each of the six pillars. The Portfolio Manager maintains the integrated master schedule, the portfolio risk register, and the benefits-realization tracking that ties every component to one of Act 167's five statutory goals; resolves the cross-component resource conflicts that the November 2025 AHS report already identifies as a structural constraint; and manages portfolio-level stakeholder relationships with the HCAC, GMCB, hospital CEOs, the legislature, and CMS.

The business case is overwhelming. PMI's research finds that organizations with mature project-management practices waste dramatically less than those without it; a 10% improvement in delivery efficiency on Vermont's $195M RHT investment alone exceeds the full multi-year cost of a staffed PMO. The total portfolio-management investment (~$4.5–7.5M over four to five years) is under 2% of the portfolio value it protects — against the alternative of the partial-transformation scenario, which is a multi-billion-dollar outcome: the continuation of the deficit trajectory, the failure of 13 of 14 hospitals to reach sustainability, and the loss of the political window the mandatory architecture has opened.

The highest-priority portfolio risks to manage actively:

- **Analytics vendor not operational by January 2027** (hospitals enter AHEAD without financial-management capability) — the highest-scored risk.
- **Portfolio-management infrastructure not established before the 2027 deadlines** (the transformation managed as a collection of projects) — the risk this recommendation is designed to avoid.
- **A descriptive rather than binding December 2028 Strategic Plan** produced by AHS overcommitment.
- **EAST Fund reduced 30%+ by federal Medicaid cuts**, constraining the Clinical and Equity pillars.
- **Social risk adjustment not adopted before FY2028 budgets**, penalizing safety-net hospitals.

## 6. The Federal Headwind — and Why It Sharpens the Case

The federal environment has turned sharply against Vermont's effort. H.R. 1 (the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, signed July 4, 2025) imposes an estimated $911 billion in Medicaid spending reductions over ten years — the largest single reduction in federal health-program spending in American history — producing roughly 10 million additional uninsured Americans by 2034, with Medicaid work requirements beginning in late 2026, six-month eligibility redeterminations, provider-tax caps, and state-directed-payment restrictions. The same law created the $50 billion Rural Health Transformation Program ($10B/year, FY2026–FY2030) from which Vermont received $195 million in December 2025.

The arithmetic is unforgiving and asymmetric: the rural fund is **temporary and front-loaded** (ending 2030), while nearly two-thirds of the Medicaid reductions are **back-loaded after 2030** with no offsetting capital. Federal guidance further constrains the fund to technology and new activities — not backfilling provider losses — and caps direct provider payments at 15% of a state's award. It is an infrastructure fund, not a revenue replacement.

This does not weaken Vermont's transformation case; it sharpens it. The honest question every RHT-receiving state faces is whether it can use the one-time capital to build a system efficient enough to survive the permanent revenue reductions that arrive after the fund expires. Vermont is one of the few states well-positioned to answer yes — because it had structural reform plans in place *before* the RHT Program existed, is treating the capital as pre-planned transformation rather than operating support, and has the regulatory and governance infrastructure to execute systematically. AHS Secretary Samuelson's framing is the correct one and should govern every spending decision: *the RHT funding is a tool for executing reform, not a substitute for reform.* Executed well, the $195 million buys the efficiency (AI scribes, remote monitoring, broadband, EMS regionalization, shared CIN services) that lets Vermont absorb the post-2030 Medicaid cuts through productivity rather than service cuts. Executed poorly — as operating support for unsustainable operations — Vermont meets the 2030–2031 cliff without the structural improvements needed to survive it.

---

## 7. Conclusion — What Vermont Is Proving

Vermont's transformation is not yet a success story; it is a system in mid-flight. As of April 2026, reference-based pricing has not taken effect, global budgets are still being designed, the Strategic Plan has not been written, the analytics vendor has not delivered, and the Northeast Kingdom hospitals remain fragile. But Vermont has committed to structural change with sufficient specificity, statutory authority, and capital that the transformation has a credible path to completion — more than any comparable American state has done.

The six-pillar framework explains both why Vermont can attempt this and where it is most exposed. The Policy and Clinical pillars are national-leading; the Technology and Economics pillars are mid-build and on track if the analytics capability and the budget mechanics land on schedule; the Equity and Operations pillars carry the most risk — equity because the federal Medicaid cuts fall hardest on the populations whose gaps are already widest, and operations because the agency that must execute all of this was built for a different era and is not yet rebuilt for the job.

The work that remains is therefore not primarily analytical. The evidence base for what must change is strong and consistent. The binding constraints are organizational capacity, political will, and execution discipline: building the restructured agency, the analytics, the CIN, and the PMO fast enough to meet the statutory deadlines, and converting the December 2028 plan from a description into a binding commitment to a quantified future state. Vermont's statutory timeline — the December 2028 deadline, the mandatory Act 68 milestones, the nine-year AHEAD performance period — is the mechanism that converts transformation from a priority that can be deferred into a deadline that cannot. Whether that accountability is enforced will determine the outcome.

If Vermont succeeds, it will have produced, in public and with detailed documentation, the answers to the questions every state will eventually face: whether mandatory all-payer price regulation can reduce hospital prices without closures; whether global budgets can shift hospital behavior toward population health; whether a state agency can restructure itself into a population-health system operator; and whether a rural state can use federal capital to build infrastructure that survives the capital's expiration. Those answers — evidence rather than argument — may be the most valuable contribution Vermont makes to American healthcare in a generation. Vermont's significance is not that it is large or powerful. It is that it is early. What Vermont proves now, the rest of the country will need to know soon.
