### **The SASH Program — Housing-Based Care for Vermont's Aging Population**

Vermont's most operationally mature response to the aging care imperative is not a clinical program — it is a housing-based one. The Support and Services at Home (SASH) program, launched in 2011 under the Blueprint for Health infrastructure, is a nationally recognized care coordination model that deploys health teams inside affordable housing communities to serve older Vermonters and people with disabilities on Medicare.

SASH operates in more than 200 affordable housing communities across all 14 Vermont counties, serving more than 13,000 participants. Each participating housing community is assigned a part-time Wellness Nurse and a full-time SASH Coordinator who conduct individualized health assessments, build care plans, provide one-on-one chronic disease coaching, coordinate care transitions, screen for SDOH barriers, and connect residents to community resources including 2-1-1 Vermont, meals programs, transportation assistance, and benefits enrollment.

The program's outcomes are documented and significant. Commonwealth Fund and Center for Health Care Strategies (CHCS) evaluations demonstrate that SASH participants have lower rates of hospitalization, fewer emergency department visits, lower per-member-per-year Medicare spending, and delayed nursing home placement compared to matched controls in similar affordable housing without SASH. These results address the precise clinical imperative Vermont's aging demographics create: keeping elderly Vermonters healthy, connected, and out of expensive institutional settings.

SASH is Vermont's proof of concept for the care-where-people-live model that global budget incentives require. Under fee-for-service payment, the program's value is invisible — it prevents the hospitalizations that generate revenue. Under a global budget, avoided hospitalization is the explicit objective. SASH is therefore not just a social services program; it is a clinical delivery strategy aligned with Vermont's payment reform architecture in a way that few other programs are. Vermont's Statewide Health Care Delivery Strategic Plan must explicitly position SASH as a scalable infrastructure asset for the aging population strategy, not merely a housing amenity.

The HTR platform's **Vermont SASH Program** page provides the full program description, eligibility criteria, service model, outcomes data, and connection to the platform's population health and equity tools.

### **Vermont's Designated Agencies — The Community Behavioral Health Infrastructure**

Vermont's Hub and Spoke SUD system operates within a broader behavioral health infrastructure anchored by the state's 11 Designated Agencies (DAs) — regionally designated non-profit organizations under contract with the Vermont Department of Mental Health (DMH) and the Department of Disabilities, Aging, and Independent Living (DAIL) to deliver community-based mental health, substance use disorder, and developmental disability services across defined geographic catchment areas.

The DA system covers every county in Vermont. Agencies include Howard Center (Chittenden County, the state's largest DA serving greater Burlington), Washington County Mental Health Services (Montpelier/Barre area), Northeast Kingdom Human Services (NKHS, covering the three-county NEK — Vermont's most rural and underserved region), and eight others spanning the remaining counties. Each DA operates crisis services, outpatient mental health, community rehabilitation, emergency designated receiving facilities, and community developmental disability services under a statutory service obligation that cannot be refused based on ability to pay.

The DA system is Vermont's primary mechanism for ensuring access to behavioral health services in the communities where crisis, chronic mental illness, and complex SUD intersect with poverty, rural geography, and housing instability. The Blueprint's Community Health Teams (CHTs) and the DA crisis infrastructure are the two institutional anchors that keep high-complexity, high-cost behavioral health patients connected to community-based care rather than cycling through emergency departments and inpatient psychiatric units.

Vermont's transformation agenda under Acts 167 and 68 depends on the financial and operational sustainability of the DA system. Several DAs operate with thin margins, limited ability to recruit and retain behavioral health clinicians in a tight rural workforce market, and Medicaid rate structures that do not fully cover the cost of the mandated service obligation. The AHS restructuring process must address DA sustainability explicitly — the Hub and Spoke model's effectiveness at keeping SUD patients in community-based treatment depends on the Spokes (primary care OBOT practices) having reliable DA hub infrastructure to refer to when patients need a higher level of care.

The HTR platform's **Vermont Designated Agencies** page provides the full list of all 11 agencies with their regional coverage, service scope, sustainability profiles, and connections to the Vermont behavioral health transformation agenda.

### **Vermont Chronic Care Initiative (VCCI) — Intensive Case Management for the Highest-Risk Members**

Vermont's most intensive population health management program is the Vermont Chronic Care Initiative (VCCI), operated by the Department of Vermont Health Access (DVHA) as the primary care management program for Vermont's highest-risk, highest-cost Medicaid members. VCCI targets the top 5–15% of Medicaid members by predicted cost and clinical complexity, providing dedicated case management by registered nurses and licensed social workers.

VCCI uses a multi-domain risk stratification methodology to identify eligible members: utilization patterns (ED visits, inpatient admissions, 30-day readmissions), chronic condition burden measured through the Chronic Disability Payment System (CDPS) — a Medicaid-specific diagnostic cost group classification system — SDOH vulnerability scores (housing instability, food insecurity, SUD treatment gaps, mental health crises, transportation barriers), and care access gaps (medication adherence below 60% on high-priority drugs, HEDIS quality gaps, absence of a primary care visit in six months).

Members meeting the Very High threshold (CDPS score ≥ 3.5, predicted in the 95th percentile of cost) receive intensive case management: a dedicated case manager as their single point of contact, a shared care plan developed collaboratively with their PCP and specialists, eco-mapping of their support network, multi-disciplinary care team conferences, medication reconciliation and adherence coaching, SDOH navigation through 2-1-1 Vermont and community action agencies, and a 30-day transition care management protocol following every hospitalization or SNF discharge.

VCCI is Vermont's operational implementation of the risk stratification logic described throughout this pillar. The CDPS scoring methodology, the SDOH screening instruments (PHQ-9, AUDIT-C, AHC HRSN), and the care tier structure represent Vermont's best current answer to the question: how do you find the 5% of members who drive 50% of cost, and how do you actively manage their care before the next crisis? The answer matters enormously under global budgets, where unmanaged high-risk members represent the difference between a budget surplus and a structural deficit.

The HTR platform's **Vermont VCCI** page and the **VCCI Risk Stratification Lab** provide the full CDPS scoring methodology, composite risk tier definitions, SDOH screening domains, and interactive patient scenario modeling. These are the operational tools for organizations building risk stratification programs aligned with Vermont's VCCI architecture.

## **What Other States Prove: Oregon and California as National Comparators**

Vermont is the book's primary teaching case because Vermont is the most complete and most transparent state-level transformation in the country. But two other states — Oregon and California — are running transformation experiments large enough and distinctive enough to provide the national comparative evidence that Vermont alone cannot supply.

### **Oregon CCO 3.0 — The Global Budget Model at Scale**

Oregon's Coordinated Care Organization (CCO) model, now in its third generation (CCO 3.0, active 2025–2030), is the closest national analogue to what Vermont is building. Oregon CCOs are regional managed care organizations that integrate physical, behavioral, and oral health services for Oregon Health Plan (Medicaid) members under global budgets with equity accountability requirements and community advisory boards with genuine decision-making authority.

Oregon's CCO model predates Vermont's global budget legislation by a decade and provides the longitudinal evidence base Vermont lacks. Key findings from the Oregon experience: CCO-based global budgets produced measurable reductions in per-member-per-month cost growth compared to the pre-CCO trend while maintaining or improving HEDIS quality metrics on key measures. The CCO model demonstrated that behavioral health integration is achievable at scale — Oregon's physical-behavioral integration rates are among the highest in the country for a Medicaid population. And Oregon's community advisory board requirement — CCOs must have governing boards with at least one-third membership from consumers and community members — produced equity accountability mechanisms that Vermont's GMCB governance structure is still developing.

CCO 3.0 adds explicit climate resilience requirements (recognizing the health impacts of Oregon's wildfire and heat events) and strengthened equity metrics — a signal that the next generation of global budget models will incorporate social and environmental determinants as performance criteria, not just clinical quality measures. Vermont's statewide strategic plan should be watching the CCO 3.0 metrics closely: the equity accountability framework Oregon is building is a template Vermont will need by 2028.

The HTR platform's **Oregon CCO 3.0** page provides the full CCO model description, CCO list by region, outcome metrics, and comparison to Vermont's AHEAD/Act 68 architecture.

### **California CalAIM — Whole-Person Care at the Largest Scale**

California's CalAIM (California Advancing and Innovating Medi-Cal) initiative — a $6.7 billion Medi-Cal transformation approved through a federal 1115 waiver — is the largest state Medicaid transformation in the country and the most comprehensive operationalization of whole-person care outside of Vermont. CalAIM's scope is deliberately national in its ambition: 15 million Medi-Cal members, 57 counties, and a transformation agenda that reaches into housing, criminal justice re-entry, long-term services, and community health workers at a scale that no other state has attempted.

CalAIM's most instructive elements for Vermont are its Enhanced Care Management (ECM) and Community Supports components. ECM targets Medi-Cal's highest-need populations — people experiencing homelessness, individuals recently released from incarceration, those with serious mental illness, long-term care enrollees — with intensive, person-centered care management that explicitly addresses SDOH. Community Supports authorizes managed care plans to pay for non-medical services — housing deposits, recuperative care, medically tailored meals, day habilitation — as alternatives to more expensive medical services.

This is the structural logic Vermont is implementing through VCCI, SASH, and the Blueprint's Community Health Teams, but at a scale that provides statistical power Vermont's 647,000-person population cannot generate. California's ECM outcome data — expected from DHCS beginning 2026 — will provide the strongest available national evidence for or against the whole-person care model that Vermont's transformation strategy depends on.

The HTR platform's **California CalAIM** page provides the full program architecture, ECM eligibility tiers, Community Supports menu, and comparison to Vermont's Medicaid transformation programs.

### **What Vermont, Oregon, and California Together Prove**

The three states represent three points on a transformation spectrum: Vermont is the most structurally complete (mandatory global budgets, legislative architecture, all-payer model participation) but the smallest. Oregon is the middle case — voluntary global budgets in their third generation, behavioral health integration proven at scale, equity accountability maturing. California is the largest — whole-person care at Medicaid scale, with the housing and SDOH investment infrastructure that neither Vermont nor Oregon has fully built.

No single state has solved the transformation problem completely. Vermont has the architecture without the scale. Oregon has the scale without Vermont's legislative mandate. California has the investment without the payment architecture. The convergence of these three models — mandatory payment reform (Vermont) + proven CCO governance (Oregon) + whole-person care investment (California) — describes what a fully transformed state Medicaid system would look like. That convergence has not happened anywhere yet. It is the transformation horizon this book is oriented toward.
