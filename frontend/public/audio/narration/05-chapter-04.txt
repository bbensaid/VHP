Chapter 4. The Policy Pillar. Legislative Architecture for Structural Reform.

We have spent three chapters on the framework. We now begin two chapters on the first pillar, Policy. Chapter 4 develops the structural argument. Chapter 5 develops the operational implementation.

Policy is the legislative and regulatory architecture inside which everything else operates. Policy creates legal authority. Policy enables enforcement. Policy aligns federal and state interests. Policy authorizes the data flows, the payment models, the quality measures, the equity reporting, and the operational mandates that the other five pillars depend on.

Most healthcare reform writing treats Policy as a constraint to be navigated. Skilled providers learn to work around the rules. Skilled payers learn to find the loopholes. Skilled lobbyists learn to shape the rules. This book takes a different view. Policy is not a constraint to be navigated. Policy is the architecture inside which transformation either works or does not. The strategy of working around the rules produces, over four decades, the system we now have. The strategy of building better rules is what transformation requires.

The chapter is organized around four ideas. The Oliver Wyman blueprint. Vermont Act 167 as the diagnostic mandate. Vermont Act 68 as the operational mandate. And the architecture of reference-based pricing as the policy precursor to global budgets.

Idea one. The Oliver Wyman System Redesign Blueprint.

The Oliver Wyman consulting firm was retained by the Vermont Agency of Human Services in two thousand twenty-two to produce a comprehensive diagnostic of the Vermont health system. The mandate came directly from Act 167. The product, published in two thousand twenty-three, runs to several hundred pages and identifies, with specificity, the structural failure modes of the Vermont hospital sector and the operational interventions required to address them.

The Oliver Wyman Blueprint identified several findings that are now operationally driving Vermont's transformation. First, the rural hospital sustainability problem is a structural one, not a temporary cash-flow problem. Without payment reform, the gradual financial deterioration of Vermont rural hospitals will continue indefinitely. Second, hospital cost growth is being driven primarily by administrative cost, not clinical cost. Third, the Vermont hospital sector has the operational and clinical foundations to support transformation, but lacks the financial architecture. Fourth, a state-level all-payer global budget regime, combined with reference-based pricing, is the structural architecture most likely to produce a sustainable system.

The Blueprint did not invent these conclusions. The conclusions had been argued by health economists and Vermont policy professionals for years. The Blueprint codified them in a form that legislators and the Green Mountain Care Board could act on. The Blueprint is, in framework terms, the bridge between health policy analysis and health policy enactment. It is the document that made Act 68 politically and analytically possible.

Idea two. Vermont Act 167 as the diagnostic mandate.

Act 167 of two thousand twenty-two was, on the surface, a modest piece of legislation. It mandated a comprehensive diagnostic of the Vermont health system. It did not, by itself, change any payment model. It did not impose any global budgets. It did not restructure any agency. What it did, structurally, was create the legal authority and the funding for the Oliver Wyman Blueprint.

The strategic significance of Act 167 is what comes next. By the time the Blueprint was published in two thousand twenty-three, the political and analytical foundations had been laid for the much more ambitious Act 68. The Blueprint provided the analytical case. Act 167 provided the legal authority for the diagnostic work. The sustained legislative attention created by Act 167 provided the political continuity required to move from diagnosis to enactment. Without Act 167, Act 68 would have been politically premature.

Act 167 is a worked example of how to structure a multi-stage reform sequence at the state legislative level. The diagnostic phase is separated from the implementation phase. The political coalition is preserved across both phases. The analytical work is procured at sufficient depth and quality to support the implementation phase that follows. Vermont's sequencing, in this respect, is more sophisticated than the sequencing in most states that have attempted comparable reforms.

Idea three. Vermont Act 68 as the operational mandate.

Act 68 of two thousand twenty-five is the central operational mandate of Vermont's transformation. Act 68 establishes, by statute, mandatory hospital global budgets, the reference-based pricing infrastructure that supports those budgets, integration with the federal AHEAD model, and a multi-year implementation timeline running through two thousand thirty.

Act 68 is structurally different from Vermont's earlier global budget attempts, including the all-payer model that produced OneCare Vermont. Earlier efforts were voluntary, demonstration-based, and dependent on federal waivers that could be withdrawn. Act 68 is mandatory under state law, structured for permanence, and politically defensible across multiple election cycles. It is not a pilot. It is the new operating regime.

The mandatory nature of Act 68 matters because it removes a class of political and operational vulnerabilities that earlier efforts could not avoid. A voluntary global budget regime is, by definition, opt-in. Hospitals can refuse to participate. Payers can structure their products around non-participating providers. The voluntary regime never reaches the level of system-wide commitment required to actually change the payment economics. Act 68 forecloses that option. Under Act 68, participation is the law.

The reference-based pricing architecture under Act 68 deserves a brief explanation. Reference-based pricing means that hospital payments are anchored to a transparent reference, typically a multiple of Medicare rates. This is structurally different from the prevailing chargemaster economics, in which hospitals set list prices that bear no defensible relationship to cost or value, and then negotiate confidential discounts with each payer. Reference-based pricing ends that opacity. The reference is public. The multipliers are public. The variation in hospital pricing is, for the first time in Vermont's history, fully visible.

Reference-based pricing is the architectural precursor to global budgets. Without a transparent reference, there is no defensible starting point for a global budget. With a transparent reference, the global budget conversation becomes one about hospital cost structure, operational efficiency, and value delivered. The conversation moves from negotiation to analysis. That is the structural significance of the policy choice.

Idea four. The federal-state interface.

State policy does not operate in isolation. Vermont's transformation is structurally dependent on continued federal cooperation. The AHEAD model brings Medicare into the global budget logic. Section 1115 Medicaid waivers permit Vermont to operate its Medicaid program in non-standard ways. Federal certified community behavioral health clinic funding supports the behavioral health architecture we discuss in Chapter 10. H.R. 1, the federal legislation enacted in July two thousand twenty-five, imposes Medicaid work requirements and provider tax restrictions that constrain state Medicaid policy.

The Vermont transformation strategy depends on federal cooperation that the Vermont political system cannot, by itself, guarantee. A future administration could withdraw AHEAD participation, deny 1115 waivers, or impose additional Medicaid funding constraints. The political sustainability question, which we address in Chapter 18, is in significant part a federal-state interface question.

A final observation about Policy. The Policy pillar tends to be undervalued in healthcare reform writing. Consultants prefer to write about Economics, because that is where the money is. Executives prefer to write about Operations, because that is what they manage. Clinicians prefer to write about Clinical, because that is their craft. Policy is everyone's responsibility and no one's specialty. The book argues that this is structurally wrong. Without Policy, the other five pillars have no foundation. With strong Policy, the other five pillars can be built. The under-investment in state policy capacity, at most American state Medicaid agencies, is one of the principal structural impediments to American healthcare transformation.

In Chapter 5, we walk through the Policy pillar in operational practice. We examine the current Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services innovation model landscape. We discuss section 1115 waivers and budget neutrality. We examine prior authorization reform under H.R. 1. We map the Medicaid policy landscape across the states. And we identify the policy levers that are currently available to state-level reformers in two thousand twenty-six.
