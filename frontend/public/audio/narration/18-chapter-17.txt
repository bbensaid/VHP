Chapter 17. The Future of Healthcare Transformation. Two Thousand Twenty-Six and Beyond.

We have now spent sixteen chapters on the framework, the pillars, the Vermont case, and the cross-cutting infrastructure. Chapter 17 looks forward. We ask three questions. What does Vermont prove about what is nationally replicable. What do Oregon and California prove about what Vermont alone cannot prove. And what does the transformation horizon look like through approximately two thousand thirty-five.

We begin with Vermont.

Vermont's transformation, taken on its own terms, will produce, by approximately two thousand thirty, one of the following three outcomes. The transformation will succeed substantially, demonstrating that mandatory state-level global budgets can produce sustainable hospital sectors, improved population health, and contained cost growth. The transformation will succeed partially, demonstrating that the architecture is workable but that operational gaps must be addressed in subsequent design iterations. Or the transformation will fail, demonstrating that the structural architecture is insufficient and that some other approach must be developed.

The book's prediction, based on framework analysis and current implementation evidence, is that Vermont's transformation will succeed partially. The structural architecture is sound. The sequencing is correct. The political support is durable. But the operational execution will surface gaps that require subsequent design iterations. The result will be a working but imperfect transformation that other states can learn from.

This is not a confident prediction. Healthcare reform has consistently disappointed confident predictions. The point of making the prediction is to put a stake in the ground that subsequent evidence can test.

What Vermont proves, regardless of the eventual outcome, is the structural architecture itself. Mandatory state-level global budgets are operationally feasible at the state level. Reference-based pricing infrastructure can be built in a defensible regulatory framework. State Medicaid agencies can develop the capacity to execute multi-year transformation work. State legislatures can sustain political support for the multi-year work required. None of these propositions was empirically demonstrated at scale before Vermont. Each will be empirically demonstrated by two thousand thirty.

What Vermont cannot prove, by itself, is generalizability. Vermont is small. Vermont is structurally different from larger states. Vermont's political culture supports a degree of regulatory ambition that many larger states cannot match. The Vermont evidence will be the foundation. The Vermont evidence will not be the conclusion.

We turn now to Oregon.

Oregon operates the Coordinated Care Organization model, currently in its third generation, called CCO three-point-zero, operating from two thousand twenty-five through two thousand thirty. The Coordinated Care Organizations are regional managed care organizations that integrate physical, behavioral, and oral health services for Oregon Health Plan, which is Oregon's Medicaid program, under global budgets with equity accountability requirements and community advisory boards with genuine decision-making authority.

Oregon's Coordinated Care Organization model predates Vermont's global budget legislation by more than a decade. The model provides the longitudinal evidence base that Vermont lacks. Several findings from the Oregon experience are operationally important. Coordinated Care Organization-based global budgets produced measurable reductions in per-member-per-month cost growth compared to the pre-Coordinated Care Organization trend, while maintaining or improving HEDIS quality metrics on key measures. The Coordinated Care Organization model demonstrated that behavioral health integration is achievable at scale. Oregon's physical-behavioral integration rates are among the highest in the country for a Medicaid population. And Oregon's community advisory board requirement, which mandates that Coordinated Care Organization governing boards include at least one-third consumer and community member representation, produced equity accountability mechanisms that Vermont's Green Mountain Care Board governance structure is still developing.

Coordinated Care Organization three-point-zero adds explicit climate resilience requirements, recognizing the health impacts of Oregon's wildfire and heat events, and strengthened equity metrics. The signal is that the next generation of global budget models will incorporate social and environmental determinants as performance criteria, not just clinical quality measures.

Oregon's value to the national transformation evidence base is the maturity of its governance and the longitudinal evidence its operations have generated. Oregon does not, however, have the legislative completeness that Vermont has. Oregon's Coordinated Care Organizations are voluntary in their participation. Oregon's global budget framework is administratively imposed rather than statutorily mandated. The political vulnerability of Oregon's model is different from Vermont's. The operational maturity is greater.

We turn now to California.

California's CalAIM initiative, which is the California Advancing and Innovating Medi-Cal program, is the largest state Medicaid transformation in the United States. The initiative is a six-point-seven billion dollar Medi-Cal transformation approved through a federal section 1115 waiver. The scope is deliberately national in its ambition. Fifteen million Medi-Cal members. Fifty-seven counties. A transformation agenda that reaches into housing, criminal justice reentry, long-term services, and community health workers at a scale that no other state has attempted.

CalAIM's most instructive elements for Vermont are its Enhanced Care Management and Community Supports components. Enhanced Care Management targets Medi-Cal's highest-need populations. People experiencing homelessness. Individuals recently released from incarceration. Those with serious mental illness. Long-term care enrollees. The program provides intensive, person-centered care management that explicitly addresses social determinants of health. Community Supports authorizes managed care plans to pay for non-medical services. Housing deposits. Recuperative care. Medically tailored meals. Day habilitation. The non-medical services are paid as alternatives to more expensive medical services.

The structural logic Vermont is implementing through VCCI, SASH, and the Blueprint's Community Health Teams is the same structural logic California is implementing through Enhanced Care Management and Community Supports. The difference is scale. California's population provides statistical power that Vermont's six hundred forty-seven thousand cannot generate. California's Enhanced Care Management outcome data, expected from the California Department of Health Care Services beginning in two thousand twenty-six, will provide the strongest available national evidence base for the whole-person care model that Vermont's transformation strategy depends on.

California's value to the national transformation evidence base is the scale of the experiment. California's limitations are the operational complexity of working across fifty-seven counties with significant local variation, the political volatility of California state government, and the dependency on federal section 1115 waiver continuation.

We turn now to what the three states together prove.

Vermont, Oregon, and California represent three points on a transformation spectrum. Vermont is the most structurally complete. Mandatory global budgets. Legislative architecture. All-payer model participation. Smallest scale. Oregon is the middle case. Voluntary global budgets in their third generation. Behavioral health integration proven at scale. Equity accountability maturing. Larger than Vermont, smaller than California. California is the largest. Whole-person care at Medicaid scale. The housing and social determinants investment infrastructure that neither Vermont nor Oregon has fully built.

No single state has solved the transformation problem completely. Vermont has the architecture without the scale. Oregon has the scale without Vermont's legislative mandate. California has the investment without the payment architecture. The convergence of these three models, mandatory payment reform from Vermont, plus proven Coordinated Care Organization governance from Oregon, plus whole-person care investment from California, describes what a fully transformed state Medicaid system would look like. That convergence has not happened anywhere yet. It is the transformation horizon this book is oriented toward.

A note on Maryland. Maryland's hospital global budget program, operating since two thousand fourteen, provides the longest American track record of hospital-sector payment reform. Maryland is not a whole-system transformation case like Vermont. Maryland is a hospital-sector reform case that other states can learn from selectively. The Maryland evidence supports Vermont's structural claims at the hospital level.

A note on the transformation horizon through two thousand thirty-five.

The next decade in American healthcare will see several trends converge. First, the aging of the population will accelerate demand for chronic care management, behavioral health services, and long-term care. Second, the workforce shortage will tighten, particularly in rural areas and in behavioral health. Third, artificial intelligence in clinical care will continue to mature, producing both opportunities and governance challenges. Fourth, federal policy will continue to volatile through political transitions, creating both risks and opportunities for state-level reform. Fifth, the climate crisis will produce increasing health impacts that state systems will have to absorb. Sixth, the economics of fee-for-service will continue to deteriorate, increasing the political feasibility of payment reform.

The convergence of these trends will, in our view, produce sustained political pressure for state-level transformation. The states that have built the architecture earliest will be best positioned to absorb the demands. The states that have not will be more vulnerable.

In Chapter 18, we address the political sustainability question. How does a state build transformation that survives multiple election cycles, federal administration changes, and political pressure for short-term retreat from long-term work.
