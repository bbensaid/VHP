Chapter 9. The Economics Pillar in Practice. Value-Based Care Financial Modeling and APM Readiness.

Chapter 8 made the structural argument for the Economics pillar. Chapter 9 examines the operational practice. We walk through five operational topics. Shared savings calculations. Risk stratification as the economic engine of value-based payment. The six domains of value-based care readiness. The sixty-five-item value-based care contract review checklist. And the analytics infrastructure required for value-based care operations.

Topic one. Shared savings calculations.

Shared savings is the simplest form of alternative payment model. A payer and a provider agree on a baseline cost of care for an attributed patient population. The provider takes operational responsibility for managing that population. If actual cost comes in below the baseline, the provider receives a share of the savings. If actual cost comes in above the baseline, depending on the contract structure, the provider may share in the losses, or may not.

The mechanics of a shared savings calculation are deceptively complex. The baseline must be defined. Risk adjustment must be applied. The attribution methodology must be specified. The quality gates that determine eligibility for savings must be set. The trend factor that adjusts for medical inflation must be agreed. Each of these design choices materially affects who wins and who loses under the contract.

Most accountable care organizations operating today are operating under shared savings arrangements. The empirical evidence on shared savings is mixed. Some accountable care organizations have produced consistent savings. Others have produced consistent losses. The differences correlate with operational sophistication, with the specific contract design, and with the population to which the contract applies.

The platform's Shared Savings Calculator, available in the research lab, allows providers and payers to model specific contract scenarios. The tool is built around the actual contract architectures currently in use, not a generic abstraction.

Topic two. Risk stratification as the economic engine of value-based payment.

Risk stratification is the analytical process of identifying, within a population, which patients are likely to drive disproportionate cost in the coming year. The clinical importance of risk stratification is in Chapter 10. The economic importance of risk stratification is here.

Under value-based payment, a provider's financial outcome depends on managing the total cost of care for an attributed population. Total cost of care is dominated by the highest-cost patients. In any insured population, approximately five percent of patients drive approximately fifty percent of total cost. The corollary is that operational attention to the highest-cost five percent has roughly ten times the financial leverage of operational attention to the average patient.

Risk stratification, done well, identifies the high-cost five percent before they generate the cost. The economic engine of value-based payment is the ability to identify, before the year begins, which patients require intensive case management, which patients require coordination across multiple specialists, which patients require behavioral health integration, and which patients require social services support.

Vermont's Vermont Chronic Care Initiative, called VCCI, operates this risk stratification at scale for Vermont's highest-risk Medicaid members. We examine VCCI's methodology in Chapter 10. For the purposes of this chapter, the relevant point is that VCCI's risk stratification is what makes Vermont's value-based payment work operationally. Without risk stratification, value-based payment is a guess. With risk stratification, value-based payment is a discipline.

Topic three. The six domains of value-based care readiness.

Not every provider organization is operationally ready to assume value-based payment risk. Provider organizations that assume risk before they are operationally ready tend to produce losses. The losses tend to discredit value-based payment as a strategy, when the actual problem is operational readiness, not the payment model.

The book proposes a six-domain readiness assessment.

Domain one. Financial readiness. The organization must have the financial cushion to absorb downside risk if it materializes. The organization must have the financial systems to track value-based payment performance in real time, not retrospectively. The organization must have the contract administration capability to manage multiple value-based contracts simultaneously.

Domain two. Clinical readiness. The organization must have the clinical workflows to manage population health, not just episodic visit-based care. The organization must have the care coordination capability to support patients across multiple settings. The organization must have the integrated behavioral health capability to address the comorbid behavioral health conditions that drive much of the cost in the high-cost population.

Domain three. Technology readiness. The organization must have the data infrastructure to identify the attributed population, to risk-stratify within it, to track outcomes, and to report on quality metrics. We addressed this in Chapter 7.

Domain four. Operations readiness. The organization must have the revenue cycle redesigned for the new payment model. The organization must have the coding accuracy to support risk adjustment. The organization must have the operational discipline to act on the data the technology infrastructure produces.

Domain five. Cultural readiness. The organization must have leadership alignment on value-based payment as the strategy. The organization must have clinician engagement that translates contract design into clinical workflow. The organization must have organizational learning capability that can course-correct as the contract performance becomes visible.

Domain six. Governance readiness. The organization must have a governance structure that supports value-based payment decision-making. This includes board-level engagement, senior leadership alignment, and a clear escalation path when contract performance issues arise.

The platform's Value-Based Care Readiness Assessment, in the research lab, walks an organization through these six domains in operational detail. The assessment is designed to produce a defensible self-evaluation that supports board decision-making about contract risk assumption.

Topic four. The sixty-five-item value-based care contract review checklist.

The sixty-five-item value-based care contract review checklist is the operational artifact that most readers of this book have actually used. The checklist is reproduced in full in Appendix D. It is the practitioner-grade tool that hospital legal teams, accountable care organization leadership, and payer-provider negotiation teams use to evaluate proposed value-based contracts.

The checklist covers seven categories.

First, target definition and methodology. How is the target population defined. How is the cost baseline established. How is the target adjusted over time. How is risk adjustment applied. How are exclusions handled.

Second, quality measures and adjustments. Which quality measures gate financial performance. How are quality scores calculated. How are quality measures weighted. What happens if quality declines.

Third, risk corridors. What is the maximum upside the provider can earn. What is the maximum downside the provider can absorb. How are risk corridors structured.

Fourth, attribution rules. How are patients attributed to providers. How is attribution adjusted when patients see multiple providers. How are out-of-network referrals handled.

Fifth, termination clauses. Under what circumstances can the contract be terminated. What happens to in-progress accruals at termination. How are post-termination liabilities handled.

Sixth, data rights. What data does the provider receive from the payer. What format. What frequency. What latency.

Seventh, equity provisions. How does the contract address health equity. How are disparate outcomes measured. How does the contract incentivize equity improvement.

The structural significance of the checklist is that it forces an explicit answer to each of these questions before the contract is signed. The history of accountable care organization contracts in American healthcare is full of cases where one or more of these questions was left ambiguous, and the ambiguity later produced losses that were difficult to recover from.

Topic five. Analytics infrastructure.

Value-based payment operations require analytics infrastructure that most provider organizations have not historically maintained. Patient attribution. Risk stratification. Total cost of care tracking. Quality measure calculation. Contract performance dashboards. Each of these is a specific analytic capability that must be operationally available, on demand, to the people running the contract.

The analytics infrastructure can be built internally, contracted from a vendor, or accessed through a partner accountable care organization. Each approach has trade-offs. The book's view is that the analytics infrastructure decision is one of the most consequential strategic decisions a provider organization makes when entering value-based payment, and is often under-deliberated.
