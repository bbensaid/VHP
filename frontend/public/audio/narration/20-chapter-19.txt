Chapter 19. Healthcare Transformation as Portfolio Management.

We have argued throughout the book that transformation is multi-year, multi-pillar work that requires sustained coordination. Chapter 19 introduces a specific discipline for managing that coordination. We borrow the discipline from project management practice, specifically from the body of knowledge maintained by the Project Management Institute, and apply it to healthcare system transformation.

The chapter is organized around four operational topics. Risk registers. Dependency tracking. Milestone gates. And the transformation portfolio office.

Topic one. Risk registers.

A risk register is a structured list of identified risks, each with an assessed likelihood, an assessed impact, and a defined mitigation strategy. Risk registers are routine in capital project management. They are less routine in healthcare reform, where risks are often discussed informally rather than tracked formally.

A well-maintained transformation risk register would include, for Vermont's current transformation, risks across the six pillars. Federal AHEAD continuation risk. Hospital financial sustainability risk. Workforce capacity risk. Behavioral health system sustainability risk. Technology infrastructure completion risk. Federal Medicaid funding risk. Political continuity risk. Each risk would be characterized with current likelihood, current impact, current mitigation actions, and the named accountable owner of the mitigation.

The value of a formal risk register is that it forces the organization to be explicit about what it is worried about. Risks that are discussed informally tend to be forgotten under operational pressure. Risks that are tracked formally surface for management attention on a defined cadence.

Vermont's transformation does maintain risk tracking. The Green Mountain Care Board's hospital financial monitoring is a formal risk register for hospital sustainability. The Agency of Human Services Act 68 implementation updates include risk discussion. The legislative committees that oversee the transformation receive risk briefings. The aggregate effect is a moderately formal risk-tracking system across institutional anchors, though the integration of the various risk views into a single transformation-wide register is not as strong as it could be.

The platform's Transformation Friction Index, available in the platform's tool set, supports risk register work. The Friction Index maps tension between policy design and operational readiness across transformation initiatives, identifying where implementation is most likely to stall.

Topic two. Dependency tracking.

We introduced the fifteen-dependency map in Chapter 1. Dependency tracking is the operational discipline of maintaining a current view of which dependencies are most active and which are most at risk.

Dependency tracking at the transformation level requires a shared analytical view across institutional boundaries. The Agency of Human Services needs to know what the Green Mountain Care Board is doing. The Department of Vermont Health Access needs to know what the Blueprint is doing. The Designated Agencies need to know what hospital implementation is doing. The shared view requires both technical infrastructure and operational discipline.

The framework's fifteen-dependency map is intended as the analytical instrument for this work. Each dependency relationship can be characterized with a current status, a current trajectory, and named accountable owners on both ends. The dependencies that are most at risk get the most management attention. The dependencies that are stable can be monitored less intensively.

Vermont's transformation does dependency tracking informally. The institutional anchors maintain working relationships. The legislative committees coordinate across institutional boundaries. The Agency of Human Services restructuring contemplates stronger formal dependency tracking. The book's view is that formal dependency tracking, using the framework's fifteen-dependency map as the analytical instrument, is one of the higher-leverage improvements Vermont could make in the next year.

Topic three. Milestone gates.

Milestone gates are decision points in a multi-year program at which the program either advances to the next phase or pauses for additional work. Milestone gates are routine in capital project management. They are less routine in healthcare transformation, where the political pressure is often to declare success regardless of whether the underlying milestones have been met.

Effective milestone gates require three elements. Defined criteria, specifying what must be true to pass the gate. Defined evidence requirements, specifying how the criteria will be evaluated. And defined decision authority, specifying who has the authority to pass the gate, defer it, or terminate the program.

Vermont's transformation has explicit milestone structure built into Act 68. The Act 68 implementation timeline includes scheduled review points. The Green Mountain Care Board has scheduled hospital budget review cycles. The AHEAD model has scheduled federal-state checkpoints. The aggregate effect is a moderately gated implementation, though the formality of the gates is uneven across the various streams.

The book argues that gating discipline is one of the principal differences between durable transformation and ephemeral transformation. Programs without gates accumulate problems until the problems become visible all at once and produce a crisis. Programs with gates surface problems at the gate, address them, and proceed.

Topic four. The transformation portfolio office.

The transformation portfolio office is the organizational structure that holds the entire transformation portfolio. It is the office that maintains the risk register, the dependency map, the milestone gates, and the overall portfolio view.

Most American health systems and state agencies do not have a transformation portfolio office. The work of holding the portfolio is dispersed across multiple offices, none of which has the full view. The result is that the portfolio drifts. Individual initiatives advance. The integration across initiatives degrades. The strategic coherence weakens.

Vermont's transformation is, in this respect, similar to most. The Agency of Human Services holds significant portfolio responsibility but not all of it. The Green Mountain Care Board holds significant portfolio responsibility but not all of it. The Governor's office holds political coordination but not operational coordination. The legislative committees hold oversight but not execution authority. No single office holds the full portfolio.

The Agency of Human Services restructuring, discussed in Chapter 20, is in part an attempt to address this gap. The restructuring contemplates a stronger portfolio function within the Agency. The success of the restructuring will determine, in significant part, whether Vermont's transformation maintains its current trajectory through the late two thousand twenties.

A note on the limits of portfolio management.

We have borrowed portfolio management discipline from capital project management. The borrowing is useful, but the analogy has limits. Capital projects have defined scopes, defined budgets, and defined endpoints. Healthcare transformation has none of these. The scope evolves as new federal policy emerges. The budget is constrained by legislative appropriation processes that are themselves political. The endpoint is, in any meaningful sense, never. Transformation is ongoing.

The implication is that healthcare transformation portfolio management is more open-ended than capital project portfolio management. The discipline is similar. The application is different. The book argues for the discipline, with awareness of the limits.
