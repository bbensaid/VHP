> Vermont's five-pillar transformation is not a project, and not a single program. It is a portfolio --- a structured collection of interdependent programs and projects managed toward a strategic objective. This chapter applies PMI portfolio-management standards to Vermont's transformation, makes the case for a dedicated Portfolio Manager at the state level and Program Managers at each pillar, and provides the governance architecture, role definitions, performance framework, and risk register the Statewide Strategic Plan requires --- and identifies the portfolio's critical-path component: the unified claims, clinical, social, and behavioral-health data on which every value-based reform depends (Section 15.8).
PMI's portfolio, program, and project standards are not Vermont-specific, or even healthcare-specific --- they are a general-purpose discipline for managing interdependent work toward a strategic objective, used across industries. Any organization running a five-pillar transformation, in any state, is by definition running a portfolio whether or not it has named it one, and the governance gap this chapter identifies --- multiple interdependent initiatives with no portfolio-level accountability --- is the default state of most transformation efforts, not a Vermont peculiarity. Vermont's 19-component portfolio is this chapter's worked example of what naming and structuring that portfolio looks like once a state's various mandates, programs, and grants are inventoried against the five-pillar framework.

15.1 The Project-Management Gap in Healthcare Transformation.

Every concept in the preceding chapters maps to a project-management discipline, whether or not it was introduced in those terms. Pillar definition is scope management. The execution sequence is schedule management with dependency logic. The failure cascade is risk management. The implementation matrix is cost and resource management. The Vermont Scorecard is benefits-realization management. The five-pillar framework is, in its entirety, a portfolio-management architecture --- and Vermont's transformation is failing to be managed as one.

15.4 The Case for a Portfolio Manager.

Nineteen components. Shared stage gates. A single January 2028 deadline. Three statutory predecessors --- Acts 167, 51, and 68 --- plus the AHEAD State Agreement's closeout obligations, each with their own federal and state reporting obligations. This is not a program-management problem. It is a portfolio-management problem, and it cannot be solved by component managers acting independently, no matter how skilled each one is. It requires a Portfolio Manager: a designated authority with the mandate and the access to manage Vermont's transformation as a whole, not as a collection of programs each doing their best.
This is not a new idea for Vermont. Oliver Wyman's Act 167 report explicitly recommended that AHS hire a dedicated project-management function and establish the "project management infrastructure needed to manage Vermont's transformation portfolio." The November 2025 report repeated the finding. The AHS Restructuring Roadmap (Chapter 16 of this book) identifies PMO establishment as a critical organizational project. What this chapter adds is a precise, PMI-grounded definition of the role, its authority, and its connection to the pillar-level Program Managers below it.
Role and authority (summary). The Portfolio Manager chairs the monthly portfolio review board; maintains the portfolio register (Figure 15.3) and risk register; authorizes component additions and removals; ensures all components stay aligned to Act 167's five goals; resolves cross-component resource conflicts; owns the Vermont Scorecard reporting to the Health Care Delivery Advisory Committee (HCAC) and the AHS Secretary; manages portfolio-level stakeholder relationships with HCAC, GMCB, hospital CEOs, the Legislature, and CMS; and tracks benefits realization, recommending component termination when benefits are no longer achievable. The role requires a direct report to the AHS Secretary, a GMCB co-reporting relationship, budget authority over the transformation PMO, and authority to require reporting from all 19 component leads. The appropriate credential is PMI's Portfolio Management Professional (PfMP), with 8+ years of program experience and health-system or large-scale government program experience.

15.7 Portfolio Sequence Is Dependency Sequence.

The nineteen components are not a flat list of work to be scheduled by deadline and resource availability. They inherit an order from the dependency structure established in Chapter 1, and a portfolio manager who schedules them by statutory date alone will produce exactly the failures that chapter documents.
Read Figure 15.3 by its first column and the point is immediate: the components are already grouped by pillar. What the PMI framing adds is that each pillar's components carry the inbound dependencies of that pillar. The Economics components --- RBP implementation, global-budget implementation, RHT Program deployment --- cannot be productively worked before the Technology components that make them measurable; only their design runs in parallel (Economics-as-design, Section 1.11.2). That is not a scheduling preference. It is the same critical-path constraint that produced the OneCare failure in Section 1.2, restated in portfolio terms.
This gives the portfolio manager a decision rule that a Gantt chart does not:
A component whose upstream pillar gate is still closed is not "behind schedule." It is correctly waiting. Accelerating it consumes budget and political capital to produce output nobody can use. The AHS-GMCB analytics deployment is the clearest case --- it carries the register's highest delivery-risk score (R-01, 20, tied only with the governance risk R-10) precisely because three downstream components converge on it, not because its own deadline is nearest.
The inverse rule matters as much. A component with no unmet upstream dependency should be running now, regardless of how distant its own deadline is. Social-risk-adjustment methodology (R-08) has a FY2028 target and no upstream blocker; it can and should proceed in parallel today. Treating it as a FY2028 problem because that is when it is due is the scheduling error that leaves equity work perpetually late.

15.8 The Critical-Path Component: Unified Health Data.

Run the sequencing test of Section 15.7 on any value-based transformation portfolio, in any state, and one component sits upstream of almost everything else: the ability to see each person's care whole. That means claims (what was paid for), clinical data (what happened), social-determinants data (the conditions that drove it), and behavioral-health data (the care most often missing from the other three), unified for the same person. States call this different things: a health data utility, a linked claims-and-clinical warehouse, an expanded health information exchange. Vermont's statute calls it the Unified Health Data Space (UHDS). The name does not matter. The dependency does: without unified data, value-based care cannot be managed, only paid for.

15.8.1 Why value-based care cannot be managed on claims or clinical data alone.

Every value-based arrangement makes the same promise: pay for outcomes and total cost rather than for units of service. Keeping that promise requires evidence that no single data source holds.
Claims record what was billed and paid, across every setting a payer covers. They are the basis for total cost of care and patient attribution. But a claim records that a blood-pressure check happened, not what the blood pressure was.
Clinical data records the readings, results, and medications. It is the basis for outcome measures. But each record sees only the care delivered inside one organization, and it carries no price.
Social-determinants data (housing, food, transportation) explains why two patients with the same diagnosis have very different costs and outcomes. Without it, risk adjustment penalizes the providers who serve the hardest-pressed communities.
Behavioral-health data covers the conditions that most often drive avoidable emergency visits and readmissions. Much of it is protected by separate consent rules and sits outside both claims and hospital records.

The gap is measurable. A study cited in Vermont's January 2026 report to its Legislature found that half of Medicaid members' cholesterol, diabetes, and kidney screenings, and their influenza vaccinations, were documented in clinical records but missing from claims. A program paid on claims-based quality measures would have scored those providers as failing at care they had delivered. The reverse is equally true: a program measured on one hospital's records cannot see the emergency visit at another hospital the following week.
The stinting row matters most wherever global budgets are adopted. A fixed budget removes the incentive to over-treat and creates the opposite risk. Claims alone would show that spending fell; clinical records alone would show one organization's patients; neither would show whether the population was worse off. Only unified data can.

15.8.2 Why it sits on the critical path.

In portfolio terms, unified data is a gate. Every Economics component that pays on outcomes or total cost depends on it: price regulation, global budgets, shared savings, quality payment. So does every Clinical component that claims to reduce avoidable use. A component scheduled ahead of that gate is not on time; it is running blind. Its results cannot be measured, and its failures will be discovered late.
Vermont shows how easily this happens even when the dependency is written into law. The Green Mountain Care Board's reference-based prices take effect in hospital fiscal year 2028. Under the Agency of Human Services' own plan, Medicare and statewide clinical data join Vermont's unified data in 2028--2029, and commercial claims in 2029--2030. Vermont will therefore regulate commercial hospital prices for one to two years before its unified data can see a commercially insured patient. That is a downstream component scheduled ahead of its gate, the error the sequencing test exists to catch, and the dependency behind risk R-01 in the portfolio risk register (Figure 15.8). How Vermont's UHDS is being built, from the Medicaid data warehouse outward, is described in Chapter 4 (§§4.3.5--4.3.8).

15.8.4 The exclusive-operator problem.

Condition 7 deserves its own treatment, because it is the one states most often create for themselves. A state that grants a single organization an exclusive franchise over its health information exchange removes the forces that normally make a technology vendor improve: no competitor sets a benchmark for price or performance, no alternative exists at contract renewal, and the operator that runs the network also decides how much of the application layer anyone else may build on the data. None of this requires bad faith by anyone. It is what a statutory monopoly produces, however well run.
Vermont legislated exactly this. Section 9352 requires that VITL "be designated in the Health Information Technology Plan to operate the exclusive statewide health information exchange network for this State." Chapter 4 (§4.3) sets out the structural case against that designation and the remedy. The public record adds the evidence:
Accountability came late. In 2016 the State Auditor reported that Vermont had paid VITL more than $38 million since 2005 under agreements without performance targets, leaving the State unable to assess its performance.
Stakeholders said so. The evaluation the Legislature ordered in 2017 found that only 19% of stakeholders thought the exchange met Vermont's needs, and only 21% thought it critical that VITL keep managing it; 53% said it was not. The Legislature put the State in charge of the Health Information Technology Plan and switched to opt-out consent, but it kept the exclusive designation.
Modern capability lags. The HIE Strategic Plan update of November 2024 put "Implement FHIR APIs," the open interfaces modern applications are built on, at 25% complete, projected to reach only 40% by the end of 2024, and rated it only partially on track.
Reported coverage overstates what flows. The same update listed "All DA's contributing production data to VITL" among the year's accomplishments; VITL's own figures for February 2025 show the designated mental-health agencies contributing no data, with twelve accessing it.
Oversight has narrowed. Act 62 of 2025 ended the Green Mountain Care Board's review of VITL's budget, leaving the contracting department as the main check on a sole-source operator.
The remedy applies to any state, and it does not require replacing an exchange that works. The state owns the core data assets (patient index, consent registry, clinical repository) and contracts for their operation competitively. Contracts publish completeness, timeliness, and matching targets by provider type, with consequences. Open, standards-based (FHIR) access lets referral, e-Consult, analytics, and patient tools compete on one shared foundation. Data and consent records are portable to any successor. And a second, state-controlled ingestion path removes the single point of failure.

15.8.5 What it takes to deliver it.

Unified data is permanent infrastructure, not a project with an end date, and the portfolio must treat it that way. It needs one accountable owner with authority over the whole data stack, from the exchange contract and the unified dataset to governance and funding. It needs data stewards responsible for the completeness of each source (claims, clinical, social, behavioral), privacy and legal staff who can bring every proposed use to the governing body in writing with a privacy assessment, and a contract manager with the standing to enforce performance terms on the exchange operator. The specialized build work can be contracted. Ownership cannot be.

15.8.6 What the portfolio should do now.

Applied to unified data, the sequencing test gives four instructions to any state, with Vermont's version in parentheses:
> 1. Put commercial data on the critical path, ahead of the price and budget regulation that depends on it. (Vermont: an AHS--GMCB agreement for approved uses of the all-payer claims database before FY2028, and restoration of the insurer-reporting requirement the Senate passed.)
>
> 2. Fund the phases that make the data unified, not only the Medicaid phase that federal match pays for, beginning with behavioral-health and social-services contributors. (Vermont: a named State source, and Rural Health Transformation funds for one-time connection work.)
>
> 3. Set the first uses by reform priority: price and global-budget monitoring, quality payment, behavioral-health integration, outcome measurement. (Vermont: publish them before the first § 9353 vote in 2027.)
>
> 4. Open the exchange: end exclusive franchises, own the core assets, contract competitively, and publish performance. (Vermont: amend § 9352.)

15.9 Predictive vs. Adaptive Approaches.

Not all 19 components should be managed identically. Predictive (waterfall) management fits components with fixed statutory deadlines and well-defined deliverables: RBP-methodology rulemaking, the Strategic Plan (with adaptive elements), analytics-vendor deployment, CCBHC certification, and AHS restructuring. Adaptive (agile) management fits components where requirements evolve and rapid learning beats adherence to a fixed plan: the statewide AI-governance framework (regulatory landscape evolving faster than any fixed plan), the CIN build-out (architecture depends on vendor outputs and the FHIR ecosystem), Blueprint PCMH expansion (practice-by-practice iteration), and HEROI methodology (iterating as data quality improves). The 14-hospital planning effort is hybrid --- fixed deliverables, hospital-specific iterative content. PMP credentials map to the predictive components; PMI-ACP to the adaptive ones.

15.14 Answering the Five Objections.

1.  "We already manage this --- we have the HCAC and monthly reports." The HCAC is a governance body, not a PMO; it provides oversight, not an integrated master schedule, resource-conflict resolution, a maintained risk register, or earned-value tracking. The monthly reports track activities, not performance metrics. The HCAC is the Portfolio Governance Body; it needs a Portfolio Manager and a PMO to give it the data it needs to govern.
2.  "This is government --- we don't run projects like the private sector." Vermont's transformation is a time-bounded, outcome-specified, heavily financed portfolio of interdependent programs with legally binding deadlines. The RHT Program is a federal grant with deliverables and reporting obligations; the withdrawn AHEAD State Agreement still carries federal closeout requirements; Act 68 is a statutory mandate with a January 2028 deliverable. Federal partners expect professional project management --- the GAO uses PMI's PMBOK as a reference standard.
3.  "We can't afford a Portfolio Manager when we're cutting budgets." Vermont cannot afford not to have one. RHT funds are contingent on demonstrated progress; a portfolio that misses its components risks federal clawback. The ~$4--6.4M cost is less than 2% of a single year of the value it protects, and the alternative is the multi-billion-dollar partial-transformation outcome.
4.  "The hospitals won't accept being managed by AHS." The Portfolio Manager does not manage the hospitals --- it manages AHS's contribution to the portfolio. Hospital plans are produced by hospitals, approved by AHS, and monitored through GMCB. The Portfolio Manager is Vermont's transformation orchestrator, not its controller.
5.  "AHS already has expert help --- consultants, a technical-assistance vendor, an analytics contractor." It does: AHS's March 2026 Act 68 report lists contracted transformation support from Manatt, Phelps & Phillips, a new hospital technical-assistance vendor, and analytics support under negotiation. But a contractor delivers a defined scope. It cannot hold a state component back until its upstream gate opens, reassign AHS staff between components, or answer to the Legislature for the portfolio as a whole. Contracted support adds capacity, not authority --- and every new contract is one more component, with its own deliverables and deadline, that someone has to sequence.

15.16 Implications for You.

If you are an AHS or state agency leader: the argument of this chapter is not that Vermont needs more oversight --- it has the HCAC, monthly legislative reporting, and federal review. It needs an integrator: one role accountable for the portfolio as a whole, with authority to resolve resource conflicts between components that today are managed by different units against different deadlines. The single most consequential decision is not whether to hire a Portfolio Manager but whether that role is given authority over sequencing. A PMO that reports status without the power to hold a component back until its upstream gate opens will document the cascade rather than prevent it.
If you lead a hospital PMO or transformation office: Figure 15.3 is a template, not a Vermont artifact. Build the same inventory for your own organization --- every initiative touching the five pillars, each assigned a pillar, an owner, a type, and a stage gate. Most organizations discover in that exercise that they are running more simultaneous transformation components than they had counted, that several have no named owner, and that two or three are downstream of a gate nobody is tracking. Then apply the sequencing test above: the components correctly waiting are not your problem; the ones running ahead of their dependencies are.
If you are a legislator or board member: the oversight question that separates a real portfolio from a reporting exercise is short. Which component is currently the binding constraint on all the others, who owns it, and what would it take to accelerate it? An organization managing a genuine portfolio can answer in one sentence. An organization managing a list will answer with a status summary of all nineteen.
If you are in another state: the portfolio does not have to be nineteen components or built on PMI's standard specifically. What transfers is the discipline of defining the portfolio at all --- naming every component, assigning each to a pillar and an owner, and scheduling by dependency rather than by deadline. States that skip this step do not avoid portfolio management; they do it implicitly, badly, and without anyone accountable for the result.
