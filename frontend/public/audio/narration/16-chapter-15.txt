> Vermont's five-pillar transformation is not a project, and not a single program. It is a portfolio --- a structured collection of interdependent programs and projects managed toward a strategic objective. This chapter applies PMI portfolio-management standards to Vermont's transformation, makes the case for a dedicated Portfolio Manager at the state level and Program Managers at each pillar, and provides the governance architecture, role definitions, performance framework, and risk register the Statewide Strategic Plan requires --- and identifies the portfolio's critical-path component: the unified claims, clinical, social, and behavioral-health data on which every value-based reform depends (Section 15.8).
PMI's portfolio, program, and project standards are not Vermont-specific, or even healthcare-specific --- they are a general-purpose discipline for managing interdependent work toward a strategic objective, used across industries. Any organization running a five-pillar transformation, in any state, is by definition running a portfolio whether or not it has named it one, and the governance gap this chapter identifies --- multiple interdependent initiatives with no portfolio-level accountability --- is the default state of most transformation efforts, not a Vermont peculiarity. Vermont's 19-component portfolio is this chapter's worked example of what naming and structuring that portfolio looks like once a state's various mandates, programs, and grants are inventoried against the five-pillar framework.

15.1 The Project-Management Gap in Healthcare Transformation.

Every concept in the preceding chapters maps to a project-management discipline, whether or not it was introduced in those terms. Pillar definition is scope management. The execution sequence is schedule management with dependency logic. The failure cascade is risk management. The implementation matrix is cost and resource management. The Vermont Scorecard is benefits-realization management. The five-pillar framework is, in its entirety, a portfolio-management architecture --- and Vermont's transformation is failing to be managed as one.

15.4 The Case for a Portfolio Manager.

Nineteen components. Shared stage gates. A single January 2028 deadline. Three statutory predecessors --- Acts 167, 51, and 68 --- plus the AHEAD State Agreement's closeout obligations, each with their own federal and state reporting obligations. This is not a program-management problem. It is a portfolio-management problem, and it cannot be solved by component managers acting independently, no matter how skilled each one is. It requires a Portfolio Manager: a designated authority with the mandate and the access to manage Vermont's transformation as a whole, not as a collection of programs each doing their best.
This is not a new idea for Vermont. Oliver Wyman's Act 167 report explicitly recommended that AHS hire a dedicated project-management function and establish the "project management infrastructure needed to manage Vermont's transformation portfolio." The November 2025 report repeated the finding. The AHS Restructuring Roadmap (Chapter 16 of this book) identifies PMO establishment as a critical organizational project. What this chapter adds is a precise, PMI-grounded definition of the role, its authority, and its connection to the pillar-level Program Managers below it.
Role and authority (summary). The Portfolio Manager chairs the monthly portfolio review board; maintains the portfolio register (Figure 15.3) and risk register; authorizes component additions and removals; ensures all components stay aligned to Act 167's five goals; resolves cross-component resource conflicts; owns the Vermont Scorecard reporting to the Health Care Delivery Advisory Committee (HCAC) and the AHS Secretary; manages portfolio-level stakeholder relationships with HCAC, GMCB, hospital CEOs, the Legislature, and CMS; and tracks benefits realization, recommending component termination when benefits are no longer achievable. The role requires a direct report to the AHS Secretary, a GMCB co-reporting relationship, budget authority over the transformation PMO, and authority to require reporting from all 19 component leads. The appropriate credential is PMI's Portfolio Management Professional (PfMP), with 8+ years of program experience and health-system or large-scale government program experience.

15.7 Portfolio Sequence Is Dependency Sequence.

The nineteen components are not a flat list of work to be scheduled by deadline and resource availability. They inherit an order from the dependency structure established in Chapter 1, and a portfolio manager who schedules them by statutory date alone will produce exactly the failures that chapter documents.
Read Figure 15.3 by its first column and the point is immediate: the components are already grouped by pillar. What the PMI framing adds is that each pillar's components carry the inbound dependencies of that pillar. The Economics components --- RBP implementation, global-budget implementation, RHT Program deployment --- cannot be productively worked before the Technology components that make them measurable; only their design runs in parallel (Economics-as-design, Section 1.11.2). That is not a scheduling preference. It is the same critical-path constraint that produced the OneCare failure in Section 1.2, restated in portfolio terms.
This gives the portfolio manager a decision rule that a Gantt chart does not:
A component whose upstream pillar gate is still closed is not "behind schedule." It is correctly waiting. Accelerating it consumes budget and political capital to produce output nobody can use. The AHS-GMCB analytics deployment is the clearest case --- it carries the register's highest delivery-risk score (R-01, 20, tied only with the governance risk R-10) precisely because three downstream components converge on it, not because its own deadline is nearest.
The inverse rule matters as much. A component with no unmet upstream dependency should be running now, regardless of how distant its own deadline is. Social-risk-adjustment methodology (R-08) has a FY2028 target and no upstream blocker; it can and should proceed in parallel today. Treating it as a FY2028 problem because that is when it is due is the scheduling error that leaves equity work perpetually late.

15.8 The Critical-Path Component: The Unified Health Data Space.

Run the sequencing test of Section 15.7 on Vermont's portfolio and one component sits upstream of almost everything else: a unified view of each person's care. That view joins what was paid for (claims), what happened clinically (clinical records), the social conditions that shape health (social determinants), and the mental-health and substance-use care that so often drives cost (behavioral health). Reference-based prices, global budgets, the Blueprint's primary care payments, the Rural Health Transformation investments, and the Strategic Plan's affordability benchmarks all depend on it. Act 68 gave this component a name, the Unified Health Data Space (UHDS), and a statute, 18 V.S.A. § 9353. Vermont's experience shows why unified data belongs at the head of the critical path, and why the institutions and laws around it can hold back a reform as surely as any shortage of money.

15.8.1 Why value-based care cannot be managed on claims or clinical data alone.

Every form of value-based payment makes the same promise: pay for outcomes and total cost rather than for units of service. Keeping that promise requires evidence that no single data source holds. Each source answers a different question, and value-based care needs every answer for the same person.
Claims record what was billed and paid: who was seen, where, for what diagnosis, at what price, across every setting a payer covers. They are the basis for total cost of care, patient attribution, and utilization trends. But a claim records that a blood-pressure check happened, not what the blood pressure was.
Clinical data from electronic health records, laboratories, and registries records what happened to the patient: the readings, the results, the medications. It is the basis for outcome measures. But each record sees only the care delivered inside one organization, and it carries no price.
Social-determinants data (housing, food security, transportation) explains why two patients with the same diagnosis have very different costs and outcomes. Without it, risk adjustment penalizes the providers who serve the hardest-pressed communities.
Behavioral-health data from designated agencies, substance-use treatment, and mental-health providers covers the conditions that most often drive avoidable emergency visits and readmissions. Much of it is protected by separate consent rules, and much of it sits outside both claims and hospital records.
Value-based care needs all four, unified at the level of the individual person:

The gap is measurable. A study cited in AHS's January 2026 report found that half of Medicaid members' cholesterol, diabetes, and kidney screenings, and their influenza vaccinations, were documented in clinical records but missing from claims. A program paid on claims-based quality measures would have scored those providers as failing at care they had delivered. The reverse is equally true: a program measured on one hospital's clinical records cannot see the emergency visit at another hospital the following week. AHS's report also notes that health care organizations reporting "hybrid" quality measures, which require claims and clinical data together, combine them today by manual chart abstraction.
The stinting row matters most for Vermont. Act 68 moves hospitals to global budgets, a fixed sum regardless of volume. That design removes the incentive to over-treat and creates the opposite risk. The only safeguard is the ability to see, quickly and across every setting, whether access and outcomes held up after the budget was set. Claims alone would show that spending fell; clinical records alone would show one organization's patients; neither would show whether Vermonters as a whole were worse off. Only unified data can.

15.8.2 What Vermont has built, and when the pieces arrive.

The mandate for unified data is older than Act 68. Act 167 of 2022 directed the HIE Steering Committee to "create one health record for each person" that integrates claims, clinical, mental-health and substance-use, and social-determinants data, and to plan a merger of VHCURES claims with the exchange's clinical data. The same act repealed 18 V.S.A. § 9410(e), which had required confidential health information to be filed in the all-payer claims database without disclosing the identity of the person --- a barrier to matching a person's claims with that person's clinical record. Act 68 then set the rules in § 9353: AHS develops the UHDS with the HIE Steering Committee; integration may not begin before January 1, 2027; it requires a favorable vote of a majority of all voting committee members; and unified data may be used only for the uses that vote approves.
Some foundations exist. Opt-out consent since 2020 means 98.9% of Vermonters' data is shared through the health information exchange (February 2025). A rebuilt patient-matching index raised record matching from about 65% to more than 95%, the precondition for joining any two sources about the same person. Since January 2026 the State's Medicaid data warehouse (MDWAS) has held linked Medicaid claims, clinical, and social-needs data: the first working slice of unified data in Vermont.
The calendar is the problem. AHS's own plan adds Medicare and statewide clinical data in 2028--2029 and commercial claims in 2029--2030. The GMCB's reference-based prices take effect in hospital FY2028. Vermont will regulate commercial hospital prices for one to two years before its unified data can see a commercially insured patient. In portfolio terms, downstream components are scheduled ahead of the gate they depend on. That is the error the sequencing test exists to catch, and it is the dependency behind risk R-01 in the portfolio risk register (Figure 15.8).

15.8.3 Where the law itself stands in the way.

Three features of the statute, not of its execution, slow the unification the reform needs.
The insurer mandate was dropped. The version of Act 68's data section that passed the Senate required health insurers to "provide clinical and claims data to the Agency of Human Services as directed by the Agency." The enacted law does not contain that sentence. AHS therefore has no power to require commercial claims for the UHDS. It can only negotiate access to VHCURES, the GMCB's all-payer claims database, which operates under its own use restrictions and captures only about 60% of commercial claims because self-insured employer plans cannot be compelled to report (Chapter 4). The phase price regulation needs most is the phase the law makes hardest.
The money stops at Medicaid. Federal match pays roughly 90% of Medicaid data development. Work serving commercial and Medicare populations earns none, and § 9353 names no State source for it. The multi-payer phases, the ones that turn a Medicaid dataset into unified data for the whole state, are unfunded by design.
The gate rewards inaction. Requiring a majority of all voting members, rather than of those present, is a sound privacy protection. It also means that every vacant or absent seat counts as "no." And the statute sets no priority among uses, so nothing guarantees that the uses the reform depends on (price and global-budget monitoring, quality payment, behavioral-health integration) are the first to be approved.

15.8.4 The designated monopoly.

The most consequential structural choice in Vermont's health data system predates Act 68 by two decades. Section 9352 requires that VITL "be designated in the Health Information Technology Plan to operate the exclusive statewide health information exchange network for this State." The operative word is exclusive. Vermont did not procure an exchange operator; it legislated one. Every phase of the UHDS that draws clinical data, and every attempt to unify clinical data with claims, social, and behavioral-health data, runs through that monopoly.
Monopolies behave like monopolies, and the public record shows it.
Accountability came late. In 2016 the State Auditor reported that Vermont had paid VITL more than $38 million since 2005 without being able to assess its performance. The evaluation ordered by Act 73 of 2017 found that only 19% of stakeholders thought the exchange met their needs.
Progress, when it came, was driven from outside. The turnaround of 2019--2022 followed State intervention: State ownership of the Health Information Technology Plan, a State-led Steering Committee, and the Legislature's switch to opt-out consent. It did not come from competitive pressure, because there is none.
Modern capability lags. The HIE Strategic Plan update of November 2024 rated the goal "Implement FHIR APIs," the open interfaces on which modern applications, analytics, and patient access are built, at 25% complete, projected to reach only 40% by the end of 2024, and rated only partially on track. Meanwhile Vermont is funding e-Consult platforms, closed-loop referral systems, a shared electronic health record, and transformation analytics through the RHT program as separate purchases, where a modern exchange would let each of them build on one unified data foundation.
Reported coverage overstates what flows. The same 2024 update listed "All DA's contributing production data to VITL" among the year's accomplishments. VITL's own February 2025 figures show the designated mental-health and specialized service agencies contributing no data: twelve accessed data; none sent it. Nursing homes and commercial payers sent none either. These are exactly the behavioral-health, long-term-care, and claims data that unified, whole-person measurement needs.
Oversight has narrowed. Until 2025 the GMCB reviewed VITL's budget. Act 62 of 2025 removed that review, leaving the contracting department as the main check on a sole-source vendor funded by a tax on health insurance claims and by federal match.
None of this requires bad faith by anyone. It is what a statutory monopoly produces: no competitor to set a benchmark for price or performance, no alternative at contract renewal to give the State leverage, and an incentive to protect the franchise rather than to open the data for others to build on. Innovation in health data now happens mostly at the application layer (analytics, care coordination, referral, patient access), and an exclusive operator that is also the gatekeeper to the data decides how much of that layer anyone else is allowed to build.
Vermont does not need to replace its exchange to fix this. It needs to change the terms on which the exchange operates:

15.8.5 What it takes to deliver it.

A unified data utility is not a project with an end date; it is permanent infrastructure, and the portfolio must treat it that way. It needs one accountable owner with authority over the whole data stack, from the exchange contract and the unified dataset to governance and funding. It needs data stewards responsible for the completeness of each source (claims, clinical, social, behavioral), privacy and legal staff who can bring every proposed use to the Steering Committee in writing with a privacy assessment, and a contract manager with the standing to enforce performance terms on the exchange operator. The specialized build work can be contracted. Ownership cannot be: the State's own staff must be able to run the utility, and hold its vendors to account, by the time the build contracts end.

15.8.6 What the portfolio should do now.

Applied to the UHDS, the sequencing test gives four instructions:
> 1. Treat commercial data as the critical path. A VHCURES data-access agreement between AHS and the GMCB for approved UHDS uses before FY2028 prices take effect, and restoration of the insurer-reporting requirement the Senate originally passed.
>
> 2. Fund the phases that make the data unified. A named State source for the multi-payer and commercial phases, and use of the time-limited RHT funds for the one-time work federal Medicaid match will not pay for, beginning with behavioral-health and long-term-care contributor connections.
>
> 3. Set the first uses by reform priority. Before the first § 9353 vote in 2027, publish the priority uses: price and global-budget monitoring, quality payment, behavioral-health integration, and RHT outcome measurement.
>
> 4. Open the exchange. Begin the statutory and contractual changes in Figure 15.7, so that the clinical data the UHDS depends on comes from an operator that competes on performance and opens its data to others.

15.9 Predictive vs. Adaptive Approaches.

Not all 19 components should be managed identically. Predictive (waterfall) management fits components with fixed statutory deadlines and well-defined deliverables: RBP-methodology rulemaking, the Strategic Plan (with adaptive elements), analytics-vendor deployment, CCBHC certification, and AHS restructuring. Adaptive (agile) management fits components where requirements evolve and rapid learning beats adherence to a fixed plan: the statewide AI-governance framework (regulatory landscape evolving faster than any fixed plan), the CIN build-out (architecture depends on vendor outputs and the FHIR ecosystem), Blueprint PCMH expansion (practice-by-practice iteration), and HEROI methodology (iterating as data quality improves). The 14-hospital planning effort is hybrid --- fixed deliverables, hospital-specific iterative content. PMP credentials map to the predictive components; PMI-ACP to the adaptive ones.

15.14 Answering the Five Objections.

1.  "We already manage this --- we have the HCAC and monthly reports." The HCAC is a governance body, not a PMO; it provides oversight, not an integrated master schedule, resource-conflict resolution, a maintained risk register, or earned-value tracking. The monthly reports track activities, not performance metrics. The HCAC is the Portfolio Governance Body; it needs a Portfolio Manager and a PMO to give it the data it needs to govern.
2.  "This is government --- we don't run projects like the private sector." Vermont's transformation is a time-bounded, outcome-specified, heavily financed portfolio of interdependent programs with legally binding deadlines. The RHT Program is a federal grant with deliverables and reporting obligations; the withdrawn AHEAD State Agreement still carries federal closeout requirements; Act 68 is a statutory mandate with a January 2028 deliverable. Federal partners expect professional project management --- the GAO uses PMI's PMBOK as a reference standard.
3.  "We can't afford a Portfolio Manager when we're cutting budgets." Vermont cannot afford not to have one. RHT funds are contingent on demonstrated progress; a portfolio that misses its components risks federal clawback. The ~$4--6.4M cost is less than 2% of a single year of the value it protects, and the alternative is the multi-billion-dollar partial-transformation outcome.
4.  "The hospitals won't accept being managed by AHS." The Portfolio Manager does not manage the hospitals --- it manages AHS's contribution to the portfolio. Hospital plans are produced by hospitals, approved by AHS, and monitored through GMCB. The Portfolio Manager is Vermont's transformation orchestrator, not its controller.
5.  "AHS already has expert help --- consultants, a technical-assistance vendor, an analytics contractor." It does: AHS's March 2026 Act 68 report lists contracted transformation support from Manatt, Phelps & Phillips, a new hospital technical-assistance vendor, and analytics support under negotiation. But a contractor delivers a defined scope. It cannot hold a state component back until its upstream gate opens, reassign AHS staff between components, or answer to the Legislature for the portfolio as a whole. Contracted support adds capacity, not authority --- and every new contract is one more component, with its own deliverables and deadline, that someone has to sequence.

15.16 Implications for You.

If you are an AHS or state agency leader: the argument of this chapter is not that Vermont needs more oversight --- it has the HCAC, monthly legislative reporting, and federal review. It needs an integrator: one role accountable for the portfolio as a whole, with authority to resolve resource conflicts between components that today are managed by different units against different deadlines. The single most consequential decision is not whether to hire a Portfolio Manager but whether that role is given authority over sequencing. A PMO that reports status without the power to hold a component back until its upstream gate opens will document the cascade rather than prevent it.
If you lead a hospital PMO or transformation office: Figure 15.3 is a template, not a Vermont artifact. Build the same inventory for your own organization --- every initiative touching the five pillars, each assigned a pillar, an owner, a type, and a stage gate. Most organizations discover in that exercise that they are running more simultaneous transformation components than they had counted, that several have no named owner, and that two or three are downstream of a gate nobody is tracking. Then apply the sequencing test above: the components correctly waiting are not your problem; the ones running ahead of their dependencies are.
If you are a legislator or board member: the oversight question that separates a real portfolio from a reporting exercise is short. Which component is currently the binding constraint on all the others, who owns it, and what would it take to accelerate it? An organization managing a genuine portfolio can answer in one sentence. An organization managing a list will answer with a status summary of all nineteen.
If you are in another state: the portfolio does not have to be nineteen components or built on PMI's standard specifically. What transfers is the discipline of defining the portfolio at all --- naming every component, assigning each to a pillar and an owner, and scheduling by dependency rather than by deadline. States that skip this step do not avoid portfolio management; they do it implicitly, badly, and without anyone accountable for the result.
