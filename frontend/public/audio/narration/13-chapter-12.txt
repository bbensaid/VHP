Chapter 12. The Equity Pillar. Closing Gaps, Not Just Averaging Them.

The Equity pillar is the access and outcomes guarantee. It is the structural commitment that the health system serves all populations and not just the easiest or most profitable populations. Chapter 12 develops the structural argument. Chapter 13 develops the operational practice.

We have said throughout the book that equity is a design constraint that applies at every step, not a downstream filter applied at the end. Chapter 12 explains what that means in practice, and why the alternative approach has consistently failed.

The chapter is organized around four ideas. Social determinants of health as a structural variable. Vermont's eight social determinants of health domains. Algorithmic bias in clinical artificial intelligence. And access disparity in rural Vermont.

Idea one. Social determinants of health as a structural variable.

The phrase social determinants of health has been used in American health policy for decades. The phrase refers to the non-medical factors that shape population health. Housing. Food security. Income. Education. Employment. Transportation. Social connection. The recognition that these factors matter is old. The operational implications of that recognition have been slow to develop.

For most of American health system history, the social determinants of health were treated as a downstream filter. The clinical system did clinical things. The social services system did social things. When a patient appeared in the clinical system whose problems were primarily social, the clinical system tried to refer the patient to social services. The referrals were often unsuccessful. The patient cycled back through the clinical system, more expensively each time.

The book argues that this approach is structurally wrong. Social determinants of health are not a downstream filter. They are an upstream structural variable that shapes everything that happens downstream. Treating them as a downstream filter produces the system we have. Treating them as a structural variable produces a different system.

What does it mean operationally to treat social determinants as structural? It means that risk stratification includes social determinants. It means that care plans include social determinants. It means that quality measurement includes social determinants. It means that payment models include social determinants. It means that workforce includes social workers, community health workers, and benefits navigators alongside clinicians.

Vermont's VCCI program, which we examined in Chapter 10, is built around this principle. VCCI's risk stratification methodology explicitly includes social determinants of health as a domain alongside utilization, chronic condition burden, and care access. The clinical team includes social workers alongside nurses. The case management protocol includes social services navigation alongside medication reconciliation. The model is integrated by design.

Idea two. Vermont's eight social determinants of health domains.

Vermont's Agency of Human Services has formalized eight social determinants of health domains for use across state programs. The eight domains are housing stability, food security, transportation, employment and income, education, social connection, community safety, and health literacy.

The structural significance of formalizing the eight domains is that it creates a shared vocabulary across state programs. The clinical team and the social services team can use the same categories. The risk stratification system can score across the same dimensions. The quality measurement system can report on the same domains. The Medicaid eligibility system can identify the same vulnerabilities.

The eight domains are not exhaustive. There are other social determinants. The point is not that eight is the right number, but that having a defined and shared set is operationally superior to having an undefined and inconsistent set. Other states have similar frameworks with different numbers of domains and slightly different categorizations. The platform's research lab includes tools that work across these frameworks.

The integration of social determinants screening into clinical workflow is operationally complex. The screening must be brief enough to fit into the visit. The screening must produce data that the care team can act on. The screening must connect to operational referral pathways. The clinical staff must be trained to handle the conversations that screening produces. The patient must trust the team enough to disclose accurately.

Vermont's primary care practices, under the Blueprint, have been working on social determinants integration for years. The work is uneven across practices. The most operationally mature practices have integrated screening into standard intake, have connected screening results to Community Health Team referrals, and have measured outcomes from the resulting interventions. The least mature practices have implemented screening as a documentation exercise without operational follow-through.

The lesson is that social determinants integration is operational work, not policy work. The policy work creates the framework. The operational work makes the framework function.

Idea three. Algorithmic bias in clinical artificial intelligence.

We introduced artificial intelligence governance in Chapter 6 and addressed it operationally in Chapter 7. Here we examine the specific equity dimension.

Clinical artificial intelligence is, on average, trained on data that reflects historical patterns of clinical care. Those patterns include disparities by race, ethnicity, sex, age, and socioeconomic status. An artificial intelligence model that learns from biased data will produce biased outputs unless explicit countermeasures are taken.

The most cited example in the academic literature is a hospital cost-prediction algorithm that systematically underestimated the clinical need of Black patients, because the algorithm used historical healthcare spending as a proxy for clinical need, and Black patients had historically received less care for any given level of clinical need. The algorithm did not encode racism. It encoded the consequence of racism. The result was the same.

Algorithmic bias auditing is the operational discipline of identifying these effects before they produce harm at scale. The discipline includes stratified performance measurement, equity-specific validation populations, ongoing monitoring of disparate outcomes, and explicit governance processes for handling identified bias.

The state of the field is uneven. Some health systems do this well. Most do not. The federal regulatory framework is developing. The civil rights enforcement framework is in place but slow. The clinical professional society guidance is improving but not yet uniform.

The book's view is that algorithmic bias is a serious and growing problem and that the operational infrastructure for addressing it is being built more slowly than the deployment of clinical artificial intelligence is moving. The risk is real. The Equity pillar response must be specific, operational, and ongoing.

Idea four. Access disparity in rural Vermont.

Vermont's rural geography produces specific access disparities that are different from the disparities of urban America. The disparities are not principally about insurance coverage. Vermont has one of the highest insurance coverage rates in the country. The disparities are about geographic access. The drive to the nearest specialist. The availability of behavioral health appointments. The reliability of broadband for telehealth. The presence of public transportation. The accessibility of grocery stores with fresh food.

Rural access disparity is a different operational problem from urban access disparity. The interventions that work in urban settings do not always work in rural settings. The clinical infrastructure must be designed for the rural geography. The transportation interventions must address the absence of public transit. The telehealth infrastructure must address rural broadband gaps. The workforce strategy must address rural recruitment and retention specifically.

Vermont's transformation strategy includes specific attention to rural access. The Rural Health Transformation Program funds rural broadband, rural workforce, and rural clinical infrastructure. The Clinically Integrated Network architecture is designed to support rural hospitals operationally. The Blueprint extends to rural practices on the same terms as urban practices. The Designated Agencies cover rural counties on the same terms as urban counties.

The structural lesson is that equity in a rural-and-mixed state is geographic equity. The framework that works for racial and ethnic equity in urban America does not translate directly. Vermont's transformation has had to develop its own operational vocabulary for rural equity, drawing on the broader equity framework but adapting it to the specific structural realities of rural geography.
