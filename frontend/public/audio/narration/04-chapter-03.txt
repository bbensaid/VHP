Chapter 3. The Execution Sequence in Practice.

Chapter 2 made the structural case for sequencing. Chapter 3 applies it to Vermont's live implementation timeline. We will walk through what is happening, what is scheduled, what is at risk, and what the next eighteen months will reveal. The goal is to show how the abstract sequencing argument cashes out as a practical implementation plan.

Vermont's transformation, as of two thousand twenty-six, is operating on a timeline that runs through approximately two thousand thirty. The major milestones, in rough order, are as follows.

Act 167, signed two thousand twenty-two, established the legislative mandate for a comprehensive system diagnostic. The result was the Oliver Wyman System Redesign Blueprint, published in two thousand twenty-three. The Blueprint identified the structural failure modes of the Vermont hospital sector, mapped the rural hospital sustainability crisis, and laid out the conceptual basis for a state-level all-payer global budget regime. Act 167 is, in framework terms, a Policy pillar action that authorized the diagnostic work required to align the other pillars.

Act 62, signed two thousand twenty-four, reorganized Vermont's health information exchange governance. The existing Vermont Information Technology Leaders organization, known as VITL, had limitations that the next phase of transformation could not absorb. Act 62 restructured the governance, clarified authority, and set the stage for the technology infrastructure that Act 68 would require. Act 62 is, in framework terms, a Technology pillar action triggered by a Policy pillar authorization.

Act 68, signed two thousand twenty-five, is the central operational mandate. Act 68 establishes mandatory hospital global budgets, reference-based pricing infrastructure, and the integration of the federal Achieving Healthcare Efficiency Through Accountable Design model, known as AHEAD, into the state-level reform. Implementation runs through two thousand thirty. Act 68 is, in framework terms, an Economics pillar action that depends on the Technology pillar progress made under Act 62 and the diagnostic foundation laid by Act 167.

The federal Rural Health Transformation Program, signed into law as part of H.R. 1 in July two thousand twenty-five, awarded Vermont one hundred and ninety-five million dollars in capital investment for rural hospital transformation. Vermont's award was issued in December two thousand twenty-five. The funds support telehealth, artificial intelligence scribe technology, broadband infrastructure, and the development of clinically integrated networks. The Rural Health Transformation Program is, in framework terms, a Technology and Operations pillar capital investment that supports the implementation of Act 68's Economics pillar mandate.

The AHEAD model, beginning two thousand twenty-six, brings Medicare into Vermont's all-payer alignment. AHEAD is, in framework terms, a federal-state Policy alignment that activates the full Economics pillar reach. Without Medicare alignment, the global budget logic of Act 68 cannot reach the population segment that drives the majority of state hospital admissions.

The Blueprint for Health, the Vermont Chronic Care Initiative known as VCCI, the SASH housing-based care program, and the eleven Designated Agencies that provide community behavioral health services are, in framework terms, Clinical pillar infrastructure that has been operating for years and is now being asked to function inside the new economic regime. We will examine each of these in Chapter 10.

That is the sequence as currently scheduled. Now let us examine what is at risk.

Risk one. Technology pillar incompleteness. Act 62 restructured governance. It did not, by itself, complete the technology infrastructure required by Act 68. The all-payer claims database, known as VHCURES, must be fully integrated with the hospital cost-reporting infrastructure of the Green Mountain Care Board. The health information exchange must support the data flows required by population health management programs. The hospital and primary care electronic health record systems must support the interoperability that AHEAD and Act 68 assume. None of this is automatic. Each of these elements is in active development, and each must be substantially complete before the corresponding Economics pillar mechanisms can be turned on without producing a failure cascade similar to OneCare.

Risk two. Workforce capacity. Vermont's behavioral health workforce, primary care workforce, and case management workforce are all under capacity strain. The Clinical pillar models of Chapter 10 assume staffing levels that, in rural Vermont, are not currently available. The Operations pillar in Vermont, as elsewhere, is constrained by labor market conditions that no policy lever can directly fix. Workforce strategies under Act 68 and the Rural Health Transformation Program are critical and underdeveloped.

Risk three. Hospital financial sustainability. Several Vermont hospitals operate with thin margins, limited cash on hand, and aging facilities. The Green Mountain Care Board's hospital financial monitoring identifies a small number of hospitals as financially fragile. The transition to global budgets, if implemented without adequate operational support, could destabilize the very hospitals whose sustainability the transformation is intended to secure.

Risk four. Federal policy disruption. H.R. 1, signed July two thousand twenty-five, mandates Medicaid work requirements, restricts provider tax mechanisms, and produces a post-two thousand thirty Medicaid funding cliff. The Vermont transformation strategy was designed before H.R. 1 was final. Vermont's exposure to Medicaid work requirements is comparatively modest, but the long-term Medicaid funding pressure that H.R. 1 produces will overlap with the operational implementation of Act 68 in ways that the original Act 68 design did not fully anticipate.

Risk five. Political continuity. Vermont's transformation depends on sustained executive and legislative support across multiple election cycles. The political consensus that produced Acts 167 and 68 is currently intact but cannot be taken for granted. We address political sustainability in Chapter 18.

Those are five identified risks. There are others. The book does not claim Vermont's transformation will succeed. It claims Vermont's transformation is the most thoughtfully sequenced live transformation in the country, and that watching it run is the most informative empirical exercise available to a healthcare policy professional in two thousand twenty-six.

Now to the three principles introduced in Chapter 2, applied to Vermont's current state.

Principle one. The critical path. Vermont's critical path runs through Technology pillar completion, AHEAD integration, and Act 68 hospital global budget implementation. These three items must remain on schedule. Slippage in any of them puts the others at risk. The Green Mountain Care Board's quarterly reports and the Agency of Human Services Act 68 implementation updates, which we discuss in Chapter 18 and which are tracked on the platform's Vermont Legislative Resources Library, are the principal documentary tracking instruments.

Principle two. Parallel work. The Clinical pillar work, the Equity pillar work, the workforce strategy work, and the Operations pillar work can all proceed in parallel with the critical path. These are the things that take a long time and that the system cannot afford to leave until the critical path is complete. Vermont is using the eighteen months between Act 68 signing and full implementation to advance parallel work substantially.

Principle three. Equity as a design constraint. Vermont's eight social determinants of health domains, the integration of two-one-one Vermont as the community resource referral platform, and the operational integration of SDOH screening into both the VCCI program and primary care practices, are all examples of equity being designed in rather than added on. The book argues this is the right strategy. Chapter 13 develops the equity measurement framework that allows the strategy to be evaluated.

A final observation about Vermont's current state. Vermont is small. Its transformation is small in absolute terms, even when it is large in proportional terms. The state's six hundred forty-seven thousand residents, of whom roughly thirty percent are Medicaid-eligible, do not generate the statistical power that a California or a Texas would generate. This is a limitation. It is also, paradoxically, an asset. Vermont's small size makes it possible to see the entire transformation at once. The trade-off is generalizability for visibility. In Chapter 17, we discuss how to think about generalizing from Vermont to states that lack Vermont's structural completeness.

We have now spent three chapters on the framework, the sequencing argument, and the implementation timeline. The remainder of the book moves pillar by pillar.

In Chapter 4, we turn to the Policy pillar. We examine the legislative architecture for structural reform. We walk through Vermont's Acts 167 and 68 in detail. We introduce reference-based pricing as the architecture that precedes global budgets. And we connect Vermont's policy choices to the broader federal policy environment that frames every state-level transformation effort.
