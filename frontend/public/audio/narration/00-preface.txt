Preface. A System at the Breaking Point.

Welcome to Transforming American Healthcare. Before we begin, a few words about why this book exists, and why now.

The American healthcare system spends more than four and a half trillion dollars every year. That is roughly seventeen percent of the national economy. It is more, per person, than any other country on earth. And in return for that spending, the United States ranks last, or near last, among wealthy nations on nearly every measure of population health that we know how to count. Life expectancy. Maternal mortality. Infant mortality. Preventable death. Chronic disease burden. Clinical workforce burnout. The data is not ambiguous, and it has not been ambiguous for decades.

This book begins from a single premise. The American healthcare system is not just expensive. It is structurally failing. And the difference matters, because expense can be managed through cost containment. Structural failure cannot. Structural failure requires transformation.

Transformation is a word that has been used so loosely in healthcare that it has nearly lost its meaning. Every consulting deck promises transformation. Every vendor pitch promises transformation. Every state agency annual report claims to be in the middle of transformation. The word has been worn smooth from overuse. Part of the work of this book is to put the edges back on it.

In the chapters that follow, transformation will be defined specifically. It is the simultaneous redesign of six interlocking pillars of the health system. Policy. Economics. Technology. Clinical care. Equity. And operations. Each of these pillars is necessary. None of them is sufficient. And the order in which they are addressed is not optional.

Most reform efforts in the United States over the last forty years have tried to move one pillar at a time. A new payment model, in isolation. A new electronic health record, in isolation. A new clinical care pathway, in isolation. A new equity initiative, in isolation. The pattern is consistent, and the result is consistent. Single-pillar reforms either fail outright, or they produce marginal gains that fade once the political attention has moved on. The system reverts. The structural problem persists.

The argument of this book is that the only reform strategy with any chance of producing durable change is one that moves all six pillars together, and in the correct sequence. That argument is not original. The framework was developed at the Health Transformation Review by working alongside policy professionals, healthcare executives, clinicians, and Vermont state government over a period of several years. What is new in this book is the integration of the framework with a complete state-level case study, namely the state of Vermont, and the development of an explicit map of fifteen dependency relationships between the pillars that allows readers to predict where reforms will fail before they fail.

You may be wondering why Vermont. The answer is that Vermont is the most legislatively complete state-level transformation in the country. Vermont's Act 167, signed into law in 2022, mandated a comprehensive system diagnostic that produced the Oliver Wyman System Redesign Blueprint. Vermont's Act 68, signed in 2025, took the diagnostic conclusions and built a mandatory global budget regime around them, with a multi-year implementation timeline running through the end of this decade. No other state has done what Vermont has done. Maryland has had global hospital budgets for over a decade, but only in the hospital sector, and without the surrounding clinical and equity infrastructure. Oregon has had Coordinated Care Organizations for thirteen years, but on a voluntary basis and without the legislative architecture Vermont has built. California has the largest state Medicaid transformation in the country, called CalAIM, but at a scale and complexity that makes its findings hard to generalize.

Vermont's combination of legislative completeness, data transparency, and small population size makes it the closest thing the United States has to a real-world laboratory for healthcare system transformation. If you can model Vermont, you can model anywhere. That is a claim we will defend over the course of the book.

A note about who this book is written for. The book is not written for healthcare consumers or patients, except in the broad sense that everyone is a healthcare consumer eventually. It is written for the people who design, run, regulate, finance, build technology for, study, and try to fix the American health system. State Medicaid directors. Hospital chief executives and chief financial officers. Accountable care organization leadership. Health policy professionals. Practicing clinicians transitioning into value-based care. Graduate students in health policy and health economics. Researchers studying state-level reform. Federal policymakers who want a comparable benchmark for their own state. Vermont implementers, for whom the book is partly written.

If you fall into one of those groups, the book is for you. If you do not, the book may still be useful, but it will demand more of you than a general-interest book would. The vocabulary is technical, the evidence is dense, and the assumed background is healthcare-fluent rather than introductory.

A note about how the book is organized. There are twenty chapters, organized into six sections. Chapters one through three lay out the framework and the sequencing argument. Chapters four and five address the Policy pillar. Chapters six and seven address the Technology pillar. Chapters eight and nine address the Economics pillar. Chapters ten and eleven address the Clinical pillar. Chapters twelve and thirteen address the Equity pillar. Chapters fourteen and fifteen address the Operations pillar. And chapters sixteen through twenty address the cross-cutting and future-facing material, including political sustainability, portfolio management, and the Vermont Agency of Human Services restructuring roadmap.

Within each pillar pair, the first chapter develops the theoretical and structural argument, while the second chapter focuses on operational implementation. You can read the book in order, or you can read by pillar, or you can read by reader profile. The reader's guide in Appendix G suggests a path for each of the four primary reader profiles. Use whichever entry point makes sense for your work.

A note about the Health Transformation Review Platform. This book is the intellectual foundation of a companion platform, available at the address printed in the front matter. The platform contains interactive tools, real-time data, simulators, a research lab, and Vermont-specific intelligence that updates continuously. The book provides the structural argument. The platform provides the current evidence and the working analytical instruments. Either can be used alone, but they are designed to be used together. Appendix G of the book gives a chapter-by-chapter map to the corresponding platform sections.

A note about currency. The data in this book is current as of the spring of two thousand twenty-six. Healthcare policy moves quickly. Some of the specific numbers in this book will be out of date by the time you read it. The structural arguments, however, are designed to be durable. The framework is meant to outlast its data. When data and framework disagree, trust the platform for the data, and the book for the framework.

A final word. This book is, in some ways, a hopeful book. It argues that transformation is possible. It identifies specific operational steps that work. It documents states that are making real progress. It does not pretend that the political and economic obstacles are easy.

But it does insist that those obstacles are smaller than the cost of continuing the present system. The present system is, every year, more expensive, more inequitable, more inefficient, and more demoralizing for the people who work in it. The status quo is not stable. Something will replace it. The only question is whether what replaces it is designed thoughtfully, or arrives as the wreckage of repeated failure.

This book is written for the people designing what comes next.

Let us begin.
