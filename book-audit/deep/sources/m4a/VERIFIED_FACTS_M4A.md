## A. Vermont Act 48 of 2011 (act48_2011_as_enacted)

- Act 48 (H.202) **approved May 26, 2011** (p.141).
- **Created the Green Mountain Care Board**: "On July 1, 2011, the Green Mountain Care board is created and shall
  consist of a chair and four members" — 18 V.S.A. § 9374(a)(1) (p.21). Members nominated by a nominating
  committee, appointed and confirmed "in the manner of a superior judge".
- Created the Vermont health benefit exchange (33 V.S.A. ch. 18, subch. 1); exchange to enroll by **Nov 1, 2013**
  and be fully operational by **Jan 1, 2014** (Sec. 34(c)(2), p.140). Purpose: "to the extent allowable under
  federal law or a waiver of federal law, becomes the mechanism to create Green Mountain Care" (§ 1801(a), p.54).
- **Green Mountain Care (GMC) implementation was conditional** — 90 days after the LAST of (33 V.S.A. § 1822(a),
  pp.73–74):
  1. receipt of an ACA **§ 1332 waiver**;
  2. enactment of a law **establishing the financing** for GMC;
  3. GMCB approval of the initial benefit package;
  4. enactment of appropriations for that package;
  5. a GMCB determination that: (A) every covered resident gets benefits of **≥80% actuarial value**; (B) GMC
     "will not have a negative aggregate impact on Vermont's economy"; (C) financing is sustainable;
     (D) administrative expenses will be reduced; (E) cost containment will slow per-capita spending growth;
     (F) providers paid enough to recruit and retain.
- **Act 48 did NOT itself finance GMC.** Sec. 9 ordered the Secretary of Administration to recommend two
  financing plans by **Jan 15, 2013** (p.101), and § 1828 a later budget proposal.
- **Eligibility**: "all Vermont residents shall be eligible ... regardless of whether an employer offers health
  insurance" (§ 1824(a)(1), p.77); non-residents not eligible (§ 1824(a)(3)).
- **Medicare**: AHS "shall seek permission from [CMS] to be the administrator for the Medicare program in Vermont.
  If ... unsuccessful ..., Green Mountain Care shall be the **secondary payer**" for Medicare-covered services
  (§ 1827(e), p.85).
- **Other coverage**: GMC is secondary payer to "any other health benefit plan, including private health
  insurance, retiree health benefits, or federal health benefit plans offered by the Veterans' Administration, by
  the military, or to federal employees" (§ 1827(f), p.85).
- **Medicaid/CHIP**: AHS "may seek" a § 1115 waiver (Medicaid) and § 2107(e)(2)(A) (CHIP) to include them; if
  not obtained, GMC is secondary payer to them (§ 1827(g), p.85).
- **GMC Fund** (§ 1829, p.87): the "single source to finance health care coverage"; federal Medicaid, Medicare and
  exchange funds go in only "**if authorized by a waiver from federal law**".

## B. The Hsiao report (Act 128 of 2010; released January 2011) (hsiao_overview_langweil)

- Three options designed: (1) government-run single payer, (2) public option, (3) public-private single payer —
  Hsiao recommended option 3 (p.1, p.4).
- Design assumptions: implementation in **2015**; "No change for Medicare beneficiaries"; long-term care excluded;
  financing by **payroll tax** (employer + employee), payroll capped at $106,800 (pp.1, 4).
- Estimated accumulated savings **16.1%–25.3%** depending on option; report itself called savings "inherently
  uncertain" (p.2).
- Assumed a federal grant equal to the exchange subsidies and small-business credits Vermont would otherwise get
  (p.4) — the § 1332 pass-through.

## C. The 2013 financing plan (vt_financing_plan_act48s9_2013-01; UMass Medical School + Wakely; Jan 24, 2013)

- 2017 sources of funds with reform (Table 7, p.59, $M): individuals & employers 332; **Medicare 1,613**;
  Medicaid match 1,247; **ACA waiver 267**; other federal (FEHB, VA etc.) 209; state Medicaid 637.
  Total system cost with reform 5,916. **Amount to be financed: $1,611M.**
- "The federal Medicare program will continue to cover approximately $1.6 billion in costs incurred by Medicare
  beneficiaries" (p.59) — i.e. Medicare stayed outside the state system.
- 2010 coverage table: **150,000 Vermonters in self-insured plans** (of 334,000 commercially insured);
  54,000 uninsured (Table 1, p.15).
- The plan "did not designate specific revenue sources" (Avalere p.2, confirming).

## D. Avalere evaluation (Nov 14, 2013) — interested-party source

- Commissioned by **Vermont Partners for Health Care Reform** (provider/employer coalition) (p.1).
- Judged a "more appropriate expected cost" at **$1.9–$2.2 billion, about 20–35% higher** than $1.61B (p.2).
- "State officials acknowledge that Vermont has no power to regulate the actions of self-insured employers, whose
  health benefits programs are governed by the federal Employee Retirement Income Security Act (ERISA)" (p.8).
- Providers might not realise admin savings because they would still bill Medicare, ERISA plans and out-of-state
  patients, "all of whom will be outside of Vermont's single-payer program" (p.4).
- Multi-state self-insured employers "cannot be required to participate in GMC as they are governed by the
  federal ERISA law, though they may still be called upon to help fund GMC" (p.22).

## E. The December 2014 decision (gmc_financing_report_2014-12-30; Dec 30, 2014)

- "On **December 17, 2014**, ... the Governor announced that he could not recommend that the legislature move
  forward with public financing at this time ... the risk of economic shock is too high" (p.6).
- Design: covers all Vermonters **except those on Medicare or TRICARE**, plus out-of-state commuters to Vermont
  employers (p.7, p.15). Maturity enrollment 544,000 in year 3 (p.15).
- 2017 coverage (Table 1, p.16): GMC 519,000; **Medicare 140,000 stays Medicare**; TRICARE/VA 10,000; ESI falls
  from 296,000 to 31,000; Medicaid 141,000, individual market 49,000, uninsured 17,000, non-resident commuters
  61,000 all into GMC.
- **ERISA**: "Any business could continue to provide health benefits ... under [ERISA]. This includes the ability
  to self-insure"; employees could take GMC or keep employer coverage as primary with GMC secondary (p.19).
- Benefits: ~94% AV (avg 6% out of pocket); **no long-term care, adult dental, adult vision, hearing** (p.7, p.20).
- **Federal § 1332 pass-through estimate fell to $106M from $267M** in the 2013 report; "our federal partners have
  not yet approved Vermont's methodology" (p.40). Federal Medicaid match $1.31B in 2017, assuming the 1115 waiver
  terms continued (p.41).
- **Balance sheet** (Table 13, p.51, $M, 2017→2021): cost 4,340→5,177; **new revenue needed 2,580→3,174**;
  payroll tax **11.5%** yields 1,510→1,639; public premium (0–9.5% of income) 1,247→1,381; fund position
  +177, +92, **−2 (2019), −95, −154** — i.e. deficits from year 3 even with the 11.5% tax and **no** small-business
  phase-in (p.51; "does not include a phase-in of the payroll tax, which is a policy priority of the Governor's").
- Dec 17 slides (costa_presentation, pp.3, 7–8) — same figures, plus: state Medicaid funding $150M below the 2013
  estimate; replacing provider taxes cost $158M; $75M General Fund reduction FY16–17; transitioning small firms
  "would reduce revenue by $500+ million"; an 80% AV plan was "only 14% less expensive"; excluding out-of-state
  commuters saves "$200+ million". (Cite the official report for numbers; use slides only for these extras.)

## F. Federal law constraints on a state

- **ERISA § 514(a)**, 29 U.S.C. § 1144(a): ERISA "shall supersede any and all State laws insofar as they may now or
  hereafter relate to any employee benefit plan". § 514(b)(2)(B) (deemer clause): an employee benefit plan is not
  deemed an insurer for state insurance law. **Only statutory exemption: Hawaii Prepaid Health Care Act**,
  § 514(b)(5) — and only as in effect Sept 2, 1974.
- **ACA § 1332**, 42 U.S.C. § 18052: a state may waive ONLY (a)(2): Parts A and B of subchapter III (QHP/exchange
  rules), § 18071 (cost-sharing reductions), and IRC §§ 36B, 4980H, 5000A. Plan years from **Jan 1, 2017**.
  Granted only if coverage is ≥ as comprehensive, ≥ as affordable, covers a comparable number, and **"will not
  increase the Federal deficit"** (b)(1). Pass-through = "the aggregate amount of such credits or reductions that
  would have been paid" (a)(3).
- **[analysis]** § 1332 cannot touch Medicare (title XVIII), Medicaid (title XIX; needs § 1115) or ERISA. A state
  can therefore capture only the ACA subsidy stream (Vermont's: $106M of a $4.34B program in 2017) — which is
  exactly why Act 48 had to make GMC a secondary payer to Medicare, federal plans and (absent waivers) Medicaid,
  and why ERISA plans could stay out while being taxed.

## G. Federal bills — what S.1506 / H.R.3069 do that a state cannot

**S.1506, Medicare for All Act (Sen. Sanders, I-VT)** — introduced **Apr 29, 2025**; referred to **Finance**; no
further action. **19 cosponsors, all Democrats** (15 original), per billstatus updated 2026-11-20; most recent
Duckworth 2026-11-19, Wyden 2026-09-24. Vermont: **Welch** cosponsors.
- § 102: every US resident entitled. § 106: full benefits **Jan 1 of the 4th calendar year after enactment**;
  children under 19 in year 1. § 1011: buy-in to Medicare at **55 / 45 / 35** in years 1–3.
- § 107: unlawful for insurers to sell, or **employers to provide**, coverage duplicating program benefits;
  supplemental coverage allowed.
- § 202: no cost-sharing except an optional evidence-based Rx schedule.
- § 204: **institutional long-term care stays in state Medicaid** (state plans must cover it, with a
  maintenance-of-effort requirement); home/community LTC is a program benefit (CRS summary).
- § 611: **hospitals, SNFs, dialysis paid quarterly lump-sum global budgets** ("payment in full for all operating
  expenses"); § 612 fee-for-service for individual providers; § 615 separates capital.
- § 701 Trust Fund: receives the prior-year appropriations for **Medicare, Medicaid, FEHB, MCH etc.** (CPI-
  adjusted) plus any residual HI/SMI trust fund balances. **The bill contains no new tax or revenue title** — its
  only IRC amendment is a premium-credit rule for transition-plan enrollees.
- § 801: **amends ERISA itself** — new § 524 bars employee benefit plans from duplicating program benefits.
- § 901: ends Medicare, Medicaid and CHIP benefits for services after the start date; **VA, TRICARE and IHS
  unaffected** (§ 901(c)). § 902: ACA exchanges terminate.
- CRS: "The bill's program must be fully implemented four years after enactment."

**H.R.3069, Medicare for All Act (Rep. Jayapal)** — introduced Apr 29, 2025; referred to E&C and six other
committees; **118 cosponsors, all Democrats** (105 original), billstatus updated 2026-10-23; Vermont: **Balint**.
Differences: benefits **2 years** after enactment (under-19 and 55+ after 1 year) (§ 106); **long-term services
and supports (institutional and home) are a federal benefit** (§ 204). Same no-revenue-title structure (§ 701).

**H.R.10924, Medicare for America Act of 2026 (Rep. DeLauro)** — introduced **Oct 29, 2026**; 0 cosponsors.
Not single-payer in the S.1506 sense: covers all residents, fully implemented **2028**; transitional public option
on exchanges 2028–2029 (CRS). **Keeps "qualified employer-based coverage"** and Medicare Advantage for America
plans; premiums capped at **8% of income**, zero below 200% FPL (§ 104); small employers (payroll ≤ $2M or <100
staff) provide coverage or enroll staff. **Has a tax title** (surtax, Medicare payroll tax, NIIT, tobacco, alcohol,
sugared-drink excises; sunset of P.L. 115-97 and 119-21).

**[analysis] Federal vs state — the four Vermont failures a federal law removes:**
1. Medicare: S.1506 § 701/§ 901 moves Medicare's appropriation into the program; Vermont could only *ask* CMS
   (§ 1827(e)) and left $1.6B / 140,000 people outside.
2. ERISA: S.1506 § 801 amends ERISA; Vermont could not (29 U.S.C. § 1144(a)), so self-insured plans
   (150,000 people in 2010) could stay out.
3. Federal money: a state gets only the § 1332 pass-through (Vermont: $267M→$106M estimate) under a deficit-
   neutrality guardrail; Congress appropriates directly.
4. Financing base: Vermont had to raise ~$2.6B of new revenue in 2017 (11.5% payroll + up to 9.5% income
   premium) from one small state's tax base, against a 2017 program cost of $4.34B; a federal program draws on
   the national base and federal borrowing — though CBO warns perpetual borrowing "would be unsustainable"
   (CBO 2022). (Do NOT write anything about a Vermont balanced-budget rule — not verified either way.)
   **What a federal law does NOT remove:** the revenue question itself — S.1506 and H.R.3069 name no revenue.

## H. CBO (cbo_swagel_testimony_2022-05-12)

- CBO analysed **five illustrative options**; it **has not analyzed S.1129 (2019 Sanders bill)**. No CBO score of
  S.1506 / H.R.3069 exists in the sources here.
- 2030 vs current law: **federal subsidies +$1.5T to +$3.0T**; **national health expenditures −$0.7T to +$0.3T**.
  Biggest savings drivers: lower provider payment rates and lower payer admin; biggest cost driver: more care used,
  "especially if long-term services and supports are a covered benefit".
- Virtually all residents enrolled; out-of-pocket falls; demand rises more than supply → "greater unmet demand",
  "increased congestion ... including delays and forgone care".
- Lower-rate scenario: hospitals −13%, physicians −7%, drugs −23% vs the higher-rate scenario → federal subsidies
  −12%, NHE −9%.
- Economy (payroll- or income-tax financed): **GDP ~1% to 10% lower by 2030**; borrowing-financed perpetually
  "would be unsustainable".
- Long-term: lower payments "might cause fewer people to enter health care professions and fewer new drugs";
  providers unable to adjust "could cease to operate".
