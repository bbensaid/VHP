## aiml-performance-metrics
42	When a hospital administrator or a vendor pitches an AI model with the claim that it is "98% accurate," that single number almost always conceals more than it reveals.
43	Healthcare is filled with exactly these situations: sepsis affects roughly 2% of ICU admissions, early-stage pancreatic cancer is present in less than 0.1% of asymptomatic adults, and pulmonary embolism occurs in fewer than 5% of emergency patients with chest pain.
44	In every one of these scenarios, a model that simply predicts "negative" for every single patient will achieve accuracy well above 95% — while being completely useless clinically.
45	A model predicting no sepsis for every patient is 98% accurate in a 2% prevalence ICU population — yet clinically useless, identifying zero true cases
46	An AUROC of 0.5 means the model performs no better than a coin flip; clinically useful models need AUROC ≥ 0.75 as a baseline starting point
47	The Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve is one of the most fundamental visualizations in clinical AI evaluation.
48	It is constructed by plotting, at every possible decision threshold, the true positive rate (sensitivity) on the y-axis against the false positive rate (1 − specificity) on the x-axis.
49	A model that perfectly discriminates patients who have a condition from those who do not would trace a curve through the upper-left corner of the plot — sensitivity of 1.0, false positive rate of 0 — at some threshold.
50	AUROC ranges from 0.5 (random) to 1.0 (perfect).
51	In clinical practice, an AUROC below 0.60 is generally considered unacceptable, 0.70–0.79 is acceptable for lower-stakes decisions, 0.80–0.89 is considered good, and values above 0.90 are excellent.
52	For clinical decision support alerts, a threshold of ≥ 0.75 is commonly cited as a minimum bar worth evaluating further.
53	Internal validation reported an AUROC of approximately 0.76–0.83, which appears acceptable on paper.
54	However, a landmark 2021 external validation study by Wong et al. published in JAMA Internal Medicine evaluated the model across 38,455 hospitalizations at the University of Michigan and found a dramatically different picture in actual practice.
55	The model achieved an AUROC of only 0.63, a sensitivity of just 33%, and — most alarmingly — a positive predictive value of only 12%.
56	In practical terms, this means that 88 out of every 100 sepsis alerts fired by the system were false alarms, and 67% of patients who actually developed sepsis were not flagged at all.
57	Alerts were generated for 18% of all hospitalized patients, creating a massive and well-documented burden of alert fatigue.
58	Consider a model with 95% sensitivity and 95% specificity — excellent performance by almost any standard.
59	Applied to a disease with 20% prevalence, the PPV is approximately 83%: most positive flags represent true cases and the model is clinically actionable.
60	Now apply that same model unchanged to a community where the disease affects only 1% of patients.
61	The PPV collapses to approximately 16%: 84 out of every 100 positive alerts are false alarms.
62	(2021) study documented with the Epic Sepsis Model.
63	Even a modest difference — say, 15% vs. 5% — can reduce PPV by more than half, transforming a useful tool into an alert fatigue generator.

69	Calibration, F1 Score, and the Metrics Beyond AUROC
70	If a model flags a patient as having a "30% probability of sepsis" but patients at that risk score actually develop sepsis only 8% of the time, the model is overconfident — its predicted probabilities do not match reality.
71	A well-calibrated model means that among all patients assigned a predicted risk of X%, approximately X% of them actually experience the event.
72	The most commonly used summary measure of calibration is the Brier Score: the mean squared error between the model's predicted probability and the actual binary outcome (0 or 1).
73	A Brier Score of 0 is perfect; a score of 0.25 corresponds to an uninformative model that assigns 50% probability to every patient regardless of their actual risk.
74	Calibration plots, also called reliability diagrams, visually display predicted probability on the x-axis against observed event rate on the y-axis; a perfectly calibrated model traces the 45-degree diagonal.
75	The F1 score addresses this by combining precision (equivalent to PPV) and recall (equivalent to sensitivity) into a single harmonic mean: F1 = 2 × (Precision × Recall) ÷ (Precision + Recall).
76	F1 is particularly useful when both false positives and false negatives carry significant costs, and when the positive class (the rare event, such as sepsis or readmission) is the focus of clinical concern.
77	The most clinically sophisticated evaluation framework available today is Decision Curve Analysis (DCA), introduced by Vickers and Elkin in 2006 in Medical Decision Making.
78	AUROC ≥ 0.75 is a starting bar, not a finish line.
79	A model with AUROC of 0.80 can still be clinically worthless if PPV at your institution's prevalence is 8%.
80	If the model predicts 40% risk and the observed event rate for those patients is only 10%, the model is systematically overconfident and the predicted probabilities cannot be used for patient counseling or risk stratification decisions.
81	Perhaps the most consequential and underappreciated dimension of clinical AI evaluation is equitable performance across patient subgroups.
82	One of the most cited and consequential demonstrations of algorithmic bias in healthcare is the landmark 2019 study by Obermeyer et al. published in Science.
83	The study estimated that this bias reduced the proportion of Black patients identified for care management programs by approximately 50% relative to medically equivalent white patients.
84	Sjoding et al., writing in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2020, analyzed pulse oximetry measurements paired with arterial blood gas (the gold standard) measurements across thousands of patients in ICUs at the University of Michigan and 178 other hospitals.

## aiml-readmission-prediction
158	A hospital readmission is defined as an inpatient admission that occurs within 30 days of a preceding discharge from an acute care hospital.
159	The modern policy focus on readmissions was crystallized by a landmark 2009 study in the New England Journal of Medicine by Stephen Jencks, Mark Williams, and Eric Coleman.
160	Analyzing Medicare fee-for-service data, the authors found that 19.6 percent of the 11.8 million Medicare beneficiaries discharged from hospital were readmitted within 30 days — approximately one in five patients.
161	Even more striking: 34 percent were readmitted within 90 days, and half of the 30-day readmissions occurred without the patient having seen a physician in an outpatient setting after discharge.
162	The study estimated that unplanned rehospitalizations cost Medicare $17.4 billion in 2004 alone.
163	The Affordable Care Act of 2010 established the Hospital Readmissions Reduction Program (HRRP), which CMS began implementing in fiscal year 2013 (covering discharges from October 2012).
164	Under HRRP, hospitals with excess readmissions — meaning readmission rates statistically higher than expected for their patient mix — face a reduction of up to 3 percent on all Medicare base inpatient payments.
165	Preliminary CMS data for fiscal year 2026 show only 641 hospitals (21.8 percent) escaping a penalty — so about 78 percent of evaluated hospitals are penalized, and 240 (8.1 percent) face reductions of 1 percent or more — making it one of the most broadly applied financial penalties in Medicare.
166	A 30-day readmission is any unplanned return to an acute care hospital within 30 days of a prior discharge, regardless of the reason for readmission.

174	LACE is an acronym for its four components: Length of stay (0 to 7 points), Acuity of admission (3 points if the admission was unplanned), Comorbidity measured by the Charlson Comorbidity Index, and Emergency department visits in the six months before admission.

## aiml-sepsis-warning
227	Sepsis is defined by the Third International Consensus Definitions (Sepsis-3, 2016) as life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection.
228	Published by Singer and colleagues in JAMA, the Sepsis-3 framework replaced the older Systemic Inflammatory Response Syndrome (SIRS) criteria — which had become notorious for flagging nearly every critically ill patient — with a more specific standard grounded in organ dysfunction.
229	Under Sepsis-3, a diagnosis of sepsis requires an acute increase of two or more points in the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score, reflecting measurable deterioration in respiratory, renal, hepatic, cardiovascular, neurological, or hematological function.
230	Septic shock, the most severe manifestation, is defined as sepsis with circulatory and cellular abnormalities severe enough to substantially increase mortality — typically operationalized as the need for vasopressors to maintain a mean arterial pressure of 65 mmHg or greater plus a serum lactate above 2 mmol/L despite adequate fluid resuscitation.
231	According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), approximately 1.7 million adults develop sepsis in the US each year.
232	Updated CDC and AHRQ data indicate that over 300,000 deaths annually are attributable to sepsis — making it the leading cause of in-hospital death and the primary driver of ICU admissions.
233	AHRQ data through 2021 show that aggregate US hospital costs for sepsis exceed $52 billion annually, making it the single most expensive condition treated in American inpatient settings.
234	At the individual episode level, costs vary sharply by severity: sepsis without organ dysfunction averages roughly $39,000 per hospitalization; severe sepsis with organ dysfunction rises to approximately $61,000; and septic shock episodes average over $68,000.
235	The most potent intervention known to reduce sepsis mortality is speed: specifically, the rapid delivery of appropriate antibiotics.
236	A landmark 2006 retrospective study by Kumar and colleagues, published in Critical Care Medicine, analyzed 2,154 patients with septic shock and found that each hour of delay in initiating effective antimicrobial therapy was associated with an average 7.6% decrease in survival.
237	The Surviving Sepsis Campaign codified this urgency in its Hour-1 Bundle (2018), which specifies five elements to be initiated within sixty minutes of sepsis recognition: measure lactate; obtain blood cultures; administer broad-spectrum antibiotics; begin crystalloid fluid resuscitation at 30 mL/kg if hypotension or lactate is elevated; and initiate vasopressors to maintain a MAP of at least 65 mmHg if hemodynamic instability persists.

## aiml-deterioration-models
299	It is one of the most measurable and, in principle, most preventable causes of inpatient harm in modern hospitals.
300	According to American Heart Association data from the Get With The Guidelines-Resuscitation registry, approximately 292,000 adults experience in-hospital cardiac arrest (IHCA) in the United States each year.
301	Of those, only about 23–25% survive to hospital discharge.
302	Research using continuous vital sign monitoring has shown that the majority of in-hospital cardiac arrests are preceded by detectable physiological changes in the 4–8 hours before the event.
303	Approximately 23–25% of IHCA patients survive to hospital discharge; most arrests are preceded by detectable warning signs
304	Physiological changes — rising respiratory rate, falling blood pressure, declining SpO2 — often begin 4–8 hours before the arrest event
305	A 2015 meta-analysis found rapid response teams associated with a 35% reduction in in-hospital cardiac arrests; evidence quality remains mixed
306	Rapid Response Teams (RRTs) — also called Medical Emergency Teams (METs) — were introduced in the early 2000s specifically to address the failure-to-rescue gap.
307	The theory was sound: deploy a specialized clinical team to the bedside at the first sign of deterioration, before the patient codes.
308	Meta-analyses have shown promising results — one 2015 systematic review found statistically significant reductions in both cardiac arrests (approximately 35%) and in-hospital mortality (approximately 13%) associated with RRT implementation.
309	The National Early Warning Score (NEWS/NEWS2)
310	The National Early Warning Score, developed by the Royal College of Physicians in the United Kingdom, is the most widely validated rule-based deterioration tool in clinical use.
311	NEWS2 (the updated version, released in 2017) incorporates seven physiological parameters: respiratory rate, oxygen saturation (SpO2), supplemental oxygen use, temperature, systolic blood pressure, heart rate, and level of consciousness using the AVPU scale (Alert, Voice, Pain, Unresponsive).
312	Each parameter is scored 0–3 based on deviation from normal, and the aggregate score ranges from 0 to 20.
313	A score of 5–6 triggers an urgent nursing assessment and senior clinician review; a score of 7 or above triggers an emergency assessment and, typically, a rapid response or critical care team call.
314	The original Smith et al. validation study found AUROC values of 0.722 for cardiac arrest prediction within 24 hours, rising to 0.857 for unanticipated ICU admission and 0.894 for death — strong performance for a simple additive tool.
315	A large-scale Singapore validation study found NEWS2 achieved an AUROC of 0.898 for general ward deterioration, while a prospective Swedish multi-center trial found much weaker performance for in-hospital cardiac arrest specifically, with AUROC values of 0.62 for composite outcomes in patients already assessed by a rapid response team.
316	The honest characterization: NEWS/NEWS2 achieves roughly AUROC 0.72–0.80 for cardiac arrest prediction in diverse general ward populations, which is meaningful but leaves a substantial performance gap.
317	It is simpler to calculate and has a longer track record in US clinical practice, but consistently underperforms NEWS in head-to-head comparisons, with AUROC values typically in the range of 0.69–0.74 for composite deterioration outcomes.
318	It remains the most common benchmark against which ML models are compared in US-based studies.
319	They are assessed only when vital signs are manually recorded — typically every 4–8 hours on a general ward, and sometimes less frequently during busy overnight shifts.
320	A patient whose respiratory rate climbs from 18 to 26 breaths per minute over three hours may not have that trend captured at all until the next scheduled vital sign round.
321	And crucially, they apply the same threshold to all patients regardless of baseline: a heart rate of 105 bpm generates the same score for a 30-year-old athletic patient recovering from minor surgery and a 78-year-old with heart failure who runs at a resting rate of 95.
322	Universally interpretable — score of 7 means the same thing to any nurse
323	Assessed only at manual vital sign intervals (every 4–8 hours)
324	Continuous scoring — updates every 15 minutes or with each EHR data point
325	Higher potential AUROC (0.80–0.88 in published studies)
326	Machine learning deterioration models share a common architecture: they ingest a broad stream of EHR data — vitals, labs, medications, nursing assessments, intake/output records, and demographic variables — and produce a continuously updated risk score reflecting the probability of a composite adverse outcome (typically some combination of cardiac arrest, unplanned ICU transfer, and death) within a defined time window, usually 12–24 hours.
327	The electronic Cardiac Arrest Risk Triage (eCART) score, developed by Matthew Churpek and colleagues at the University of Chicago, is the most extensively published ML deterioration model in the peer-reviewed literature.
328	The landmark 2016 paper in Critical Care Medicine — "Multicenter Comparison of Machine Learning Methods and Conventional Regression for Predicting Clinical Deterioration on the Wards" — compared multiple ML approaches on data from five hospitals and found that a random forest model achieved AUROC of 0.80 for the composite outcome of cardiac arrest, ICU transfer, or death, outperforming conventional logistic regression and rule-based scores on the same dataset.
329	In a subsequent comparison across 107,868 admissions at five U.S. hospitals (Green et al., Resuscitation, 2018), which also tested Australia's "Between the Flags" calling criteria, eCART achieved AUROC of 0.801, compared to NEWS at 0.718 and MEWS at 0.698 for the same composite outcome.
330	A prospective feasibility study at the University of Chicago found eCART AUROC of 0.88 for cardiac arrest and 0.80 for ICU transfer — and critically, 8 of 10 patients who experienced a ward cardiac arrest had high-risk eCART scores in the hours before the event, while the RRT had been activated for only 2 of those 10 patients.
331	The most recent iteration, eCARTv5, trained on even larger datasets using gradient boosting, demonstrated improved performance across diverse subgroups and outperformed NEWS and MEWS in both retrospective and prospective validation.
332	The Epic Deterioration Index is the most widely deployed commercial deterioration model in US hospitals, embedded directly in the Epic EHR and available to the hundreds of large health systems that run on Epic.
333	The EDI uses gradient boosting and produces a score from 0 to 100 — higher scores indicating greater risk of a composite adverse outcome (rapid response, resuscitation, ICU-level care, or death) within the next 12–38 hours.
334	The model updates every 15 minutes, pulling from the full EHR data stream: vitals, labs, nursing assessments, intake/output records, and flowsheet documentation.
335	A systematic review and meta-analysis of externally validated Epic clinical decision support tools found seven studies across 15 sites reporting EDI results, making it one of Epic's more-studied proprietary tools.
336	A Yale New Haven Health System study found the EDI underperformed other early warning scores, reporting an AUROC of 0.808 — the lowest among tools tested.
337	Other implementations, including at RWJBarnabas Health, reported striking operational results: an estimated 100 lives saved and a nearly 30% reduction in ICU transfer mortality within six months of deployment.
338	Researchers at Penn Medicine conducted a simulated prospective evaluation of a deep recurrent neural network (DRNN) deterioration model, analyzing 146,446 hospitalizations from 103,930 unique patients across four Pennsylvania hospitals.
339	A deterioration model that accurately predicts clinical decline 4–6 hours in advance only prevents patient harm if a clinical response occurs within that window.
340	Studies on EHR-generated clinical alerts document override rates ranging from 49% to 96% — with some estimates showing that only 7.3% of alerts generated by clinical decision support systems are clinically appropriate.
341	A 2017 study in BMC Medical Informatics and Decision Making found that the likelihood of a clinician accepting a reminder fell by 30% for each additional reminder in an encounter, and by 10% for each 5-point rise in the share of reminders that were repeats for the same patient.
342	Physicians report seeing 100–200 alerts per day in some clinical environments; 81% report being overwhelmed by alert volume, and 55% admit dismissing alerts without reading them.

## aiml-fda-regulation
632	Not because the FDA is merely a compliance hurdle—but because the framework defines what counts as a medical device in the first place, which pathway to market a product must travel, how it may change after clearance, and what obligations follow it into clinical practice.
633	The 21st Century Cures Act of 2016 carved out certain clinical decision support software from device regulation.
634	The FDA's 2021 AI/ML Action Plan committed to a new oversight model suited to continuously learning algorithms.
635	A final Predetermined Change Control Plan (PCCP) guidance landed in December 2024.
636	And in January 2026, FDA issued an updated final Clinical Decision Support guidance that further refined which AI tools escape device regulation entirely.
637	The term Software as a Medical Device (SaMD) was formally defined by the International Medical Device Regulators Forum (IMDRF) in 2013 as 'software intended to be used for one or more medical purposes that perform these purposes without being part of a hardware medical device.' This definition was deliberately broad.
638	The critical threshold is whether software meets the statutory definition of a device under Section 201(h) of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic (FD&C) Act—intended for use in the diagnosis, cure, mitigation, treatment, or prevention of disease, or intended to affect the structure or function of the body.
639	The 21st Century Cures Act, signed into law in December 2016, refined the boundary further.
640	Section 520(o)(1)(E) of the FD&C Act creates the Non-Device CDS exclusion, which applies when four specific criteria are all met simultaneously.
641	The FDA's subsequent guidance documents have spent nearly a decade clarifying how to apply those criteria in practice, with the most recent final guidance issued in January 2026.

669	The most consequential boundary in healthcare AI regulation is not between different clearance pathways—it is the line between 'regulated medical device' and 'not a device at all.' The 21st Century Cures Act created that line for clinical decision support software through Section 520(o)(1)(E) of the FD&C Act.
670	After an initial draft guidance in 2017, a final guidance in 2022, and years of industry feedback, FDA issued a revised final Clinical Decision Support Software Guidance on January 6, 2026 (reissued January 29, 2026), followed by a town hall on March 11, 2026.
671	This 2026 guidance is the current controlling document for the Non-Device CDS determination.
672	The 2026 guidance introduced one significant policy shift from the 2022 version.
673	Under the prior guidance, FDA had taken a position that CDS tools producing only a single output (rather than multiple ranked recommendations) failed Criterion 3 because they were not truly 'supporting' independent clinical judgment.
674	In the 2026 guidance, FDA announced it will exercise enforcement discretion for single-output CDS functions that meet all other non-device criteria, acknowledging that in some clinical contexts only one recommendation is clinically appropriate and withholding it creates a worse outcome than surfacing it clearly.
675	The most common failure point is Criterion 4: if the AI uses a black-box neural network whose outputs cannot be independently reviewed by the clinician, the tool does not qualify as Non-Device CDS and is a regulated medical device—regardless of how it is marketed.
676	Provides a singular recommendation that is clinically unambiguous (2026 update)

678	The FDA's January 2025 draft guidance on 'Artificial Intelligence-Enabled Device Software Functions: Lifecycle Management and Marketing Submission Recommendations' (building on the December 2024 PCCP final guidance) specifically recommends that AI device submissions include a post-deployment monitoring plan describing how real-world performance will be tracked, how dataset drift will be detected, how demographic subgroup performance will be monitored, and at what thresholds a performance degradation will trigger corrective action.
679	This traceability requirement reflects FDA's recognition that an AI device that performed well in a 2022 validation study may underperform when deployed in a new patient population or when upstream data pipelines change.

725	Cardiology has been shaped by two parallel tracks of FDA-cleared AI: consumer-facing wearable ECG devices and institutional AI algorithms that augment standard 12-lead electrocardiography.

728	Atrial fibrillation affects an estimated 2.7 to 6.1 million Americans and is a leading cause of preventable ischemic stroke — yet paroxysmal AFib is notoriously difficult to capture on a clinic-based 12-lead ECG, which is only a ten-second snapshot.
729	The institutional AI track is exemplified by a landmark 2019 study from the Mayo Clinic published in Nature Medicine, which trained a convolutional neural network on paired 12-lead ECG and echocardiogram data from 44,959 patients to detect left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of 35 percent or lower — the threshold for heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF).

787	It is easy to compute and intuitively appealing: if 20 percent of White patients are classified as high-risk, then 20 percent of Black patients should be too.
788	Equalized odds, formalized by Hardt, Price, and Srebro in 2016, requires that the model achieve equal true positive rates and equal false positive rates across groups.
789	This is often the most clinically meaningful fairness criterion because it directly concerns the accuracy of the model for each group, not just the volume of predictions.
790	Predictive parity (calibration within groups) requires that when the model assigns a risk score of, say, 0.7, approximately 70 percent of patients with that score actually experience the predicted outcome — and this calibration should hold equally well for each demographic subgroup.
791	Here is where the mathematics becomes uncomfortable for practitioners who want a single perfect metric: Chouldechova proved in 2017 — in a paper titled "Fair Prediction with Disparate Impact" published in the journal Big Data — that when protected groups have unequal base rates of the outcome, it is mathematically impossible for any binary classifier to simultaneously achieve equal false positive rates, equal false negative rates, and predictive parity across those groups, except in the degenerate case of a perfect predictor.
792	The Fairness Impossibility Theorem in Chouldechova 2017: when base rates differ across groups, demographic parity, equalized odds, and predictive parity cannot all be satisfied by the same model at the same time.
793	Within-group calibration is often the most actionable metric in healthcare: are scores equally accurate for each patient subgroup?
794	It involves disaggregating standard performance metrics — AUC, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, F1 score — by every available demographic dimension: race and ethnicity, sex and gender, age group, insurance type, language preference, and geographic region.
795	A model reporting 85 percent AUC overall can simultaneously achieve 91 percent AUC for White patients and 74 percent AUC for Black patients — and the aggregate number obscures the disparity entirely.
796	The standard "four-fifths rule" checks whether the rate of positive predictions (or favorable outcomes) for a protected group is at least 80 percent of the rate for the most-advantaged group.
797	If a readmission risk model flags 30 percent of White patients for care management but only 18 percent of Black patients — despite equivalent disease burden — that gap exceeds the threshold for disparate impact and warrants immediate investigation.
798	SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations), introduced by Lundberg and Lee in their seminal NeurIPS 2017 paper "A Unified Approach to Interpreting Model Predictions," provides a theoretically grounded method for attributing the contribution of each input feature to an individual prediction.
799	The intervention that is easiest to implement is rarely the most durable, and the intervention that addresses root cause is rarely the fastest.
800	Post-processing calibration is the most common intervention for organizations using vendor AI: it accepts the model's outputs as given and applies group-specific threshold adjustments to balance performance across subgroups.
801	For example, if a model has a 0.5 decision threshold and produces systematically lower scores for Black patients, lowering the threshold for that group to 0.42 can equalize sensitivity without retraining the underlying model.
802	Model replacement — retiring a biased model and substituting one built with fairness constraints from the outset — is the most thorough intervention but also the most disruptive.
803	The ONC HTI-1 rule implicitly acknowledges this by requiring ongoing transparency about algorithm performance rather than a one-time disclosure.

966	We begin with the four principles that have anchored biomedical ethics since 1979, applying each to AI-specific scenarios.
967	In 1979, philosopher Tom Beauchamp and theologian James Childress published the first edition of Principles of Biomedical Ethics, a work that would go on to define how medicine reasons about right and wrong.

970	The WHO's 2021 Ethics and Governance of Artificial Intelligence for Health guidance extends Beauchamp and Childress into the AI domain with six principles: protect autonomy; promote human well-being and the public interest; ensure transparency and explainability; foster responsibility and accountability; ensure inclusiveness and equity; and promote AI that is responsive and sustainable.
971	Published after eighteen months of deliberation by twenty leading experts in ethics, digital technology, law, and human rights, the WHO document represents the first global consensus on what ethical AI in health actually requires.
972	The EU AI Act, which entered into force in August 2024 and whose obligations for high-risk AI embedded in regulated products such as medical devices apply from August 2027, classifies any AI component integrated into a medical device requiring Notified Body review as high-risk under Article 6(1)(b).
973	Separately, the EU's General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) Article 22 grants individuals the right not to be subject to decisions based solely on automated processing, and Article 13 requires meaningful information about the logic involved.

975	The Safe Harbor method requires the removal of 18 specific identifiers — including names, Social Security numbers, medical record numbers, detailed dates, and geographic subdivisions smaller than a state — after which the data is considered de-identified and is no longer subject to HIPAA's Privacy Rule.
976	Modern machine learning models can reconstruct personal identities from data patterns that contain none of the 18 Safe Harbor identifiers.
977	A 2019 study estimated that 99.98% of Americans could be correctly re-identified using just 15 demographic attributes.

980	The most widely studied case is the Optum health risk stratification algorithm, exposed in a landmark study published in Science in October 2019 by economist and physician Ziad Obermeyer and colleagues.
981	The algorithm was used by hospitals and insurers to identify high-risk patients who would benefit from care management programs — it touched care decisions for over 200 million people in the United States annually.
982	Because Black patients historically spent approximately $1,800 less per year on healthcare than white patients with the same conditions — a product of systemic barriers to access, not better health — the algorithm concluded they were healthier.
983	A 2021 study examining 27,697 patients across 38,455 hospitalisations found the model's area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) was only 0.63 — substantially below the performance Epic reported.
984	The model missed 67% of sepsis cases.
985	Beyond overall poor performance, researchers identified disparate performance across demographic subgroups, with statistically significant differences for Asian and Hispanic patients, partly because the original training data did not use the Sepsis-3 definition and because racial and socioeconomic differences in care access created systematic variation in the training signal.
