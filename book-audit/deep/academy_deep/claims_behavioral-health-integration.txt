## collaborative-care-model
135	The Collaborative Care Model (CoCM) is the most rigorously studied approach to integrating behavioral health into primary care.
136	Jürgen Unützer at the University of Washington in the 1990s and is now maintained and disseminated by the university’s AIMS Center (Advancing Integrated Mental Health Solutions), founded in 2004.
137	The principles are: (1) patient-centered team care, in which the PCP and behavioral health team collaborate; (2) population-based care, using a registry so no patient "falls through the cracks"; (3) measurement-based treatment to target, using validated tools like the PHQ-9 for depression and GAD-7 for anxiety; (4) evidence-based care, applying treatments proven to work; and (5) accountable care, with reimbursement and quality tied to outcomes rather than volume.
138	The team scores symptoms with a validated instrument at every contact and actively changes the treatment — adjusting medication or therapy — until the patient reaches a defined goal, such as a substantial drop in PHQ-9 score.
139	Measurement-based treatment to target is the heart of CoCM: validated tools like the PHQ-9 and GAD-7 are used to track symptoms and adjust treatment until the patient actually reaches their goal.
140	It began with the landmark IMPACT trial (Improving Mood — Promoting Access to Collaborative Treatment), published in 2002.
141	IMPACT followed 1,801 older adults with depression across 18 primary care clinics affiliated with eight health care organizations in five states (Washington, California, Texas, Indiana, and North Carolina).
142	Since IMPACT, the model has been tested in more than 90 randomized controlled trials in the United States and abroad, consistently showing better clinical outcomes, greater patient and provider satisfaction, improved functioning, and lower total health care costs.
143	Jürgen Unützer at the University of Washington, IMPACT enrolled 1,801 depressed older adults across 18 diverse primary care clinics in five states and randomized them to collaborative care or usual care.
144	Older adults with depression (2002)
145	CPT codes 99492 and 99493 reimburse the first and subsequent months of CoCM (based on care-manager time and required activities like registry tracking and caseload review), with 99494 and HCPCS code G2214 for additional increments.
146	But with both a deep evidence base and a payment pathway, CoCM has become the leading template for integrating behavioral health into primary care at scale.
147	The Collaborative Care Model treats common behavioral health conditions inside primary care using a defined team and a systematic, registry-driven process — it is the most rigorously studied integration model.
148	CoCM is defined by five principles, with measurement-based treatment to target (using tools like the PHQ-9 and GAD-7) as its central engine.
149	The evidence is exceptional: the 2002 IMPACT trial (1,801 patients) more than doubled depression treatment effectiveness, and over 90 randomized trials have followed.
150	Medicare created specific monthly billing codes (99492, 99493, 99494, G2214) that pay for CoCM as it actually works, removing a major barrier to adoption.

## asam-criteria-levels-of-care
169	The ASAM Criteria, published by the American Society of Addiction Medicine, are the most widely used set of standards for assessing people with substance use disorders and matching them to the right intensity of treatment.
170	The criteria are revised periodically; the Fourth Edition was released in 2023.

173	Two people with the same diagnosis can need very different care: one with severe withdrawal risk and an unstable living environment may need a 24-hour setting, while another who is medically stable, motivated, and supported at home can succeed in outpatient care.

180	The ASAM Criteria describe broad levels of care running from Level 0.5 to Level 4, with higher numbers indicating more intensive services and more clinical supervision.
181	Level 0.5 is early intervention — for people with risky use who do not yet meet full criteria for a disorder.
182	Level 1 is outpatient treatment, generally fewer than nine hours of service per week for adults, delivered while the person lives at home.
183	Level 2 is intensive outpatient or partial hospitalization — more hours per week for those needing structure but not residential care.
184	Level 3 is residential treatment, provided in a structured setting staffed 24 hours a day, with sublevels ranging from clinically managed low-intensity to higher-intensity residential care.
185	Level 4 is medically managed intensive inpatient care, with daily physician oversight and 24-hour nursing — the most intensive setting, used for example when withdrawal is medically dangerous.

## vermont-opioid-response
286	Vermont’s Hub-and-Spoke model is one of the most influential state responses to the opioid crisis in the United States.
287	Vermont divided the state into five geographic regions and began a staggered rollout of the system in January 2013.
288	Vermont’s Hub-and-Spoke model treats opioid use disorder as a chronic condition and builds a statewide network linking specialty addiction "hubs" with primary-care "spokes." It launched across five regions in January 2013 under the Blueprint for Health.
289	The "hubs" are regional specialty centers built around Opioid Treatment Programs (OTPs) — the SAMHSA-certified clinics that, as covered earlier, are the only settings legally able to dispense methadone for opioid use disorder.
290	First, they treat the patients who need the most intensive, structured care — including those starting treatment, those with complex needs, or those who are not stable enough for a primary care setting.
291	Second, they act as the regional center of addiction expertise: hub staff assess patients at intake and determine the most appropriate placement, including referral out to a primary-care spoke when a patient is stable.
292	Each hub is organized around an existing Opioid Treatment Program with the authority to dispense both methadone and buprenorphine, providing daily dosing and intensive services for patients with the most complex needs.
293	Each spoke is backed by a "MAT team" — staffing of roughly one full-time registered nurse and one master’s-level licensed behavioral health or addictions provider for every 100 Medicaid office-based patients.
294	Crucially, this MAT team support is provided to the practice at no direct cost, funded through a Medicaid Health Home arrangement (a 90/10 federal–state funding split enabled by the Affordable Care Act).
295	The spoke’s MAT team — nurse plus behavioral health provider per ~100 patients — provides care management; patients can move back to the hub if their needs change.
296	By around 2017, Vermont had the highest capacity for treating opioid use disorder in the country — on the order of 10.56 people in treatment per 1,000 population.

## crisis-continuum-988
311	The Behavioral Health Crisis Continuum and 988
312	The first pillar, someone to contact, is a crisis line: a number a person can call, text, or chat any hour of the day to reach trained crisis counselors — the role 988 now fills nationally.
313	A 988 counselor can dispatch a mobile crisis team; a mobile team can bring someone to a crisis stabilization facility; and stabilization can connect the person to ongoing treatment — all without defaulting to police or the ED.
314	SAMHSA updated these national guidelines in 2025, reflecting new research and emerging best practices for a coordinated crisis system.

316	988: The National Crisis Line
317	The centerpiece of the contact pillar is 988, the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline.
318	In July 2022, the United States transitioned the older ten-digit National Suicide Prevention Lifeline to the simple, three-digit 988 — an easy-to-remember number analogous to 911, but for behavioral health crises.
319	It provides free, confidential, 24/7 support for people in suicidal, mental health, or substance-use crises, by call, text, or chat.
320	988 is not a single call center but a national network of more than 200 local and state crisis centers, supported by SAMHSA.
321	The transition to 988 was explicitly framed by SAMHSA as a first step toward a fully transformed crisis care system — the contact pillar that the response and stabilization pillars are meant to build around.
322	Since its July 2022 launch, 988 received roughly 16.5 million contacts — about 11.1 million calls, 2.9 million texts, and 2.4 million chats — with monthly volume rising over time and text and chat growing especially fast.
323	Importantly, the vast majority of contacts are resolved on the line: more than 98% of calls are handled without dispatching emergency services, underscoring that most crises can be defused through skilled conversation.
324	Transition from the 10-digit Lifeline
325	The national answer rate for 988 calls, texts, and chats rose substantially after launch — to around 91% — while wait times fell, meaning more people in crisis are actually reaching a counselor quickly.
326	Most strikingly, research has begun to link 988 to reduced suicide.
327	A study published in JAMA found that after the launch of 988, suicide deaths among adolescents and young adults dropped about 11% compared with projected rates, and that the states with the largest increases in answered calls saw the largest declines in suicide rates.
328	The contact pillar is necessary but not sufficient on its own — its full promise depends on the response and stabilization pillars being built out so that 988 can connect callers to in-person help when a phone call is not enough.
