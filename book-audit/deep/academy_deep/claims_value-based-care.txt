
## fee-for-service-origins

## relative-value-units
51	Before 1992, Medicare paid physicians based on "customary, prevailing, and reasonable" (CPR) charges — essentially whatever a doctor had historically billed, benchmarked against regional and specialty norms.
52	In the late 1980s, Harvard economist William Hsiao led a landmark study commissioned by the Health Care Financing Administration (now CMS) to develop a more rational basis for physician payment.
53	The Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1989 authorized the creation of the Medicare Fee Schedule based on RBRVS, which took effect on January 1, 1992.

## mips-merit-based-incentive
93	For nearly two decades, Medicare physician payment operated under the shadow of the Sustainable Growth Rate (SGR) formula — a mechanism Congress invented in 1997 to prevent physician spending from exceeding GDP growth.
94	By 2015, the accumulated deficit had grown to the point where the pending automatic cut was 21.2% — an amount no one considered politically or practically possible to implement.
95	On April 16, 2015, President Obama signed the Medicare Access and CHIP Reauthorization Act (MACRA) into law, with rare bipartisan support.
96	MIPS launched January 1, 2017, as the default track for most Medicare-participating clinicians.
97	For the first time, physician payment adjustments under Medicare would be explicitly tied to measured performance — not just volume.
98	Key Concept: MIPS (Merit-Based Incentive Payment System) is the primary track of the CMS Quality Payment Program, established by MACRA in 2015.
99	It scores eligible clinicians on four performance categories — Quality, Cost, Promoting Interoperability, and Improvement Activities — and adjusts Medicare Part B payment rates up or down by up to ±9% based on their composite score.
100	CMS estimated approximately 572,000 eligible clinicians required to participate in the 2024 MIPS performance period — CMS QPP Final Rule 2024
101	MIPS-Eligible Clinicians (2024)
102	The maximum MIPS payment adjustment for 2026 (based on 2024 performance) is +/-9% of Medicare Part B allowed charges — CMS MIPS Final Rule
103	2026 Payment Adjustment Range
104	MIPS organizes performance measurement into four weighted categories, each contributing a share of the 100-point composite score used to determine a clinician's payment adjustment.
105	For 2024, the category weights are: Quality (30%), Cost (30%), Promoting Interoperability (25%), and Improvement Activities (15%).
106	These weights represent a significant evolution from the program's early years, when Quality carried 60% of the score and Cost was minimal.
107	The Promoting Interoperability (PI) category, worth 25% of the composite score, evaluates the use of certified electronic health record technology, with measures focused on patient access to records, electronic prescribing, health information exchange, and public health reporting.
108	The Improvement Activities category, at 15%, allows clinicians to earn points by attesting to participation in one of approximately 95 defined activities — such as care coordination, patient engagement, clinical practice improvement, or population health management — each weighted as medium or high impact.
109	Report at least 6 quality measures (including 1 outcome measure) via claims, registry, EHR, or QCDR submission over a 12-month performance period.
110	Clinicians attest to completing one or more improvement activities worth a total of at least 40 points (medium activities = 10 pts, high = 20 pts).
111	Clinicians report on EHR-based PI measures through their certified EHR technology for a minimum 180-day period.
112	CMS weights the four category scores (30/30/25/15) and sums them into a 0–100 MIPS Final Score for the performance year.
113	The composite score is compared to the performance threshold (75 points in 2024) and a payment adjustment of up to +/-9% is applied to Medicare Part B payments two years later.
114	Performance Thresholds, Payment Adjustments, and the 2026 Outlook
115	The MIPS payment adjustment applies to Medicare Part B payments in the year two years after the performance year — so 2024 performance determines 2026 payment rates.
116	For 2024, the performance threshold is 75 points on the 100-point composite scale.
117	Clinicians who score at or above 75 points receive a neutral or positive payment adjustment; those who score below 75 receive a negative adjustment; those below 18.75 points (the exceptional negative threshold) receive the maximum penalty of -9%.
118	The statutory maximum positive adjustment is also ±9%, but the actual positive adjustment is constrained by the program's budget-neutrality requirement: the total positive adjustments paid must be funded by the total negative adjustments collected.
119	For 2026 payments (based on 2024 performance), CMS estimated the maximum positive adjustment at only approximately +1.05% — the lowest in program history — despite the statutory maximum of +9%.
120	The threat of the -9% penalty has been more behaviorally significant than the promise of a +1.05% bonus — a pattern that raises questions about whether MIPS is actually changing clinical behavior or primarily ensuring compliance.

130	CMS defines eligibility through a low-volume threshold: clinicians must exceed all three of the following criteria to be required to participate — billing more than $90,000 for Medicare Part B-covered services, seeing more than 200 Medicare patients, and providing more than 200 covered professional services to Part B patients in a year.
131	Through 2022, this incentive included a 5% lump-sum bonus on Medicare Part B payments.
132	That bonus expired at the end of 2022, creating a significant policy debate about whether the financial case for joining an Advanced APM had been sufficiently weakened to slow the transition to value-based care.
133	A physician who earns the maximum +9% MIPS bonus still receives only a 9% increase on their Medicare-specific claims, which may represent a fraction of their overall practice revenue.
134	According to American Medical Association data, 49% of solo physicians received MIPS payment penalties in recent performance years, compared to 29% of small practices and 18% of rural practices.
135	In surveys of physician practices, the AMA found that MIPS-related administrative work can consume 15 or more hours per physician per week when preparing for reporting periods, diverting clinical staff to documentation and data-entry tasks rather than patient care.
136	For 2026, the Advanced APM incentive is now subject to policy review following the expiration of the 5% bonus at end of 2022.
137	MIPS is like a performance review at work — your score affects your raise by a few percent, but your underlying job and pay structure remain the same.
138	MIPS was created by MACRA (2015) to replace the failed SGR formula and consolidate multiple quality reporting programs into a single system, launching January 1, 2017.

## aco-basics
195	The legal foundation for Medicare ACOs is Section 3022 of the Affordable Care Act (ACA), which amended Title XVIII of the Social Security Act by adding Section 1899 to establish the Medicare Shared Savings Program (MSSP).
196	The MSSP regulations were finalized in November 2011 and became effective January 3, 2012, with the first ACO agreements starting as early as April 1, 2012.
197	In performance year 2024, 476 ACOs participated in the Medicare Shared Savings Program, and the highest share of ACOs since the program began earned performance payments.
198	Source: CMS PY2024 Fact Sheet.
199	More than 10 million Medicare fee-for-service beneficiaries were assigned to an MSSP ACO in 2024.
200	Medicare saved $2.5 billion relative to ACO benchmarks in performance year 2024, the highest since the program began.
201	Net Savings to Medicare 2024
202	75% of participating ACOs earned performance payments totaling $4.1 billion in 2024.
203	Specifically, CMS examines claims data to identify which ACO-affiliated clinician provided the most primary care evaluation and management visits to a given patient, and that ACO receives credit (and accountability) for that patient.
204	For new agreement periods beginning January 1, 2024, CMS introduced the Accountable Care Prospective Trend (ACPT)—a fixed projected national spending growth rate—as a third component of the benchmark update factor, creating what is now known as a three-way blended update.

## vbc-fundamentals-module-3-economics-pillar
327	Value-Based Care Fundamentals — Module 3: The Economics Pillar — Following the Money in Value-Based Care
328	Here is a question that sounds simple but is genuinely hard to answer: if a hospital successfully reduces its patients’ hospitalization rate by 15% through better care management, is that good news or bad news for the hospital’s finances?
329	But in a world where 40% of that hospital’s revenue still comes from fee-for-service Medicare and Medicaid payments, fewer hospitalizations means fewer inpatient admissions, which means fewer billed days, which means lower revenue.
330	The Medicare Shared Savings Program is the most widely adopted VBC model in the United States, and shared savings is the financial mechanism at its core.
331	This projected spend is your “benchmark.” If your ACO’s actual spending on that population comes in below the benchmark, the difference is “savings.” You share those savings with CMS according to a pre-agreed formula — typically retaining 40–75% depending on your risk track and quality performance, with CMS keeping the remainder.
332	CMS has revised the benchmark methodology repeatedly in response to this problem — adding regional adjustments for agreement periods beginning in 2017, reworking them in the 2018 Pathways to Success rule, and, in the CY2023 Physician Fee Schedule rule, adding a prior-savings adjustment and an administratively set growth factor (the Accountable Care Prospective Trend) to blunt the ratchet effect — but the fundamental tension between historical benchmarking and equitable VBC participation for already-efficient providers remains unresolved.
333	One of the most consequential decisions a provider organization makes when entering a shared savings arrangement is whether to accept only upside risk — where you share in savings if you beat the benchmark but owe nothing if you miss it — or two-sided risk, where you both share savings and repay losses if spending exceeds the benchmark.
334	Upside-only arrangements (MSSP Track 1 and its successors) are appropriate for organizations beginning their VBC journey: they provide financial experience with population cost management without the catastrophic downside scenario of a major loss repayment obligation.
335	Two-sided arrangements (the MSSP ENHANCED Track, and CMMI’s ACO REACH Model — the 2023 successor to Global and Professional Direct Contracting, which runs through December 31, 2026 and is to be succeeded by the Long-term Enhanced ACO Design (LEAD) Model from January 1, 2027) offer higher shared savings percentages — sometimes up to 80% of savings — but require the organization to maintain actuarial reserves sufficient to cover a loss scenario, typically modeled at 1–3% of benchmark spending.

361	Shared Savings Contract Economics: MSSP Track Comparison for a Hypothetical 5,000-Beneficiary ACO
362	For a health system with $30 million in unrestricted net assets, maintaining a $2.5 million actuarial reserve for ACO REACH participation represents a 8.3% allocation of available capital to a risk management buffer — a rational investment if leadership has high confidence in the organization’s ability to manage population cost, but a significant commitment that requires board-level approval and careful opportunity cost analysis against other capital needs.
363	Rather than holding a provider accountable for the total cost of care across an entire attributed population, bundled payments establish a single target price for all the care associated with a specific clinical episode — typically defined as a procedure or hospitalization plus 90 days of post-acute care.
364	BPCI Advanced, CMS’s voluntary bundled payment model, ran from October 1, 2018 through December 31, 2025, with 29 inpatient and 3 outpatient clinical episode categories at launch, ranging from major joint replacement and cardiac procedures to pneumonia and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease hospitalizations.
365	Its final evaluation (The Lewin Group for CMS, August 2026) found that participants reduced Medicare episode payments in every model year — by $743 per episode on average in Model Years 1 and 2 and by about $1,000 per episode in Model Years 3 through 6 — and that the model produced about $800 million in net Medicare savings across its eight model years: net losses in Model Years 1–3, when reconciliation payments to participants exceeded the spending reductions, were outweighed by net savings once CMS redesigned target pricing in Model Year 4 (2021).
366	Readmission rates were similar to the comparison group, although patients in Model Years 7–8 reported less favorable changes in functional status.
367	Critically, the savings came primarily from post-acute care — fewer patients discharged to skilled nursing or inpatient rehabilitation facilities, skilled nursing stays about three days shorter, and skilled nursing payments about $500 lower per episode — rather than from reductions in the procedural care itself, confirming the bundled payment hypothesis that the primary driver of unnecessary cost in high-volume episodes is the post-acute care tail, not the procedure.
368	CMS concluded that BPCI Advanced did not meet its criteria for expansion, and carried its lessons into the mandatory Transforming Episode Accountability Model (TEAM), which began January 1, 2026.
369	Capitation: The Most Advanced — and Most Misunderstood — VBC Model
370	Capitation is the payment model that most completely aligns provider incentives with patient health outcomes, and the one that generates the most anxiety among providers who have not operated under it before.
371	This concern is real and has historical basis: the managed care capitation models of the 1990s were frequently criticized for exactly this failure, and some HMO capitation arrangements did produce documented under-treatment in specific service categories.
372	But the lesson of the 1990s HMO experience is not that capitation is inherently dangerous.
373	The capitation model that exists in 2026 is substantively different from the managed care capitation of 1996.
374	The single most common financial mistake health systems make in VBC transitions is underestimating the revenue disruption of the transition period — the window, typically 18 to 36 months, during which care management investments have begun reducing utilization but VBC contract revenue has not yet scaled to replace the lost fee-for-service volume.
375	Consider a regional health system generating $400 million in annual net patient revenue, with 30% of that revenue from inpatient admissions billed on a fee-for-service basis.
376	The system joins MSSP and invests $2 million in care management infrastructure — care coordinators, a risk stratification platform, a post-discharge follow-up program — that begins generating measurable results in year one, reducing its attributed Medicare population’s hospitalization rate by 12%.
377	That 12% reduction represents approximately 240 avoided admissions.
378	At an average net revenue of $8,500 per admission, it also represents a $2.04 million reduction in fee-for-service inpatient revenue.
379	The system’s MSSP shared savings payment for year one, based on total population savings, might be $1.4 million.
380	Net financial impact of year one VBC investment: negative $640,000.
381	CMS’s own results show what is achievable at scale: in Performance Year 2024, Shared Savings Program ACOs generated gross savings of $651 per assigned beneficiary (shared between ACOs and Medicare) and net Medicare savings of $245, and 75% of the 476 participating ACOs earned performance payments.
382	Present this model honestly to your board, or the VBC investment will be defunded the first time a quarterly loss appears.
383	One of the most powerful and counterintuitive findings in health economics is that investment in primary care produces returns that are comparable to, and in many cases superior to, investment in specialty care infrastructure — not in terms of individual clinical case intensity, but in terms of total population health spending and outcomes.
384	The evidence is strongest on outcomes and quality: Baicker and Chandra (Health Affairs, 2004) found that states with more general practitioners per capita delivered higher-quality care to Medicare beneficiaries at lower spending, while states with more specialists spent more for lower quality; and Basu and colleagues (JAMA Internal Medicine, 2019) found that every 10 additional primary care physicians per 100,000 people was associated with a 51.5-day increase in life expectancy.
385	Supply alone is not a cost-control lever, however — a 2009 Health Affairs analysis found that simply having more primary care physicians did not slow spending growth — which is why payment models that reward primary care for total cost matter.

## vbc-quality-measures-hedis-ecqm
523	The Healthcare Effectiveness Data and Information Set (HEDIS) is maintained by the National Committee for Quality Assurance (NCQA) and represents the most widely used quality measurement framework in American healthcare.
524	HEDIS comprises approximately 90 measures across 6 domains: effectiveness of care, access and availability of care, patient experience, utilization, health plan descriptive information, and measures reported using Electronic Clinical Data Systems (ECDS).
525	Administrative data has 100% population coverage and low collection cost but is subject to coding completeness variations and cannot capture clinical nuances not reflected in billing codes.
526	NCQA has been progressively expanding electronic HEDIS (eHEDIS) — versions of HEDIS measures calculated directly from structured EHR data rather than claims or medical record abstraction. eHEDIS has the potential to reduce the administrative burden of HEDIS collection while improving data accuracy, since clinical values like HbA1c results and blood pressure readings can be captured directly rather than inferred from billing codes.
527	Electronic Clinical Quality Measures (eCQMs) are a parallel quality measure infrastructure developed by CMS and the Office of the National Coordinator for Health IT (ONC) specifically for use with certified EHR technology. eCQMs are specified in Health Quality Measure Format (HQMF), with measure logic written in Clinical Quality Language (CQL) since the 2019 reporting period — a machine-readable specification that allows EHR systems to calculate measure results automatically from structured clinical data.
528	The FHIR plumbing that digital measurement depends on is being laid by two 2020 rules: the CMS Interoperability and Patient Access final rule, which required CMS-regulated payers (Medicare Advantage, Medicaid, CHIP and federal-exchange plans) to offer FHIR-based Patient Access APIs, and the ONC Cures Act final rule, which required certified EHRs to support standardized FHIR APIs.
529	Since 2023, CMS's consensus-based entity (CBE) contract has been held by Battelle, which runs measure endorsement and pre-rulemaking review through the Partnership for Quality Measurement (PQM).
530	Process measures assess whether a specific evidence-based clinical action was taken — for example, whether a diabetic patient received an HbA1c test within the measurement year.
531	Outcome measures assess whether patients achieved a desired health state — for example, whether a diabetic patient achieved HbA1c control below 8%.
532	A 2016 Health Affairs study (Casalino et al.) estimated that US physician practices spend more than $15.4 billion a year, about 785 hours per physician, reporting quality measures to CMS and commercial payers.
533	Many of these measures are similar but not identical — measuring hemoglobin A1c with slightly different age cutoffs or different denominator specifications across programs — requiring separate data collection and reporting workflows for technically similar clinical concepts.
534	Progress on all three fronts has been substantial since the 21st Century Cures Act's interoperability provisions took effect, but the transition from a claims-based to a digital quality measurement ecosystem will take years and significant investment to complete.
535	One of the most significant evolution in quality measurement over the past five years has been the growing imperative to stratify quality data by race, ethnicity, language, income, and disability status.
536	Aggregate quality scores can conceal dramatic disparities: a health plan that achieves the 75th percentile on overall HbA1c control may have excellent performance for white, commercially insured members and significantly lower performance for Black, Hispanic, or Medicaid-enrolled members.
537	NCQA introduced the Health Equity Accreditation program in 2021 and has been adding race and ethnicity stratification to HEDIS measures.
538	CMS finalized a Health Equity Index reward for the Medicare Advantage Star Ratings (later renamed Excellent Health Outcomes for All), then cancelled it in the CY2027 final rule of April 2026 and kept the historical reward factor, a reminder that equity incentives in federal programs remain politically contingent.
539	The Enhancing Oncology Model launched in 2023 requiring participants to screen for health-related social needs, collect sociodemographic data and submit health equity plans; a 2025 amendment dropped the equity plan requirement.
540	The transition to digital quality measures represents the most promising path forward — enabling richer, more frequent, more equitable quality intelligence at lower operational cost — but requires sustained investment in health information infrastructure and regulatory alignment that the market alone will not produce.

## vbc-patient-reported-outcomes
541	They are the most patient-centered form of quality evidence available, and their use in value-based care is expanding rapidly.
542	First, they measure outcomes that patients actually care about — the FDA increasingly asks oncology drug sponsors to collect core PRO data (symptoms, symptomatic side effects and physical function) alongside tumor response, as set out in its June 2021 draft guidance on core patient-reported outcomes in cancer clinical trials.
543	PROMIS, developed by the NIH beginning in 2004 and now maintained by Northwestern University's Health Measures organization, is the most comprehensive and methodologically rigorous PRO instrument system available in American healthcare.
544	Third, it implements Computerized Adaptive Testing (CAT), where an algorithm selects the most informative questions from the item bank based on the patient's answers to previous questions, achieving precise measurement with typically 4 to 12 questions per domain, fewer than traditional fixed-length instruments require.
545	PROMIS is normed to the US general population with a mean T-score of 50 and standard deviation of 10, meaning a patient with a PROMIS Physical Function score of 40 is approximately one standard deviation below the population mean.
546	This general-population norming allows direct comparison across conditions: a patient with knee osteoarthritis who scores 35 on physical function can be compared to a patient with heart failure or multiple sclerosis on the same scale, enabling cross-condition burden measurement that condition-specific instruments cannot provide.
547	The most commonly used domains in clinical practice include: Physical Function (assessment of physical activities of daily living and higher-level physical activity); Pain Interference (the degree to which pain disrupts daily activities and participation); Fatigue (the subjective experience of tiredness, weakness, and lack of energy); Depression (mood, affect, and somatic symptoms associated with depressive disorders); Anxiety (subjective fear, apprehension, and tension); and Social Function (ability to participate in social activities and roles).
548	Global Health short forms — a 10-item instrument covering physical and mental health global assessments — provide a rapid screening tool that is widely used in population health management contexts.
549	Their short forms, the HOOS JR and KOOS JR, are the instruments behind CMS's hip and knee replacement PRO measures: voluntary PRO data submission in the Comprehensive Care for Joint Replacement (CJR) model since 2016, and the Hospital IQR Program's THA/TKA patient-reported outcome performance measure.
550	The FDA's June 2021 draft guidance on core patient-reported outcomes in cancer clinical trials set out which PRO domains sponsors should collect, signaling that PROs are becoming a permanent component of oncology evidence.
551	The CJR bundled payment model rewarded hospitals for voluntarily submitting HOOS JR and KOOS JR data for hip and knee replacement beginning in 2016, and the Hospital IQR Program has since adopted a THA/TKA PRO performance measure built on the same instruments.
552	The Enhancing Oncology Model (2023) requires participating practices to implement electronic PRO collection for symptom monitoring.
553	The most clinically impactful application of digital PRO collection is real-time symptom monitoring in oncology.
554	A randomized trial by Ethan Basch and colleagues, published in the Journal of Clinical Oncology (2016) with survival results in JAMA (2017), demonstrated that weekly web-based symptom monitoring — where patients report symptoms between clinic visits and clinicians receive alerts for concerning symptom escalation — reduced emergency department visits, improved quality of life, and extended overall survival in cancer patients receiving systemic treatment.
555	Patient burden is the most commonly cited: completing multiple PRO instruments at each visit, particularly for patients with multiple chronic conditions who may be asked to complete instruments by multiple providers, creates fatigue and declining response rates over time.
556	Patient-reported outcomes represent the most direct measurement of whether healthcare is achieving what patients come to clinicians for: less pain, better function, improved quality of life, and the ability to live according to their values.

## medicare-star-ratings
631	The Star Ratings system, formally established under the Medicare Modernization Act and expanded significantly by the ACA, provides that signal on a scale of 1 to 5 stars.
632	Second, it is a financial incentive mechanism: plans with 4 or more stars receive quality bonus payments that can total hundreds of millions of dollars annually for large insurers.
633	Each annual cycle of measure updates and cut-point changes produces winners and losers among the nation's more than 700 Medicare Advantage contracts — and the financial consequences can run into the hundreds of millions of dollars per percentage point of star rating change.
634	Medicare Advantage Star Ratings simultaneously function as a consumer choice signal published on Medicare Plan Finder and as a financial incentive mechanism that awards billions of dollars in quality bonus payments to plans achieving 4 or more stars.
635	The overall star rating for a Medicare Advantage Prescription Drug (MA-PD) plan is computed from performance across nine domains, with a maximum of 42 individual measures (40 unique measures, with 2 duplicated across Part C and Part D calculations).
636	Cut points — the thresholds separating 1-star from 2-star, 2-star from 3-star, and so on — are recalculated each year based on the distribution of plan performance during the measurement period.
637	This means that achieving a 4-star rating requires hitting a different absolute performance threshold each year, depending on how the industry as a whole performed.
638	In a year when most plans improved, cut points shift upward, and a plan that maintained the same absolute score might drop from 4 to 3 stars.
639	Plans that achieve at least 4 stars receive a quality bonus payment (QBP) that increases their county-level benchmark rate by 5 percentage points.
640	For a large Medicare Advantage plan enrolling hundreds of thousands of beneficiaries, a 5-percentage-point benchmark increase can mean hundreds of millions of dollars in additional annual revenue — the difference between profitability and loss in many markets.
641	Plans with 3 stars or below retain 50 percent of the rebate to fund additional benefits.
642	Plans with 3.5 to 4 stars retain 65 percent.
643	Plans with 4.5 or 5 stars retain 70 percent.
644	Higher-rated plans therefore have both higher benchmarks and higher rebate retention — a compounding financial advantage that makes the 4-star threshold the most consequential boundary in Medicare Advantage finance.

## vbc-fundamentals-module-2-policy-pillar
860	Value-Based Care Fundamentals — Module 2: The Policy Pillar — Laws, Rules, and the Regulatory Architecture That Makes (or Breaks) Value-Based Care
861	Healthcare is one of the most heavily regulated industries in the United States, and for good reason: the decisions made in healthcare settings involve life and death, profound information asymmetry between providers and patients, and the expenditure of trillions of dollars of public funds.
862	What you do need is a working knowledge of four things: the major federal legislation that authorized and funds VBC programs, the agency — CMS and its Innovation Center — that designs and operates most federal VBC models, the specific legal waivers that allow VBC arrangements to operate outside standard fee-for-service rules, and the state-level Medicaid policy environment that governs VBC adoption for the Medicaid population, which accounts for about 18% of all U.S. healthcare spending and the majority of payer mix for rural and safety-net hospitals.
863	The Center for Medicare and Medicaid Innovation, universally known as CMMI, was established by Section 3021 of the Affordable Care Act in 2010 with a mandatory appropriation of $10 billion over ten years and a statutory mission to test, evaluate, and scale new payment and delivery models that reduce spending while preserving or improving quality.
864	This streamlined authority was deliberately designed to allow rapid policy experimentation — the legislative drafters recognized that VBC model design requires iteration and learning, and that subjecting every model modification to a 12-month rulemaking cycle would make meaningful innovation impossible.
865	Since 2011, CMMI has launched more than 50 distinct payment model pilots across Medicare and Medicaid, covering primary care transformation, specialty care bundling, accountable care organizations, direct contracting, and state-level all-payer models.
866	The budget allocated per model varies enormously — from small demonstration projects covering a few thousand beneficiaries to large-scale national programs like the Medicare Shared Savings Program, which enrolled 11.2 million beneficiaries by 2025.
867	CMMI's own 2021 strategy review found that only six of its more than 50 models had produced statistically significant savings, and in March 2025 it announced it would end four models early, effective December 31, 2025 — Primary Care First, Making Care Primary, ESRD Treatment Choices, and the Maryland Total Cost of Care Model — citing evaluations and forecasts of increased spending; it is a record that reflects honest evaluation but has also drawn criticism from providers who invested substantially in model infrastructure before termination decisions.
868	If CMMI is the engine of federal VBC development, the Medicare Access and CHIP Reauthorization Act of 2015 — universally known as MACRA — is the law that turned VBC from an optional experiment into the default trajectory for physician payment in Medicare.
869	MACRA was passed with rare bipartisan consensus, repealing the deeply unpopular Sustainable Growth Rate formula that had governed Medicare physician payments since 1997 and replacing it with a two-track system that explicitly links physician payment updates to VBC participation.
870	Physicians who remain in traditional fee-for-service Medicare participate in the Merit-based Incentive Payment System, or MIPS, which adjusts their annual Medicare payments up or down by up to 9% based on performance across four categories: quality (30% weight), cost (30%), promoting interoperability (25%), and improvement activities (15%) — the weights in effect since the 2022 performance year.
871	Physicians who generate a sufficient share of their Medicare revenue through APM participation become Qualifying APM Participants — originally earning a 5% lump-sum bonus (since reduced and, for 2025 performance onward, lapsed) and a higher fee-schedule update — and are exempt from MIPS reporting requirements — a substantial financial incentive to move toward more advanced VBC structures.
872	The same physician who joins an Advanced APM, such as a two-sided Medicare ACO, and receives at least 75% of their Medicare Part B payments (or sees 50% of their Medicare patients) through it qualifies as an Advanced APM participant, earns the higher QP fee-schedule update (the original 5% lump-sum bonus lapsed after the 2024 performance year), and escapes the MIPS reporting burden.

888	A 2021 study of independent practices by Khullar and colleagues (JAMA Health Forum) put the cost of MIPS participation at $12,811 per physician per year in 2019, driven by about 200 hours of physician and staff time.
889	Two of the most significant legal barriers to value-based care adoption are laws that were designed to protect patients from fraud: the Stark Law, which prohibits physician self-referral for designated health services when a financial relationship exists between the physician and the referred entity, and the Federal Anti-Kickback Statute, which prohibits offering or receiving anything of value to induce referrals of federal healthcare program business.
890	Both laws were enacted in the 1980s and 1990s to address genuine abuses in fee-for-service medicine — physicians referring patients to laboratories or imaging centers they owned, and pharmaceutical companies paying physicians to prescribe specific drugs.
891	In November 2020, CMS (for the Stark Law) and the HHS Office of Inspector General (for the Anti-Kickback Statute) simultaneously published new rules — commonly called the Stark Law and AKS VBC Exceptions — that created explicit regulatory safe harbors for value-based arrangements meeting specific criteria.
892	What the 2020 VBC Exceptions Actually Allow
893	The 2020 Stark Law and AKS VBC exceptions created three tiers of safe harbor protection based on the level of financial risk the arrangement assumes.
894	The first tier, the “care coordination arrangement” exception, covers low-risk VBC arrangements where physicians and hospitals share non-monetary resources — care coordinators, technology platforms, data analytics tools — without direct financial remuneration flowing between them.
895	Medicaid, the joint federal-state health insurance program for low-income Americans, covered about 76 million people together with CHIP at the end of 2025 and accounts for about 18% of all U.S. healthcare spending.
896	For rural and safety-net hospitals, Medicaid is often the dominant payer — in some Critical Access Hospitals, Medicaid accounts for more than 40% of patient volume.
897	Under the Social Security Act, the federal government sets minimum Medicaid standards and matches state spending at a regular rate (FMAP) that runs from a 50% floor to about 77% depending on state per-capita income, with a separate 90% match for the ACA expansion population — but states have substantial flexibility in how they design their Medicaid programs, including how they pay providers.
898	This flexibility has produced a 50-state patchwork of Medicaid VBC approaches that range from nationally leading to years behind, often with little correlation to state wealth or healthcare system capacity.
899	States access this flexibility primarily through two mechanisms: Section 1115 waivers, which allow states to use Medicaid funds in ways not otherwise permitted by federal statute in exchange for CMS approval of a formal waiver application and evaluation framework, and Section 1915 waivers, which allow states to require Medicaid beneficiaries to enroll in managed care organizations rather than traditional fee-for-service Medicaid.
900	As of 2025, 41 states (including DC) contract with comprehensive Medicaid managed care organizations, and many states use Section 1115 waivers to add VBC-oriented payment reform components.

910	Massachusetts’ MassHealth ACO Program, which by June 2023 enrolled more than 1.3 million members — over half of all MassHealth members — in its ACOs, required a decade of incremental managed care development, a sophisticated state health information exchange, and a robust community health worker infrastructure before global budgets were feasible.

912	The CMS AHEAD Model — announced in 2023 as the States Advancing All-Payer Health Equity Approaches and Development Model and renamed Achieving Healthcare Efficiency through Accountable Design after CMS redesigned it in September 2025 — is one of the most ambitious federal VBC policy experiments currently active in the United States.
913	In the participating states — Maryland, Connecticut, Hawaii, Rhode Island, and downstate New York (Vermont signed in January 2025 but withdrew in July 2026) — CMS is working with state governments and commercial insurers to establish coordinated spending targets that apply to Medicare, Medicaid, and commercial insurance simultaneously.
914	Hospital Global Budgets establish annual spending targets for inpatient and outpatient hospital care, similar to the all-payer hospital global budgets Maryland has operated statewide since 2014, built on the all-payer rate-setting system it has run under a Medicare waiver since 1977.
915	Primary Care Investment establishes a per-capita funding floor for primary care services in each participating state, addressing the underfunding of primary care that Module 1 identified as a structural driver of the U.S. spending-outcomes gap.
916	And population health accountability — originally a state Health Equity Plan with disparity-reduction goals — was replaced in CMS's September 2025 redesign by statewide and hospital Population Health Accountability Plans focused on preventive care and chronic disease prevention, while the requirements to report quality by race and ethnicity and to submit demographic data were dropped and states must now adopt at least two policies promoting choice and competition.
917	AHEAD’s results will not be statistically meaningful for several years — Cohorts 2 and 3 begin performance only in January 2028 — but its structural design is one of the most coherent attempts yet to address the whole-system payment reform that health economists have argued is necessary for genuine transformation.
918	One of the most practical skills a healthcare leader can develop is the ability to read federal policy signals early — to understand what CMS, Congress, and state Medicaid agencies are signaling about the future direction of payment policy before new rules take effect.

938	The third layer is analytics and intelligence: the population health management platforms, risk stratification tools, and increasingly AI-powered predictive models that transform raw clinical data into actionable care management intelligence — identifying which patients are at highest risk of imminent high-cost events, which chronic disease patients are overdue for interventions, and which populations are generating the most preventable utilization.
939	The United States made a massive federal investment in EHR adoption through the HITECH Act of 2009, which appropriated $27 billion in Medicare and Medicaid incentive payments to encourage providers to implement certified EHR systems and demonstrate “meaningful use” — a defined set of capabilities including electronic prescribing, clinical decision support, and patient portal access.
940	Certified EHR adoption among non-federal acute-care hospitals rose from roughly 10% in 2008 to about 96% by 2021 (Health Affairs; ONC), one of the most rapid technology adoption curves in any industry, and it created the clinical data foundation that value-based care analytics requires.
941	These include structured problem lists and medication lists that use standardized coding (ICD-10 for diagnoses, RxNorm for medications, SNOMED CT for clinical concepts) rather than free-text notes; care gap reporting that identifies patients overdue for preventive services or chronic disease monitoring; patient panel management tools that allow care coordinators to view their entire assigned patient population sorted by risk level; and FHIR API connectivity that allows the EHR to exchange data with external systems using the standardized data format that the 21st Century Cures Act mandated in 2020.
942	Federal data show the gap is real: in 2023, 70% of non-federal acute care hospitals engaged in all four domains of interoperable exchange (sending, receiving, finding, and integrating patient data), but only 43% did so routinely, and small, rural, critical access, and independent hospitals engaged less often than better-resourced hospitals (ONC Data Brief No. 71, 2024).
943	The 21st Century Cures Act: The Law That Changed EHR Interoperability Requirements
944	The 21st Century Cures Act, enacted in December 2016 with its implementing regulations finalized in 2020, made two changes to the EHR landscape that are directly relevant to VBC technology readiness.
945	First, it prohibited “information blocking” — defined as any practice that unreasonably restricts the access, exchange, or use of electronic health information — and established civil monetary penalties of up to $1 million per violation for health IT developers, networks, and exchanges that engage in information blocking.
946	Second, it mandated that all certified EHR systems implement standardized FHIR (Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources) application programming interfaces by 2022, enabling third-party developers to build population health applications that connect directly to EHR data without requiring custom integrations.
947	The 21st Century Cures Act’s information blocking prohibition changed the competitive dynamics of health IT in a way that CMS’s interoperability rules alone could not have.
948	When health IT developers and exchanges face penalties of up to $1 million per violation for restricting data access, and providers face their own federal disincentives, the legal calculus of data hoarding changes overnight.
949	The importance of HIE to VBC has been recognized in federal policy through TEFCA — the Trusted Exchange Framework and Common Agreement — which ONC published in January 2022 as the national framework for interoperable health information exchange.
950	TEFCA establishes a set of technical and legal standards that health information networks must meet to become Qualified Health Information Networks (QHINs), and creates a framework under which QHINs are required to share data with each other upon request, effectively creating a nationwide health information exchange network for the first time.
951	By early 2026, eleven networks had been designated as QHINs — eHealth Exchange, Epic Nexus, Health Gorilla, KONZA National Network, and MedAllies (December 2023); CommonWell Health Alliance and Kno2 (February 2024); Netsmart; eClinicalWorks; Surescripts; and Oracle Health Information Network (November 2025).
952	A rural Critical Access Hospital that lacks FHIR R4 API implementation, state HIE connectivity, and a population health platform is not technically capable of the patient data workflows that MSSP ACO participation requires — not because of organizational will or leadership quality, but because the digital infrastructure that enables those workflows has not been built.
953	The federal programs designed to address this gap — including the FCC’s Rural Health Care Program, capped at about $724 million for funding year 2025, ONC’s Health IT Certification Program, and HRSA’s Rural Health Information Technology funding stream — are necessary but insufficient at their current funding levels to close the rural-urban technology gap within a policy-relevant timeframe.
954	In a VBC model managing 10,000 attributed patients, a care management team cannot proactively reach out to every patient every month.
955	They need a system that identifies the 300 or 400 patients most likely to have a preventable hospital admission, emergency department visit, or serious clinical deterioration in the next 90 days, so that care coordination resources can be concentrated where they will have the greatest impact.
956	These models incorporate diagnosis trajectories (the sequence and pattern of diagnoses over time, not just their presence or absence), medication adherence signals derived from pharmacy data and EHR medication reconciliation records, laboratory value trends (a gradually rising HbA1c over three visits is a stronger predictor of near-term hospitalization than a single elevated value), social determinants of health flags derived from screening questionnaires and zip-code-level data, and behavioral health diagnoses and substance use disorder flags that are strongly predictive of high-cost medical utilization.
957	A landmark 2019 study published in Science by Obermeyer et al. found that a widely used commercial risk stratification algorithm systematically underestimated the health needs of Black patients because it used healthcare costs as a proxy for health need — and because Black patients historically had lower healthcare costs not because they were healthier, but because structural barriers limited their access to care.
958	Obermeyer and colleagues (Science, 2019) showed that a widely used commercial risk-prediction algorithm systematically underestimated the health needs of Black patients, because it was trained on historical cost data shaped by unequal access — the model learned to encode access barriers as lower predicted need.
959	The COVID-19 pandemic produced one technology outcome that has proven durably beneficial for value-based care: the rapid normalization of telehealth as a routine care delivery modality.
960	Between March and June 2020, CMS issued emergency waivers that allowed Medicare to reimburse telehealth visits at parity with in-person visits, removed geographic restrictions that had historically limited telehealth to rural areas, and authorized audio-only visits for patients without video capability.
961	Telehealth surged from about 0.2% of outpatient evaluation-and-management visits in February 2020 to a peak of roughly 50.7% in April 2020 — on the order of 78 times the pre-pandemic level (CDC MMWR; HHS ASPE) — demonstrating both latent demand and the clinical feasibility of delivering much of primary care and chronic disease management by video or phone.
962	The scale of Medicare adoption is documented: the HHS Office of Inspector General found that more than 28 million Medicare beneficiaries — over 2 in 5 — used telehealth in the first year of the pandemic, using 88 times more telehealth services than the year before, with office visits making up just under half of that use.
963	The same review found urban beneficiaries more likely than rural ones to use telehealth — a reminder that the rural populations with the most to gain also face the broadband and device barriers that limit uptake.
964	Congress answered with a series of short-term extensions rather than a permanent fix: the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2023 carried the flexibilities through 2024, later stopgap bills carried them into 2025, they briefly lapsed during the funding gaps of late 2025 and January 31, 2026, and the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2026 (H.R. 7148, enacted February 2026) extended them — including home as an originating site, audio-only visits, and FQHC/RHC distant-site services — through December 31, 2027.
965	An ACO serving a rural population where 40% of attributed patients live more than 30 miles from the nearest primary care office cannot achieve the visit frequency that effective chronic disease management requires without telehealth as a structural component of its care model.
966	For healthcare leaders assessing their organization’s VBC technology readiness, the most common mistake is attempting to deploy advanced analytics capabilities before the foundational data quality and connectivity infrastructure is in place.
967	Phase 1 — Data Foundation (Months 1–6): Audit your EHR’s structured data quality.
968	Implement standardized coding for diagnoses (ICD-10), medications (RxNorm), and procedures (CPT).
969	Phase 2 — Connectivity (Months 4–12): Activate your state HIE connection and configure admission-discharge-transfer (ADT) alert feeds so that care coordinators receive real-time notifications when attributed patients are admitted to or discharged from any participating hospital in your market.
970	Phase 3 — Panel Management (Months 9–18): Implement a population health management platform that allows care coordinators to view and filter their attributed patient panel by risk level, care gap status, and recent utilization events.
971	Phase 4 — Predictive Intelligence (Months 18–36): Implement AI-powered risk stratification once your EHR data quality and HIE connectivity are sufficient to feed the model with the multi-modal data it requires for accurate predictions.
972	Phase 5 — Continuous Improvement (Ongoing): Establish a quarterly data quality review cycle that monitors EHR documentation completeness, risk model accuracy, care gap closure rates, and population health outcome trends.
