## interop-hl7-v2
51	HL7 Version 2: The Workhorse of Healthcare Messaging
52	HL7 v2 Origins and Dominance
53	In March 1987, a group of hospital CIOs, software vendors, and healthcare informaticists convened at the Hospital of the University of Pennsylvania, convened by Dr.
54	The first version of HL7 (Health Level Seven) emerged from that 1987 meeting, and Version 2 was published the following year in 1988, adding billing and lab results workflows.
55	HL7 v2 was not the most elegant technical design ever produced, but it was pragmatic, vendor-agnostic, and — crucially — it shipped at exactly the moment hospitals needed it.
56	Nearly four decades later, HL7 Version 2 remains the dominant messaging standard in global healthcare.
57	HL7 International reports that 95% of U.S. healthcare organizations use HL7 v2, and the standard has been adopted across more than 35 countries.
58	Every major EHR, laboratory information system, radiology information system, and pharmacy system supports HL7 v2.
59	The enduring dominance of HL7 v2 is a case study in how standards succeed in high-stakes industries.
60	Newer standards like HL7 FHIR offer dramatically better design and web-native interoperability, but replacing HL7 v2 requires replacing thousands of carefully tuned, mission-critical interfaces.
61	Most health systems will run HL7 v2 alongside FHIR for many years to come, not replace it outright.
62	Use HL7 v2 according to HL7 International
63	Have adopted the HL7 v2 standard
64	Mirth Connect, a leading HL7 engine, operates in over 40 countries
65	HL7 v2 messages are plain text, structured hierarchically using delimiter characters rather than XML tags or JSON keys.
66	This means a raw HL7 v2 message looks, to the uninitiated, like line noise — but to integration engineers, each position carries precise clinical meaning.
67	If any segment is the "shipping label" of the HL7 envelope, it is MSH.
68	A realistic HL7 v2 lab result message (ORU^R01) might look like this: "MSH|^~\&|LAB_SYS|MAIN_HOSPITAL|EHR|MAIN_HOSPITAL|20240315143022||ORU^R01|MSG00123|P|2.5" on the first line, followed by "PID|1||MRN123456^^^MAIN_HOSPITAL||JOHNSON^SARAH^M||19820714|F" identifying the patient, then "OBR|1|ORD987654|LAB987654|85025^COMPLETE BLOOD COUNT^CPT" describing the CBC order, and finally a series of OBX lines such as "OBX|1|NM|718-7^HEMOGLOBIN^LN||13.8|g/dL|12.0-16.0|N|||F" carrying the hemoglobin result with value, units, reference range, and a normal flag.
69	Parsing that string correctly requires knowing not just the delimiters but the HL7 data type and table definitions for every field position.
70	OBX|1|NM|718-7^HEMOGLOBIN^LN||13.8|g/dL|12.0-16.0|N|||F
71	Field 1 (Set ID): 1 — sequence number within the message
72	Field 2 (Value Type): NM — numeric
73	Field 3 (Observation Identifier): 718-7^HEMOGLOBIN^LN — LOINC code, name, coding system
74	Field 5 (Observation Value): 13.8 — the result
75	Field 7 (Reference Range): 12.0–16.0
76	Field 8 (Abnormal Flag): N — normal
77	Field 11 (Observation Result Status): F — final result
78	When a patient arrives and is admitted, the registration system fires an ADT^A01 message to every downstream system — the pharmacy, the dietary system, the billing engine, the clinical documentation platform — so that each knows a new patient encounter has begun.
79	When that patient moves from the emergency department to the ICU, an ADT^A02 transfer message propagates the location change.
80	When they go home, ADT^A03 closes the encounter.
81	And whenever patient demographics are corrected — a misspelled name, an updated insurance ID — ADT^A08 broadcasts the update.
82	These four event types (A01, A02, A03, A08) alone account for the majority of ADT message volume in most health systems, and their reliable delivery is a prerequisite for accurate care coordination, billing, and quality reporting.
83	When a clinician places a lab or radiology order, the EHR sends an ORM^O01 to the laboratory or radiology information system.
84	When results are available, the lab system returns an ORU^R01 carrying OBX segments for each result.
85	For scheduling, SIU (Scheduling Information Unsolicited) messages — ranging from SIU^S12 (notification of new appointment) to SIU^S15 (notification of appointment cancellation) — synchronize scheduling modules across EHR, radiology, and referral management platforms.
86	MDM (Medical Document Management) messages, particularly MDM^T02, carry transcribed clinical notes, operative reports, and discharge summaries from dictation and transcription platforms back into the EHR.
87	When a provider complains that lab results are not appearing in the EHR, the problem is almost certainly in the ORU pipeline — perhaps a malformed OBX segment, an unrecognized LOINC code in OBX-3, or a mismatch in the patient identifier that prevents the result from attaching to the correct encounter.
88	When the pharmacy does not know a patient has been admitted, the ADT^A01 message likely failed delivery or was silently dropped due to a message profile mismatch.
89	Tracing the HL7 message type immediately narrows the diagnostic search space from the entire hospital IT ecosystem to a specific interface and segment.
90	Clinician selects a lab test in the EHR; the system generates an ORM^O01 message containing the order details in OBR and patient details in PID.
91	The LIS generates an ORU^R01 with one OBX segment per result value.
92	The LIS transmits the ORU^R01 back through the integration engine to the EHR.

94	The HL7 v2 Interoperability Problem: Standards That Do Not Standardize
95	HL7 v2's greatest strength — flexibility — is also its deepest flaw.
96	Two systems that both claim compliance with HL7 v2.3 can be entirely incompatible in practice.
97	One laboratory might encode hemoglobin results using a local code "HGB" in OBX-3, while another uses the LOINC code "718-7^HEMOGLOBIN^LN".
98	Both are technically valid under HL7 v2.3.
99	The receiving EHR, however, may only be configured to recognize LOINC codes — meaning the first lab's results will be received but silently fail to map, leaving clinicians staring at an empty result field while the data sits orphaned in an integration engine queue.
100	Every HL7 v2 segment beginning with the letter "Z" is a locally defined, proprietary extension.
101	The practical consequence of HL7 v2's implementation flexibility is a phenomenon called "interface engine proliferation." Because each connection between two systems requires custom translation logic to reconcile differences in field usage, code sets, and Z-segments, health systems have accumulated integration engines — Mirth Connect, Rhapsody, InterSystems HealthShare, Cloverleaf — running thousands of individual channel configurations.
102	A large academic medical center may operate a very large number of active HL7 interfaces, each one a small bespoke software artifact.
103	HL7 v2 interfaces frequently accept and acknowledge messages (sending back an ACK) that they then fail to process correctly due to code mapping errors or unrecognized Z-segments.
104	That is HL7 v2 interoperability in practice.
105	HL7 v2 defines message structure (the plug shape) but leaves vocabulary, coding systems, and local extensions (the voltage and custom pins) to each implementer — creating the illusion of standardization while preserving the reality of point-to-point incompatibility.
106	HL7 v2 and FHIR Coexistence: The Long Transition
107	HL7 FHIR (Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources), published in 2014 and now at Release 4 and beyond, was designed to address almost every limitation of HL7 v2: it uses RESTful APIs and JSON/XML, enforces structure through defined resource types, mandates standard terminologies, and is natively web-compatible.
108	The question is not whether FHIR is technically superior to HL7 v2 — it clearly is.
109	The question is how to get from here to there without disrupting the billions of clinical transactions that depend on HL7 v2 every day.
110	HL7 International has explicitly recognized this reality by publishing the official HL7 Version 2 to FHIR Implementation Guide, a project maintained by the Orders and Observations working group that provides standardized mappings from HL7 v2 messages, segments, and data types to their FHIR R4 equivalents.
111	The transport layer for HL7 v2 — MLLP, the Minimal Lower Layer Protocol — is another dimension of the legacy that will persist.
112	MLLP wraps HL7 v2 messages in a simple envelope using start-of-block (ASCII 0x0B) and end-of-block (ASCII 0x1C) characters, then transmits the wrapped message over a persistent TCP connection.
113	IANA registers port 2575 for HL7, though in practice each interface listens on whatever port the two parties agree — and it requires a persistent, stateful TCP connection between sender and receiver.
114	As long as legacy systems speak only MLLP, the integration engine's job is to receive MLLP HL7 v2, transform it to FHIR, and deliver it to modern API endpoints — acting as a universal translator at the boundary between eras.
115	The v2-to-FHIR mapping project (available at build.fhir.org/ig/HL7/v2-to-fhir) defines, for example, how the fields of an HL7 v2 PID segment map to FHIR Patient resource attributes: PID-3 (Patient Identifier List) maps to Patient.identifier, PID-5 (Patient Name) maps to Patient.name, PID-7 (Date of Birth) maps to Patient.birthDate, and so on.
116	Similarly, an ADT^A01 message maps to a FHIR Bundle containing a MessageHeader, Patient, Encounter, and related resources.
117	These mappings allow integration engines to serve as automated translators — converting the legacy pipe-delimited messages arriving from a 20-year-old laboratory system into standards-conformant FHIR resources that can be consumed by a modern clinical decision support platform or a patient-facing app operating under 21st Century Cures Act API mandates.
118	The coexistence of v2 and FHIR is not a failure of modernization; it is the realistic, pragmatic path forward for an industry where patient safety cannot tolerate a big-bang replacement of working infrastructure.

## interop-fhir-intro
144	For most of the past four decades, healthcare data exchange ran on a standard called HL7 version 2, first released in 1987.
145	HL7 v2 is a pipe-delimited text format — essentially a structured text file where each field is separated by a vertical bar character.
146	The problem is that HL7 v2 was never truly standardized: each hospital, lab, and payer customized the format to their own needs.
147	The result was that two organizations could each claim HL7 v2 compliance yet be completely unable to exchange data without expensive custom interface engines sitting between them.
148	HL7 attempted to solve this chaos with version 3 and its companion standard, the Clinical Document Architecture (CDA).
149	Released in 2005, CDA introduced XML-based documents like the Continuity of Care Document (CCD) and brought more rigorous modeling using a formal Reference Information Model (RIM).
150	In 2011, Grahame Grieve — an Australian health informaticist running a small consulting firm called Health Intersections — put forward a proposal called "Resources for Healthcare." He argued that instead of modeling the entire healthcare universe in a single monolithic schema, the industry should define discrete, self-contained data objects — resources — that could be exchanged individually over the web using the same RESTful HTTP conventions already powering every modern web application.
151	HL7 adopted the proposal, renamed the project Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources (FHIR), and Grieve went on to lead its development as HL7's FHIR Product Director.
152	HL7 v2 and CDA are like sending a fax — the technology works, but every machine has its own quirks, and you need a human on both ends to interpret the smudged output.
153	Grahame Grieve publishes the resource-oriented framework that becomes HL7 FHIR
154	The first normative release of FHIR, establishing a stable regulatory baseline
155	FHIR R4 defines 145 resource types spanning clinical data, administrative data, financial records, and the specification infrastructure itself.
156	The US Core Implementation Guide, developed by HL7 and referenced by U.S. federal regulation, defines the minimum data set that certified EHR systems must expose — ensuring that a patient's medications, allergies, and problem list are available in a predictable, consistent format regardless of the underlying vendor.
157	A minimal Patient resource looks like: { "resourceType": "Patient", "id": "12345", "name": [{ "family": "Smith", "given": ["Jane"] }], "birthDate": "1975-04-12", "gender": "female" }.
158	That entire record — parseable by any JSON library in any language — is retrieved with a single HTTP GET to https://server.example.com/fhir/Patient/12345.
159	FHIR R4 vs R4B vs R5: Understanding the Version Landscape
160	FHIR R4, published on January 2, 2019, was a landmark release for one specific reason: for the first time, core components of the FHIR specification — the RESTful API framework, the XML and JSON serialization formats, and a set of foundational data types — passed HL7's normative ballot process.
161	"Normative" in HL7 terminology means the specification has been formally reviewed, balloted, and approved as a stable standard that will not undergo breaking changes without a full new normative ballot cycle.
162	R4 became the regulatory baseline for U.S. federal interoperability rules and remains so today.
163	FHIR R4B (version 4.3.0) was published on May 28, 2022.
164	Rather than a full new release, R4B was a limited, targeted update designed to bring selected new clinical features — particularly around subscriptions, citations, and evidence-based medicine resources — to maturity without breaking R4 compatibility.
165	R4B maintained full backward compatibility with R4 for the vast majority of resources, meaning an R4 server could largely interoperate with an R4B client.
166	For most implementers, especially those building systems to meet U.S. regulatory requirements, R4B represented a refinement rather than a migration target.
167	FHIR R5 was published on March 26, 2023.
168	R5 expanded the normative core, refined the subscription framework, improved support for clinical reasoning and evidence-based medicine, and introduced a number of new resource types and cross-version analysis tools.
169	However, because U.S. regulation explicitly cites FHIR R4 as the required standard, and because certification bodies and major EHR vendors have invested years building R4 implementations, R4 remains the de facto deployment standard for regulated healthcare in the United States.
170	R5 adoption will grow over time — particularly as new Implementation Guides are written against it — but implementers in 2025 still encounter R4 as the universal baseline in production systems.

172	Substantial maturation; many large implementations deployed on STU3 and have not migrated.

175	The single most consequential event in FHIR's adoption history was the publication of two companion federal rules on May 1, 2020.
176	The ONC 21st Century Cures Act Final Rule — formally titled "21st Century Cures Act: Interoperability, Information Blocking, and the ONC Health IT Certification Program" — required certified EHR developers to implement a new standardized API criterion (§ 170.315(g)(10)) based specifically on HL7 FHIR Release 4.
177	EHR developers were originally given 24 months from publication to update their technology; ONC's November 2020 interim final rule moved the deadline to December 31, 2022.
178	On the same day, the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services published the CMS Interoperability and Patient Access Final Rule (CMS-9115-F).
179	Payers were required to make this API available by July 1, 2021.
180	Together, these two rules created an unprecedented regulatory pull: the entire U.S. healthcare ecosystem — hospitals, clinics, payers, and health IT vendors — faced federal mandates to deploy FHIR R4 APIs within a defined timeline.
181	The FHIR Roadmap for TEFCA, published by the Sequoia Project in December 2023, laid out a phased four-stage plan for FHIR exchange under TEFCA, targeting full production availability of QHIN-facilitated FHIR exchange (Stage 2) in 2024 and pilots of QHIN-to-QHIN FHIR exchange (Stage 3) in 2025.

184	SMART — which stands for Substitutable Medical Applications and Reusable Technologies — originated in 2010 as a project at Harvard Medical School and Boston Children's Hospital, funded by a four-year, $15 million grant from the Office of the National Coordinator for Health Information Technology.
185	In the second half of 2013, the SMART team updated their framework to use FHIR as the underlying data model, and SMART on FHIR was publicly demonstrated at HIMSS in February 2014.
186	SMART on FHIR works by layering OAuth 2.0 authorization flows on top of FHIR APIs.
187	The entire flow uses industry-standard OAuth 2.0 and OpenID Connect protocols, meaning healthcare apps can leverage the same security infrastructure as consumer and enterprise software.
188	SMART on FHIR 2.0 added backend services authorization for server-to-server use cases — enabling population health analytics, quality reporting, and payer data exchange without a human in the loop.
189	The major EHR vendors have fully embraced FHIR R4 and SMART on FHIR, in large part because federal regulation left them no choice — but adoption has also created genuine market opportunity.
190	Oracle Health's Ignite APIs — the FHIR platform for the former Cerner EHR — operate entirely on R4 and support SMART on FHIR launches from within the clinical workflow.
191	This write-once, run-anywhere vision, first articulated in 2010, is now a practical reality across the majority of U.S. hospital beds.

195	FHIR (Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources) was created in 2011 by Grahame Grieve to replace the complexity of HL7 v2 and CDA with a web-native, resource-oriented approach to health data exchange.
196	FHIR R4, published January 2, 2019, was the first normative release — meaning core components (REST API, JSON/XML formats, foundational data types) are stable and will not break without a new ballot cycle.

## interop-cures-act
258	The 21st Century Cures Act and Interoperability: What the Law Actually Requires
259	On December 13, 2016, President Obama signed the 21st Century Cures Act into law.
260	The legislation was one of the most sweeping health information technology reforms in the United States since the HITECH Act of 2009, and its interoperability provisions — concentrated in Title IV — set off a regulatory cascade that would reshape how hospitals, insurers, app developers, and patients exchange electronic health information for years to come.
261	The actual requirements that providers and health IT vendors had to meet came primarily through two landmark final rules published simultaneously on May 1, 2020: the ONC 21st Century Cures Act Final Rule (85 FR 25642) and the CMS Interoperability and Patient Access Final Rule (CMS-9115-F).
262	The 21st Century Cures Act is a sprawling omnibus law covering topics ranging from cancer research to mental health to drug approvals.
263	Title IV ("Delivery," Sections 4001–4012) holds its health information technology provisions, alongside a few Medicare items such as telehealth.
264	Four of its sections — 4001 through 4004 — together define the statutory framework for interoperability and information blocking.
265	Section 4001 directed HHS to set a strategy for reducing the regulatory and administrative burden that EHR use places on clinicians.
266	Section 4002 updated the certification program for electronic health records, requiring that certified technology not block information sharing.
267	Section 4003 directed ONC to develop a Trusted Exchange Framework for nationwide interoperability.
268	And Section 4004 — the most consequential from a compliance standpoint — formally prohibited information blocking and established penalties for violations.
269	USCDI Version 1, which became the baseline for the Cures Act final rules, defined 16 data classes including patient demographics, clinical notes, laboratory results, medications, vital signs, and provenance information.
270	Subsequent versions have expanded these requirements, with USCDI v2 adding social determinants of health data elements, v3 becoming the certification baseline under HTI-1, and v4 incorporating additional clinical data.
271	Both ONC and CMS rules appeared in Federal Register simultaneously (85 FR 25642)
272	Sections 4001–4004 covering strategy, certification, TEFCA, and information blocking
273	The Information Blocking Prohibition: Section 4004 in Detail
274	Section 4004 of the Cures Act created an entirely new category of federal health care violation: information blocking.
275	The definition of EHI itself was phased in: initially limited to data elements in USCDI v1, and then expanded by October 2022 to encompass the full scope of a patient's designated record set as defined under HIPAA.
276	ONC's 2020 final rule established eight regulatory exceptions that, when met, mean a practice does not constitute information blocking.
277	These are: (1) Preventing Harm — blocking is permitted when it substantially reduces a genuine risk of harm to a patient or third party; (2) Privacy — compliance with state or federal privacy laws or patient-requested restrictions; (3) Security — protecting against threats to the confidentiality, integrity, or availability of EHI, but only when applied consistently and non-discriminatorily; (4) Infeasibility — technically or organizationally impossible to fulfill a specific request; (5) Health IT Performance — system maintenance or upgrades requiring temporary unavailability; (6) Content and Manner — responding in an alternative manner when a request is unreasonable in its scope; (7) Fees — charging fees based on objective, verifiable criteria that are applied uniformly and reasonably related to costs; and (8) Licensing — licensing interoperability elements on reasonable and non-discriminatory terms.
278	HTI-1 (December 2023) later added a TEFCA Manner exception and narrowed Content and Manner to Manner, and a December 2024 rule added a Protecting Care Access exception; ASTP's HTI-5 proposed rule (December 2025) would eliminate the TEFCA Manner exception.
279	From April 5, 2021 through July 31, 2026, ONC's Report Information Blocking Portal logged 2,450 claims of possible information blocking; patients are the largest group of claimants, and health care providers are the most frequently named actors.

286	The ONC Cures Act Final Rule's most technically consequential provision was the creation of a new certification criterion for standardized APIs using HL7 FHIR Release 4 (FHIR R4).
287	This criterion — codified at 45 CFR 170.315(g)(10) — requires that certified EHR technology (CEHRT) support application programming interfaces that allow both individual patients and authorized third-party apps to access health data programmatically, without special effort, and at no cost to the patient.

292	The CMS Interoperability and Patient Access Rule (CMS-9115-F) extended parallel FHIR API requirements to payers — specifically Medicare Advantage organizations, Medicaid and CHIP fee-for-service programs, Medicaid managed care organizations, CHIP managed care entities, and Qualified Health Plans in the Federally Facilitated Marketplace.
293	These payers were required to implement a FHIR R4-based Patient Access API making claims, encounter data, and clinical data (conforming to USCDI v1) available to members.
294	Data had to be available from January 1, 2016 forward for all current enrollees.

304	ONC published TEFCA's foundational documents in January 2022, and in December 2023 reached the pivotal milestone of designating the first Qualified Health Information Networks (QHINs) — the organizations that anchor TEFCA's exchange infrastructure.
305	By February 2024, seven organizations had been designated as QHINs: eHealth Exchange, Epic Nexus, Health Gorilla, KONZA, MedAllies, CommonWell Health Alliance, and Kno2. eClinicalWorks (January 2025), Surescripts (April 2025), Netsmart (August 2025), and Oracle Health Information Network (November 2025) brought the total to eleven.

307	Under TEFCA: (1) The hospital's EHR, connected to TEFCA through a QHIN, sends a Treatment-purpose query to the TEFCA network.
308	(2) The TEFCA infrastructure routes the query to any QHIN whose participants include the Florida practice.
309	(3) The QHIN serving the Florida practice returns the patient's medication list, problem list, and allergy information, typically as a standardized C-CDA document.
310	(4) The Vermont EHR displays the results — no phone calls, no fax, no bilateral agreement required.
311	Since the information blocking prohibition took effect in April 2021 and the FHIR API certification deadlines began hitting in late 2022, measurable change has occurred in the health IT landscape.
312	ONC reported in August 2022 that only about 5% of certified health IT developers had updated to the certified API technology, yet those developers supported roughly 66% of inpatient and 77% of ambulatory users, because the dominant vendors adopted the criterion early.
313	Payers subject to CMS-9115-F have stood up Patient Access APIs, giving millions of Medicaid and marketplace enrollees machine-readable access to their claims and clinical data for the first time.
314	CMS's own reviews of Medicare Advantage online directories found that 45–52% of listed provider locations had at least one inaccuracy (2016–2018 review rounds), undermining the value of the API mandate.
315	ONC's Health Data, Technology, and Interoperability (HTI-1) final rule, published in December 2023, added a TEFCA Manner exception to the information blocking rules and made USCDI v3 the certification baseline.
316	A subsequent HTI-2 rulemaking proposed additional standards and strengthened API transparency requirements.
317	HHS also finalized provider disincentives for information blocking in June 2024 — a separate track from OIG enforcement — under which CMS can, for example, zero out a clinician's MIPS Promoting Interoperability score, withhold part of a hospital's market-basket update, or bar a provider from the Medicare Shared Savings Program for at least a year.
318	The cumulative effect of these rules, if fully implemented, would represent the most comprehensive federal interoperability infrastructure the United States has ever had.

## interop-info-blocking
339	In 2016 Congress passed the 21st Century Cures Act with a deceptively simple goal: make health data flow freely.
340	Section 4004 of that law created a legal prohibition against "information blocking" — defined in regulation at 45 CFR 171.103 as any practice by an "actor" that is likely to interfere with, prevent, or materially discourage the access, exchange, or use of electronic health information (EHI), unless required by law or covered by one of the enumerated regulatory exceptions.
341	Three categories of entities qualify as "actors" under the rule: (1) health IT developers of certified health IT, (2) health information exchanges and health information networks (HIEs/HINs), and (3) healthcare providers.
342	From April 5, 2021 through October 5, 2022, EHI was limited to the data classes and elements defined in the United States Core Data for Interoperability (USCDI) Version 1 — a relatively narrow set of clinical data including demographics, vital signs, laboratory results, and medications.
343	Starting October 6, 2022, the definition expanded dramatically to encompass all information in a patient's HIPAA Designated Record Set (DRS) — every medical record, billing record, and other documentation used to make decisions about a patient, including images, wave files, and any other form of ePHI.
344	Scope shifted from USCDI v1 to the entire HIPAA Designated Record Set
345	The 21st Century Cures Act is the law that says: the bridge must be open, the tolls must be reasonable, and the road must actually be drivable.
346	ONC's 2020 rule finalized eight regulatory exceptions — codified at 45 CFR Part 171, Subparts B and C — that describe the circumstances under which an actor's practice of limiting EHI access does NOT constitute information blocking.
347	Later rules added a TEFCA Manner exception (HTI-1, December 2023; Subpart D), renamed Content and Manner to Manner, and added a Protecting Care Access exception (December 2024); ASTP's HTI-5 proposed rule (December 2025) would eliminate TEFCA Manner.
348	The five "not fulfilling" exceptions are: (1) the Preventing Harm Exception, which allows actors to limit EHI access when there is a reasonable belief that doing so would prevent substantial harm to a patient or another person; (2) the Privacy Exception, which covers situations where applicable state or federal privacy law prohibits the disclosure — this exception has multiple sub-conditions tied to specific HIPAA and state law scenarios; (3) the Security Exception, which permits practices directly related to safeguarding the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of EHI, tailored to specific security risks and implemented consistently and non-discriminatorily; (4) the Infeasibility Exception, which acknowledges that there are genuine technical, operational, or legal constraints that can make it infeasible to fulfill a particular request; and (5) the Health IT Performance Exception, which permits health IT systems to be taken offline or have performance temporarily degraded for maintenance, upgrades, and other routine operational activities.
349	The three "fulfilling requests" exceptions govern the manner in which actors respond to legitimate EHI requests: (6) the Content and Manner Exception allows actors to negotiate and maintain reasonable terms for the access, exchange, and use of EHI — but importantly, this exception requires that the actor first attempt to reach agreement using content and manner that meets the requestor's needs; (7) the Fees Exception permits actors to charge fees for providing EHI access, but those fees must be based on objective and verifiable cost-based criteria uniformly applied to all similarly situated requestors — a $50,000 fee for a connection that costs $500 to maintain would fall well outside this exception; and (8) the Licensing Exception allows health IT developers to require licensees to obtain licenses for interoperability elements, so long as the licensing terms are non-discriminatory and commercially reasonable.

408	The single most consequential regulatory update to the certification program arrived on May 1, 2020, when ONC published the "21st Century Cures Act: Interoperability, Information Blocking, and the ONC Health IT Certification Program" Final Rule in the Federal Register (85 FR 25642).
409	This rule — commonly called the Cures Act Final Rule — substantially upgraded the 2015 Edition certification criteria and created what is now known as the 2015 Edition Cures Update.
410	Developers of certified health IT were required to meet the new API standards and provide them to customers by December 31, 2022 (the original May 2, 2022 deadline was extended by a COVID-19 interim final rule).
411	The headline technical requirement introduced by the Cures Update is a standardized API for both single-patient and population-level data services, built on HL7 FHIR Release 4 (FHIR R4).
412	Specifically, certified products must implement the FHIR US Core Implementation Guide, the FHIR Bulk Data Access Implementation Guide for population-level queries, and the HL7 SMART Application Launch Framework Implementation Guide so that third-party applications can authenticate and retrieve data securely.
413	Alongside the API requirement, the Cures Update mandated support for the United States Core Data for Interoperability Version 1 (USCDI v1) as the minimum data set.
414	USCDI v1 defines 52 individual data elements organized into 16 data classes — covering allergies and intolerances, assessment and plan of treatment, care team members, clinical notes, goals, health concerns, immunizations, laboratory, medications, patient demographics, problems, procedures, provenance, smoking status, unique device identifiers for implantable devices, and vital signs.
415	Before the Cures Update, a hospital could use a 2015 Edition-certified EHR that supported only proprietary or HL7 v2 interfaces for data exchange.
416	After the update, that same hospital's vendor was required to ship a FHIR R4-based patient-facing API, a bulk data export endpoint for population analytics, and an EHI export tool — all tested against specific implementation guides.
417	This meant that a patient could, for the first time, connect a smartphone app directly to their hospital's EHR using the same open standards their bank uses for account aggregation.

464	The HIPAA Privacy Rule, codified at 45 CFR §164.524, grants every patient the right to inspect and obtain a copy of their protected health information (PHI) maintained in a designated record set.
465	A patient submits a written request to a covered entity — a hospital, physician practice, insurer, or their business associates — and the covered entity must act on that request within 30 calendar days of receipt.
466	A single 30-day extension is permitted if the entity notifies the patient in writing of the reason for delay and provides a date by which it will complete the action.
467	OCR launched a Right of Access Initiative in 2019, specifically targeting providers who delayed, denied, or overcharged patients for their records.
468	In December 2024, OCR finalized a $200,000 civil monetary penalty against OHSU — its 53rd Right of Access enforcement action — and in January 2025 it announced a $60,000 settlement with South Broward Hospital District (Memorial Healthcare System).
469	Under HIPAA 45 CFR §164.524, patients have the right to direct their PHI to a third party of their choosing — including a health app, a caregiver, or another provider.
470	The 21st Century Cures Act's information blocking rules extend further, covering all electronic health information (EHI) — a broader category than HIPAA's designated record set.
471	21st Century Cures Act: The Information Blocking Prohibition
472	The 21st Century Cures Act, signed into law in December 2016, took the concept of patient access and reframed it as a prohibition framework.
473	This prohibition, codified in the ONC Cures Act Final Rule at 45 CFR Part 171, took effect April 5, 2021.
474	ONC's 2020 rule codified eight exceptions — among them preventing harm, privacy, security, and infeasibility — and later rules added TEFCA Manner and Protecting Care Access; practices required by law fall outside the definition itself.
475	The scope of EHI covered by the prohibition expanded in October 2022 from a subset of structured data to nearly all information in a patient's designated record set — including clinical notes, imaging reports, and behavioral health records.
476	HHS-OIG began enforcing against health IT developers and health information exchanges and networks for conduct on or after September 1, 2023, with civil monetary penalties of up to $1,000,000 per violation.

## interop-smart-fhir
535	The conceptual breakthrough came in 2010 when researchers at Harvard Medical School and Boston Children's Hospital launched a project called Substitutable Medical Applications and Reusable Technologies — SMART.
536	In the second half of 2013, the team updated the platform to use the emerging HL7 FHIR standard for data exchange, giving birth to SMART on FHIR.
537	The new platform debuted publicly at the HIMSS conference in February 2014, with prototype demonstrations from several commercial EHR vendors and app developers.
538	For the first time, the EHR could become an extensible platform rather than a monolithic silo.
539	How SMART on FHIR Works: OAuth 2.0 Meets the Clinical Workflow
540	At its core, SMART on FHIR is a profile on top of two well-established internet standards: OAuth 2.0 for authorization and HL7 FHIR for clinical data.
541	OAuth 2.0 is the same protocol that powers "Sign in with Google" and "Connect with Facebook" across the web — a battle-tested framework for letting a user grant a third-party application limited access to their data without sharing their password.
542	OAuth 2.0 is the intercom system.
543	SMART on FHIR uses OAuth 2.0 to grant apps time-limited, scoped access to EHR data — never the master credentials.
544	Both flows use the same underlying OAuth 2.0 authorization code exchange mechanism.
545	The app uses the access token as a Bearer token in HTTP Authorization headers to call FHIR R4 endpoints — e.g., GET /Patient/{id}/MedicationRequest — and retrieves structured clinical data.
546	The ASCVD Risk Calculator, embedded in EHRs at multiple institutions, pulls a patient's lab results, demographics, and medication list via FHIR and computes a 10-year cardiovascular risk score in real time — without the clinician copying any data.
547	CDS Hooks is now published by HL7 as part of the broader FHIR-based interoperability ecosystem, and the two standards are designed to work together: a CDS Hook suggestion can launch a SMART app for deeper investigation or action.
548	The SMART Backend Services specification, published by HL7 International as part of the SMART App Launch Implementation Guide, defines exactly how to handle this scenario.
549	It uses the OAuth 2.0 Client Credentials grant flow, adapted for healthcare, with JSON Web Token (JWT) assertions for client authentication.
550	It posts this JWT to the authorization server's token endpoint using a standard OAuth 2.0 client credentials request.
551	The client then uses this token to call FHIR APIs — including the $export operation for Bulk FHIR — without any human interaction.
552	The specification requires TLS 1.2 or higher for all exchanges.
553	When a hospital system needs to export a million patient records to a population health vendor for annual quality reporting, or when a payer needs to automatically retrieve claims-linked clinical data, Backend Services provides the secure, auditable, machine-to-machine credential exchange that makes it possible.
554	SMART on FHIR moved from an academic research project to a federal legal requirement through the 21st Century Cures Act and the ONC's Health IT Certification Program.
555	The original 2015 Edition rules introduced patient-facing API criteria without mandating FHIR.

## interop-cms-patient-access-api
576	The Policy Origin: CMS-9115-F and the Interoperability Mandate
577	On May 1, 2020, the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services published the landmark Interoperability and Patient Access final rule, formally known as CMS-9115-F.
578	The rule arrived amid a pandemic, yet it represented years of policymaking work aimed at dismantling one of the most persistent barriers in American healthcare: the inability of patients to easily obtain and move their own health data.
579	CMS-9115-F changed the legal landscape fundamentally by requiring regulated payers to open standardized, secure digital doors directly to patients and the applications patients choose.
580	This scope is intentional — together these programs cover well over 100 million Americans, and establishing a uniform standard across them creates the critical mass needed to make patient-facing data exchange a market reality rather than a pilot curiosity.
581	The technical standard chosen by CMS was HL7 FHIR Release 4.0.1, the then-current version of the Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources specification.
582	FHIR R4 provides a REST-based API architecture with well-defined resource types, enabling developers to build consumer applications without requiring bespoke integrations for each payer.
583	CMS recognized implementation complexity created by the COVID-19 pandemic and exercised enforcement discretion through July 1, 2021, at which point active enforcement of the Patient Access API and Provider Directory API requirements began in earnest.
584	CMS-9115-F signed into the Federal Register
585	CMS-9115-F identified three primary categories of data that must be available: claims and encounter information (including cost data such as provider remittances and enrollee cost-sharing), clinical data including laboratory results when maintained by the payer, and formulary and coverage data.
586	CMS-0057-F (the 2024 successor rule) escalated the bar by requiring conformance with USCDI v3, a step up from the USCDI v1 that most CMS-9115-F implementations targeted.
587	Because payers primarily hold administrative data, the clinical data tier remains the most challenging compliance frontier.
588	Access to this data is member-authorized and flows through the OAuth 2.0 SMART App Launch protocol, which allows a member to grant a third-party application permission to retrieve their records directly from the payer's FHIR server.

591	Payer-to-Payer Data Exchange: From CMS-9115-F to CMS-0057-F
592	One of the most ambitious provisions of the original CMS-9115-F was the requirement that payers exchange data with each other when a member transitions between plans.
593	On December 8, 2021, CMS announced enforcement discretion on the payer-to-payer provisions of CMS-9115-F, acknowledging that the member-mediated authentication model outlined in the original rule was not well-suited for business-to-business data transfers.
594	The 2024 CMS Interoperability and Prior Authorization final rule (CMS-0057-F), published January 17, 2024, resolved these tensions with a redesigned Payer-to-Payer API framework.
595	Under CMS-0057-F, payers are no longer required to use patient-mediated SMART App Launch authentication for payer-to-payer exchanges — CMS recognized that "protocols requiring user level credentials managed by the payer are generally not appropriate for business-to-business data exchanges." Instead, the new model requires that when a member enrolls in a new plan, the receiving payer must collect the member's opt-in consent and information about former or concurrent plans, then — within one week of receiving both — request the required data from identified prior payers.
596	The full Payer-to-Payer API compliance deadline under CMS-0057-F is January 1, 2027.
597	The prior payer assembles up to 5 years of claims, USCDI clinical data, and prior auth history using the PDex IG format.
598	Payer-to-Payer Exchange: How It Works Under CMS-0057-F
599	Alongside the Patient Access API, CMS-9115-F required covered payers to implement a publicly accessible, FHIR R4-based Provider Directory API.
600	The implementation guide endorsed by CMS for this purpose is the Da Vinci PDEX Plan-Net IG, currently at version 1.2.0 (published February 25, 2025).
601	Provider directory accuracy has been a chronic industry problem, and the No Surprises Act — which took effect January 1, 2022 — added regulatory teeth by creating a 90-day attestation cycle.
602	Health plans must verify all provider details at least once every 90 days, including physician name, specialty, address, phone number, and network participation status.
603	Despite mandatory requirements, the state of provider directory FHIR APIs as of 2024 remains uneven.
604	CMS-0057-F introduced a Provider Access API — distinct from the public-facing Provider Directory API — that allows providers to query payer data about their own patients, with a separate compliance deadline of January 1, 2027.
605	CMS's own reviews of Medicare Advantage online directories found that 45–52 percent of listed provider locations had at least one inaccuracy (2016–2018).
606	More than four years after the July 2021 enforcement date, the Patient Access API landscape reflects the messy reality of federal mandates meeting real-world implementation complexity.
607	Flexpa's November 2024 report, covering more than 488 payer endpoints across 28 vendors, found that many payers had yet to implement a Patient Access API; Flexpa itself had facilitated about 120,000 successful member connections across 216 health plans — modest against the more than 100 million people the rule covers.
608	The contrast with established health information networks is stark: Carequality reports moving approximately 940 million clinical documents per month through provider-initiated queries, while patient-mediated FHIR pulls via the Patient Access API are orders of magnitude smaller.
609	CMS did, however, signal a new accountability lever: starting January 1, 2026, impacted payers must annually report Patient Access API usage metrics to CMS (first report due March 31, 2026), creating a transparency mechanism that will enable comparative assessment of engagement across plans.
610	Perhaps the most consequential unresolved gap is the privacy protection asymmetry for data that leaves the payer system.
611	This creates a scenario where a member's complete insurance claims history — a detailed proxy for their medical conditions, prescriptions, and provider relationships — could lawfully be sold to data brokers or used for targeted advertising, with the only safeguard being whatever terms the app's privacy policy contains.
612	The ONC and FTC have flagged this gap repeatedly, but as of mid-2025 no federal legislation has closed it comprehensively.
613	California's SB 1419 (enacted 2022; compliance extended to January 1, 2025) and Tennessee's SB 2012 (signed May 2024; APIs due July 1, 2024) extended Patient Access API-style requirements to commercial plans sold outside the federally facilitated exchanges — populations not covered by CMS-9115-F.
614	Meanwhile, the January 2027 deadline under CMS-0057-F for the full suite of new APIs — Payer-to-Payer, Provider Access, and Prior Authorization — represents the next major wave of compliance pressure that will test whether payers can scale from partial Patient Access API conformance to a comprehensive interoperability architecture.

## interop-payer-to-payer
636	This lesson traces the regulatory journey from CMS-9115-F's original 2022 mandate through its supersession by CMS-0057-F's January 2027 deadline, explains the technical architecture built on HL7's Da Vinci PDex Implementation Guide, and examines the real-world challenges of member identity matching, consent management, and data operationalization that stand between the regulatory vision and clinical impact.
637	Before the COVID-19 public health emergency froze Medicaid eligibility redeterminations, studies found that among full-benefit Medicaid beneficiaries enrolled at any point in a given year, roughly 10% experienced a gap in coverage of less than one year.
638	Approximately 4% were disenrolled and re-enrolled within three months, and nearly 7% within six months.
639	The Medicaid unwinding that followed the end of pandemic-era continuous enrollment protections in April 2023 provided a stark illustration of this dynamic at extreme scale.
640	States resumed eligibility redeterminations after a pause of more than three years, and over the following two years, more than 25 million people were disenrolled from Medicaid.

643	In the Affordable Care Act Marketplace, switching rates are substantially higher: 43% of enrollees switched plans between 2015 and 2016, driven by premium volatility, insurer exits, and annual re-evaluation of cost-sharing structures.

648	The Regulatory Journey: From CMS-9115-F to CMS-0057-F
649	The regulatory effort to mandate payer-to-payer data exchange begins with the CMS Interoperability and Patient Access Final Rule, published May 1, 2020 and commonly cited as CMS-9115-F.
650	This landmark rule required Medicare Advantage organizations, Medicaid managed care plans, CHIP managed care entities, and Qualified Health Plans on the Federally Facilitated Exchanges to implement not only a Patient Access API for members but also a payer-to-payer data exchange process.
651	The original compliance deadline for the payer-to-payer provisions was January 1, 2022.
652	On December 8, 2021, weeks before the deadline, CMS announced that it would exercise enforcement discretion and not take action to enforce the payer-to-payer provisions of CMS-9115-F.
653	The agency committed to addressing the challenges through future rulemaking and delivered on that commitment through CMS-0057-F.
654	The CMS Interoperability and Prior Authorization Final Rule, known as CMS-0057-F, was released on January 17, 2024 and represents the definitive federal framework for payer-to-payer data exchange.
655	The rule establishes that the same categories of impacted payers must implement and maintain a FHIR R4-based Payer-to-Payer API.
656	The compliance deadline is January 1, 2027.
657	The rule explicitly recommends the Da Vinci Payer Data Exchange PDex Implementation Guide as the technical standard for this API, building on the HL7 infrastructure that payers were already deploying for the Patient Access API.
658	The scope of data that must be exchanged under CMS-0057-F is substantial and specific.
659	Critically, the rule requires payers to exchange data with a date of service within five years of the exchange request, and for data originating before January 1, 2016, the requirement is limited to data with a service date on or after that date.

664	CMS-0057-F: Payer-to-Payer Data Exchange
665	The technical foundation for payer-to-payer exchange is the Da Vinci Payer Data Exchange Implementation Guide, version 2.2.0, developed by the Da Vinci Project, a collaboration of HL7 and health industry members including payers, providers, and health IT vendors.
666	The PDex IG defines how FHIR R4 resources should be packaged, queried, and exchanged specifically for payer use cases.
667	It establishes the data model covering which FHIR profiles apply to claims, diagnoses, medications, and prior authorizations in a payer context, the access pattern using FHIR RESTful APIs with SMART on FHIR authorization, and the identity-matching operation called $member-match that allows a requesting payer to establish that the member they are asking about is the same person the previous payer has records for.
668	The $member-match operation is the linchpin of the entire exchange.
669	The $member-match operation allows the new payer acting as the requesting payer to send these demographics to the previous payer's FHIR endpoint and receive back a confirmed patient identifier, specifically a FHIR UMB Unique Member Business identifier, that can then be used to query clinical and administrative data.
670	The operation uses mTLS mutual Transport Layer Security for the connection and OAuth 2.0 dynamic client registration for authorization, ensuring that only verified, credentialed payers can initiate data requests.
671	Bulk exchange, added as draft guidance in PDex STU 2.1 to meet CMS-0057-F, allows a new payer to perform member matching and data requests for large populations simultaneously, for example at the end of an open enrollment period when thousands of new members join at once.
672	Bulk exchange uses the FHIR Asynchronous Request Pattern with an HTTP POST kick-off that returns a 202 Accepted response with a Content-Location status endpoint, followed by polling until a completion manifest referencing NDJSON output files is available.
673	Payer-to-payer exchange works the same way: when you switch health plans, you give consent, your new payer sends a $member-match request to your old payer using FHIR APIs, and your five years of claims history and clinical records follow you to your new plan instead of disappearing when you sign up.
674	CMS-0057-F requires payers to obtain and honor member consent; the new payer must initiate the data request within one week of enrollment.
675	Using the secure connection, the requesting payer performs OAuth 2.0 Dynamic Client Registration to acquire client credentials from the previous payer's authorization server.
676	OAuth 2.0 Client Registration
677	The requesting payer calls the $member-match operation on the previous payer's FHIR endpoint, submitting the member's enrollment demographics.
678	Using the matched patient identifier and an OAuth 2.0 access token, the requesting payer queries the previous payer's FHIR API for up to five years of adjudicated claims, USCDI clinical data, and prior authorization history.
679	The previous payer must respond to data requests within one business day under CMS-0057-F.
680	This tight turnaround is designed to ensure that clinical continuity information is available to the new plan before the first care interaction, not weeks later when the member may have already faced a prior authorization denial or a duplicated diagnostic workup.
681	The data scope defined by CMS-0057-F for payer-to-payer exchange is deliberately broad.
682	CMS-0057-F explicitly excludes provider remittances and enrollee cost-sharing information from the required exchange, focusing the mandate on clinically relevant data rather than financial settlement details.
683	Prior authorization history may be the most operationally critical component of the exchange.
684	CMS-0057-F requires the previous payer to include prior authorization data in the exchange package, including the service authorized, the authorization number, the dates, and the clinical rationale.
685	CMS-0057-F mandates exchange of adjudicated claims and encounter data with service dates within 5 years of the exchange request
686	The mandatory compliance date for impacted payers to implement and maintain the Payer-to-Payer FHIR API under CMS-0057-F
687	A 52-year-old woman with relapsing-remitting multiple sclerosis switches from a Medicaid managed care plan to a Marketplace plan during open enrollment.
688	If payer-to-payer data exchange were purely a regulatory and technical standards problem, the January 2027 deadline would be straightforward to meet.
689	The $member-match operation in the PDex IG provides a standardized protocol for matching demographics, but it cannot compensate for demographic data quality problems that are endemic to the health insurance industry.
690	Social Security Numbers, which would provide a reliable matching key, are not universally collected for privacy reasons and are not included in the FHIR $member-match operation's standard input.
691	CMS-0057-F requires member opt-in consent before a payer can initiate a data request to a previous plan.
692	The PDex IG includes consent resource handling within the $member-match workflow, but payers must implement internal processes for capturing, storing, and transmitting consent documentation in a way that both the requesting plan and the disclosing plan trust.
693	For members who have behavioral health or substance use disorder treatment history, categories of data that carry heightened legal protections under 42 CFR Part 2, consent requirements are even more stringent, and the current PDex framework does not yet have a fully standardized solution for segmenting these sensitive records from a bulk exchange.
694	A 2026 Black Book Research report concluded that payer-to-payer interoperability most often fails in the post-ingestion layer where member identity confidence, provenance clarity, completeness signaling, workflow routing, and exception handling remain too immature to translate received data into clinical action.
695	CMS-0057-F establishes a compliance deadline for implementing and maintaining the Payer-to-Payer FHIR API, meaning the technical capability must be in place and functional.

700	The Road to 2027 and Beyond: Adoption Landscape and Future Trajectory
701	With the January 2027 deadline approaching, the health insurance industry faces a substantial implementation sprint.
702	That would address one of the most persistent operational barriers to payer-to-payer exchange: the need for each requesting payer to negotiate individual trust agreements with each potential responding payer, creating an exponential scaling problem in a market with hundreds of health plans.
703	CMS-0057-F is not the final word on payer-to-payer exchange.
704	The Da Vinci Project continues to evolve the PDex IG, with STU 2.1 (2025) adding draft bulk exchange guidance and STU 2.2 published in August 2026, and future versions expected to address behavioral health data segmentation and enhanced provenance requirements.
705	CMS-9115-F (2020) originally required payer-to-payer data exchange by January 1, 2022, but CMS exercised enforcement discretion in December 2021 because FHIR standards had not yet matured sufficiently for reliable standardized exchange at scale.
706	CMS-0057-F (January 2024) supersedes the earlier framework with a definitive mandate: impacted payers must implement a FHIR R4 Payer-to-Payer API by January 1, 2027, exchanging up to 5 years of claims, USCDI clinical data, and prior authorization history.
707	The technical backbone is the Da Vinci PDex Implementation Guide (current release 2.2.0, August 2026), which defines the $member-match operation for identity resolution, mTLS and OAuth 2.0 for security, and both single-member and bulk asynchronous exchange patterns.

## interop-bulk-fhir
735	Standard FHIR REST APIs are brilliant for the single-patient interaction: a clinician clicks into a chart, the app fires a GET /Patient/{id}/$everything, and within seconds the full record arrives.
736	A payer managing a Medicare Advantage panel of 500,000 members cannot issue half a million sequential HTTP requests to compute a single quality measure.
737	Under CMS rules finalized in 2024, impacted payers must support this exchange via FHIR APIs and must provide data going back five years.
738	The governing standard is the HL7 FHIR Bulk Data Access Implementation Guide, a Standard for Trial Use currently at version 3.0.0 (STU 3, December 2025), published by HL7 International and available at hl7.org/fhir/uv/bulkdata/.
739	The specification builds on top of FHIR R4 and defines three export endpoints that correspond to different scopes of data.
740	The Group/$export endpoint exports all FHIR resources for members of a named Group resource — the most common use case for payer populations and clinical cohorts.
741	The Patient/$export endpoint exports resources for all patients on the server.
742	The System/$export endpoint (sometimes written /$export) exports every resource in the system regardless of patient linkage, useful for operational data migrations.
743	The server immediately returns an HTTP 202 Accepted response — it does not start streaming data yet.
744	Crucially, the 202 response includes a Content-Location header whose value is a polling URL.
745	The client then polls that URL at regular intervals (using exponential back-off as recommended by the specification) until the server returns a 200 OK response with a JSON manifest listing the completed output files.
746	Client sends GET [base]/Group/{id}/$export with header Prefer: respond-async and optional parameters like _type (resource types to include) and _since (date filter).
747	Server responds 202 Accepted with Content-Location header pointing to a status URL.
748	Server returns 202 (still in progress) with optional X-Progress header, or 200 OK (complete) with JSON manifest of output files.
749	The 200 OK body is a JSON object with fields: transactionTime, request (original URL), requiresAccessToken (boolean), output (array of file descriptors), and error (array of error file descriptors for any resources that failed to export).

753	Bulk FHIR moved from a promising technical idea to a federal mandate through two landmark rules.
754	The ONC 21st Century Cures Act Final Rule, published in May 2020, required certified health IT developers to support the HL7 FHIR Bulk Data Access IG (v1.0.0, STU 1) as part of their certification criteria.
755	Specifically, the rule mandated support for the Group/$export operation, enabling population-level data extraction from electronic health records.
756	The compliance deadline for certified EHR systems was December 31, 2022, meaning that since January 2023, any CEHRT-certified system must support bulk export of USCDI data elements for defined patient groups.
757	On the payer side, CMS issued the Interoperability and Patient Access Final Rule (CMS-9115-F) in 2020, requiring Medicare Advantage organizations, Medicaid managed care plans, CHIP agencies, and QHP issuers to implement FHIR-based Patient Access APIs.
758	The subsequent CMS Interoperability and Prior Authorization Final Rule (CMS-0057-F), published February 2024, extended these requirements and introduced mandatory Payer-to-Payer Bulk Data Exchange.
759	Under CMS-0057-F, impacted payers must implement a FHIR-based payer-to-payer API that uses Bulk FHIR to transfer clinical data, claims and encounter data, and prior authorization records for members transitioning between plans.
760	The enforcement deadline for this requirement is January 1, 2027.
761	ONC's FHIR Roadmap for TEFCA Exchange version 2 (January 2024) charts three phases toward full FHIR adoption across Qualified Health Information Networks (QHINs): facilitated FHIR exchange through QHINs (targeted for early 2024), QHIN-to-QHIN FHIR exchange, and end-to-end FHIR exchange.
762	CMS-0057-F set the compliance date for payer-to-payer API requirements at January 1, 2027.
763	Payers subject to CMS-0057-F — including Medicare Advantage organizations, Medicaid managed care plans, and QHP issuers — must have compliant Bulk FHIR APIs operational by that date.
764	CMS-0057-F Deadline: January 1, 2027

766	The most prominent active initiative involving Bulk FHIR in 2024 and 2025 is the NCQA Bulk FHIR Quality Coalition.
767	NCQA has converted the lengthy HEDIS Volume 2 measure specifications into Clinical Quality Language (CQL), enabling automated, consistent computation directly against FHIR data rather than relying on manual chart abstraction, fax-based data collection, or proprietary extracts.
768	A Bulk FHIR pipeline for HEDIS measurement works as follows: a health plan kicks off a Group/$export against a network provider's FHIR server for its enrolled members, receives NDJSON files containing claims (via CARIN Blue Button FHIR IG) and clinical data (via US Core FHIR IG), and runs CQL measure logic against the combined dataset.
769	NCQA aims to transition HEDIS reporting to fully digital measures by 2030, and Bulk FHIR is the foundational data transport layer for that transition.

## interop-query-vs-event
886	Understanding when to reach for each pattern is one of the most important practical skills in health IT.
887	Examples: HL7 v2 ADT, FHIR Subscription, Direct
888	A FHIR R4 server exposes standardized endpoints such as GET /Patient, GET /Condition, GET /MedicationRequest, and GET /Observation.
889	This granularity is a significant upgrade from CDA-based document exchange, where a 200-page continuity of care document might be returned when all a clinician needed was the current medication list.
890	The querying system authenticates to each remote server, typically via SMART on FHIR OAuth 2.0, presenting patient context and purpose-of-use.
891	TEFCA, which went live in December 2023 and had seven designated Qualified Health Information Networks (QHINs) by February 2024, is built primarily around query-based document exchange.
892	By mid-2025 participants could also run FHIR-based queries through TEFCA-trusted FHIR APIs, with QHIN-to-QHIN FHIR exchange the roadmap's next phase of network evolution from document retrieval toward discrete resource query.
893	Event-Based Exchange: HL7 v2 ADT, FHIR Subscriptions, and Direct
894	Event-based exchange has been part of health IT since the earliest days of HL7.
895	HL7 version 2 (v2), first released in 1988 and still running on the majority of hospital interfaces worldwide, is fundamentally an event-driven messaging standard.
896	When something happens in a clinical system — a patient is registered, transferred, or discharged — the system generates a v2 message and pushes it to every downstream system that needs to know.
897	The most common v2 message type for this purpose is the ADT (Admit, Discharge, Transfer) message.
898	ADT A01 signals an admission; ADT A02 signals an intra-facility transfer; ADT A03 signals a discharge.
899	These three message types, along with ADT A04 (registration) and ADT A08 (patient information update), handle the vast majority of care transition notifications in U.S. hospitals today.
900	The CMS Interoperability and Patient Access Final Rule (85 FR 25510), finalized in May 2020 and effective April 30, 2021, codified event-based exchange as a federal Condition of Participation.
901	Under 42 CFR 482.24(d), Medicare and Medicaid participating hospitals, psychiatric hospitals, and critical access hospitals (CAHs) that use an EHR must send electronic patient event notifications upon admission, discharge, and transfer to the patient's established primary care practitioner, the patient's established care team, and post-acute care providers with whom the patient has an established relationship.
902	The rule permits hospitals to transmit notifications using HL7 v2 ADT messages, C-CDA summary care records, or FHIR-based APIs, giving flexibility while mandating the workflow.
903	CMS required hospitals to send ADT event notifications by April 30, 2021.
904	On the modern FHIR side, the R4 Subscription resource introduced a mechanism for clients to register interest in data changes on a FHIR server.
905	However, the original R4 Subscription model — based on criteria strings evaluated against the server's data set — proved difficult to implement consistently across vendors.
906	The FHIR community responded with a significant redesign in R5, introducing SubscriptionTopic resources that define specific event types (such as "patient admitted" or "lab result critical") as canonical, versioned artifacts.
907	Because FHIR R4 remains the dominant version in production systems, HL7 published the Subscriptions R5 Backport Implementation Guide (version 1.2.0-ballot) to extend topic-based subscription semantics to R4 and R4B servers through extensions and profiles, without requiring a full upgrade.
908	The HL7 Da Vinci Project's Unsolicited Notifications Implementation Guide (version 1.1.0, published April 2025) builds directly on the R5 backport to define a standardized FHIR messaging framework for Admit, Discharge, and Transfer notifications.

916	Unlike HL7 ADT, which sends structured event messages to known endpoints registered in an interface engine, Direct allows any credentialed provider to push a C-CDA summary document to any other credentialed provider using a Direct address that looks like a standard email address (provider@hospital.directaddress.net).
917	Meaningful Use Stage 2 required providers to electronically transmit a C-CDA summary of care for more than 10% of transitions and referrals, and Direct became the most common way to do it.
918	In many markets, a single patient discharge triggers both a Direct C-CDA push to the receiving facility's Direct address and an HL7 ADT push to the PCP's care management platform — both event-based mechanisms, but serving different receivers and different purposes.
919	When the receiver is initiating a scheduled care encounter and wants to assemble the most complete picture of a patient's history before the visit — annual wellness, specialist consultation, post-acute admission assessment — query-based exchange is more appropriate.

921	The initial TEFCA launch in 2024 focused on query-based document retrieval — enabling providers to locate and retrieve C-CDA documents across all participating QHINs through a single governance framework.
922	FHIR-based query through TEFCA-trusted FHIR APIs became available by mid-2025; QHIN-to-QHIN FHIR exchange, enabling discrete resource queries at national scale, is the next phase of ONC's FHIR roadmap for TEFCA.
923	An accountable care organization (ACO) managing 15,000 Medicare beneficiaries subscribes to ADT feeds from every hospital within a 50-mile radius.
924	When an attributed patient is admitted, the ACO's care management platform receives an ADT A01 notification within minutes.
925	The platform automatically fires a series of FHIR GET requests — Condition, MedicationRequest, AllergyIntolerance, Observation (recent labs) — against the hospital's FHIR R4 endpoint.
926	A notification sent to an outdated Direct address or decommissioned HL7 endpoint meets the letter of the rule but fails to achieve its clinical purpose.

## interop-ehr-burden
1117	Instead, a mountain of peer-reviewed evidence now documents one of the most consequential unintended consequences in modern medicine: physicians routinely spend more hours each day interacting with their EHR than they spend in face-to-face contact with patients.
1118	A landmark time-motion study by Sinsky and colleagues, published in the Annals of Internal Medicine, found that for every hour physicians spent in direct clinical face time with patients, they spent nearly two additional hours on EHR and desk work.
1119	A 2024 study of 141 academic primary care physicians, published in the Annals of Family Medicine, found that their EHR time per 8 hours of scheduled appointments kept rising after the pandemic — up 28.4 minutes (7.8%) between 2019–20 and 2022–23, driven by order entry (+58.9%) and the inbox (+24.4%).
1120	Overall, physicians today report spending roughly 13 hours per week on indirect patient care tasks such as order entry, documentation, and referral management, within a 57.8-hour reported workweek.
1121	That math leaves precious little time for the human encounter that drew most physicians to medicine in the first place.
1122	A study in the Annals of Family Medicine found that primary care physicians spent approximately 86 minutes — nearly an hour and a half — on the EHR after clinic hours every day.
1123	The AMA's 2024 national burnout survey of nearly 18,000 physicians across 43 states found that 43.2 percent of physicians reported at least one burnout symptom — and ineffective EHR systems ranked among the most consistently cited sources of occupational stress.

1128	One of the most pervasive and dangerous documentation behaviors to emerge from EHR design failures is copy-forward documentation — the practice of copying a prior note into the current encounter and editing only minimally.
1129	A 2022 analysis of over 100 million EHR notes found that 50.1 percent of all text in clinical notes was duplicated from prior documentation on the same patient.
1130	As many as 90 percent of clinicians admit to using copy-and-paste functions in the EHR.

1133	Research published in JMIR Medical Informatics found that in one medication-related CDS system, only 7.3 percent of generated alerts were clinically appropriate.
1134	When physicians override 98% of EHR alerts — as documented at Brigham and Women's Hospital — the few clinically critical warnings become invisible in the noise.
1135	The AMA's 2024 nationwide survey of 1,000 practicing physicians found that practices complete an average of 39 prior authorization requests per physician per week, consuming approximately 13 hours of physician and staff time — roughly one and a half business days — every single week.
1136	The status quo is expensive in human terms: 89 percent of physicians in the AMA's 2024 survey (fielded December 2024) reported that prior authorization somewhat or significantly contributes to physician burnout.
1137	That near-universal figure makes prior authorization one of the most universally agreed-upon sources of professional dissatisfaction in medicine today.

1139	Seventy-eight percent of physicians reported that prior authorization often or sometimes results in patients abandoning a recommended course of treatment entirely — unable or unwilling to wait for approval, they simply go without care.
1140	Most seriously, nearly one in four physicians (24 percent) reported that prior authorization has led to a serious adverse event for a patient in their care, including hospitalization, permanent impairment, or death.

1147	The legislative response began in earnest with the 21st Century Cures Act of 2016, which included sweeping provisions designed to promote the free flow of electronic health information and prohibit "information blocking" — practices by EHR vendors, health systems, or health information networks that impede access to or exchange of patient data.
1148	Penalties for information blocking violations can reach up to $1 million per violation for health IT developers and health information networks.
1149	Enforcement against healthcare providers became effective July 1, 2024, marking a turning point in the federal government's willingness to hold the ecosystem accountable.
1150	The ONC's HTI-1 Final Rule, which took effect March 11, 2024, further implements the EHR Reporting Program from the Cures Act, requiring transparent reporting on certified health IT metrics.
1151	The most targeted federal intervention on prior authorization specifically is the CMS Interoperability and Prior Authorization Final Rule, known as CMS-0057-F.
1152	Finalized in early 2024, the rule requires impacted payers — including Medicaid managed care plans, CHIP managed care entities, and qualified health plan issuers on federal exchanges — to implement FHIR-based Prior Authorization APIs, establish turnaround time limits of 72 hours for expedited requests and 7 calendar days for standard requests, and provide specific reasons for denial decisions.
1153	The operational provisions took effect January 1, 2026, with full API requirements due January 1, 2027.

1160	The most discussed — and arguably the most promising — is ambient artificial intelligence documentation, often called the "ambient AI scribe." These systems use natural language processing to listen to physician-patient conversations and automatically generate structured clinical notes inside the EHR, dramatically reducing the manual typing and clicking that consumes so much of a physician's cognitive bandwidth.
1161	A quality improvement study of 263 physicians and advanced practice practitioners across six health care systems — published in JAMA Network Open — found that after 30 days of using an ambient AI scribe, burnout rates dropped significantly from 51.9 percent to 38.8 percent.
1162	The odds of burnout fell by 74 percent.
1163	Eighty-four percent of physicians reported a positive effect on patient communication, and 82 percent said their overall work satisfaction improved.
1164	Between October 2023 and December 2024, 7,260 Permanente physicians used ambient AI tools across more than 2.5 million patient encounters, saving an estimated 15,791 hours of documentation time — equivalent to approximately 1,800 eight-hour workdays.
1165	A 2026 real-world prospective observational time-motion study published in JMIR Medical Informatics documented significant time savings per clinical encounter using ambient AI tools in outpatient settings.
1166	Fifty-six percent of patients reported that ambient AI scribes had a positive impact on the quality of their visit, suggesting that the benefits extend beyond the clinician experience to the patient relationship itself.
1167	Team-based inbox management, in which clinical staff handle prescription refill requests, routine test result communications, and referral coordination, can reduce physician inbox volume by 30 to 40 percent in well-designed implementations.

## ehr-business-case-roi
1197	The EHR Business Case: Total Cost of Ownership & 5-Year ROI
1198	Choosing and adopting an EHR is one of the largest capital and operational decisions a health system makes — and the business case is notoriously hard to build.
1199	(1) Implementation — configuration, data migration, interface build, and vendor professional services, typically the largest one-time line.
1200	(2) Licensing or subscription — per-provider annual cost, higher for on-premise capex-heavy models, recurring for cloud/SaaS.
1201	(3) Training — scaled to headcount and to how long the rollout runs.
1202	(4) The go-live productivity loss — see below.
1203	In 2017 the University of Vermont Medical Center asked the Green Mountain Care Board for a certificate of need to replace the EHR and related IT systems at four UVM Health Network hospitals — UVM Medical Center, Central Vermont Medical Center, Porter Medical Center and Champlain Valley Physicians Hospital — with a single Epic platform.
1204	Because Vermont subjects hospital capital spending above $3 million to CON review, the whole cost model went on the public record.

## interop-deidentification
1578	Every time a patient visits a clinic, fills a prescription, or receives a laboratory result, that encounter generates data — and that data contains some of the most sensitive details a person can share about themselves.
1579	The Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act of 1996 (HIPAA) defines Protected Health Information, or PHI, as any individually identifiable health information that is created, received, maintained, or transmitted by a covered entity or its business associates.
1580	The 21st Century Cures Act and the CMS Interoperability and Patient Access Rule have accelerated data sharing mandates across payers, providers, and health information networks.

1585	HIPAA's Privacy Rule, codified at 45 CFR § 164.514(b), provides two legally recognized pathways for de-identifying health information.
1586	The first and most commonly used method is Safe Harbor.
1587	Under Safe Harbor, a covered entity must remove all 18 categories of direct identifiers specified in the regulation: (1) names; (2) geographic subdivisions smaller than a state, including street address, city, county, precinct, and ZIP codes, with a narrow exception for three-digit ZIP codes serving populations greater than 20,000; (3) all date elements other than year that are directly related to an individual, plus ages over 89 which must be aggregated into a single "90+" category; (4) telephone numbers; (5) fax numbers; (6) email addresses; (7) Social Security numbers; (8) medical record numbers; (9) health plan beneficiary numbers; (10) account numbers; (11) certificate and license numbers; (12) vehicle identifiers and serial numbers including license plates; (13) device identifiers and serial numbers; (14) web URLs; (15) IP addresses; (16) biometric identifiers including fingerprints and voice prints; (17) full-face photographs and comparable images; and (18) any other unique identifying number, characteristic, or code.
1588	Remove all 18 enumerated identifiers
1589	This distinction matters enormously, and a series of landmark empirical studies has demonstrated just how porous the anonymity of seemingly de-identified data can be when an adversary has access to even modest amounts of auxiliary information.
1590	The most cited demonstration came from computer scientist Latanya Sweeney in 1997.
1591	The state of Massachusetts had released medical encounter data for approximately 135,000 state employees with obvious identifiers — names, addresses, and Social Security numbers — removed.
1592	Her subsequent analysis of the 1990 U.S.
1593	Census showed that 87 percent of the American population could be uniquely identified by ZIP code, date of birth, and sex alone.
1594	A decade later, in 2008, Arvind Narayanan and Vitaly Shmatikov of the University of Texas at Austin published "Robust De-anonymization of Large Sparse Datasets," applying their methodology to the Netflix Prize dataset — a collection of 100 million movie ratings submitted by approximately 500,000 anonymous subscribers that Netflix released for a machine-learning competition.
1595	k-anonymity, introduced by Sweeney in 1998, is a formal privacy model that requires every record in a dataset to be indistinguishable from at least k-1 other records with respect to all quasi-identifier fields.
1596	A dataset with k=5 guarantees that any re-identification attempt narrows the adversary's target to a pool of at least five people.
1597	The most widely used open-source synthetic patient generator in health IT is Synthea, developed by MITRE Corporation.
1598	It generates output in standard formats including HL7 FHIR and C-CDA, and MITRE has made one million synthetic patient records freely available for download.
1599	Between identified data and fully de-identified data sits the Limited Data Set, defined at 45 CFR § 164.514(e).
1600	For research that requires identified data — full medical record numbers, individual diagnoses, and linked longitudinal records — covered entities must obtain patient authorization or qualify for a waiver of authorization approved by an IRB or Privacy Board under 45 CFR § 164.512(i).
1601	PCORnet, the national patient-centered clinical research network, runs distributed queries so that patient data stays at each participating site, while the NIH's National COVID Cohort Collaborative (N3C) took the enclave route — pooling limited data sets into a single secure environment where approved analysts work without downloading records.

1605	Narayanan and Shmatikov's 2008 Netflix Prize study proved that sparse, anonymized datasets can be re-identified using publicly available auxiliary data, even with only partial or noisy background knowledge.
