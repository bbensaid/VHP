
## medicaid-history-origins
0	Medicaid's Origins: 1965 to Today
1	Born in 1965: A Great Society Program
2	Medicaid was created on July 30, 1965, when President Lyndon B.
3	Johnson signed the Social Security Amendments of 1965 (Public Law 89-97).
4	Medicare was designed as a uniform federal program for people 65 and older.
5	Because states administer their own programs, "Medicaid" is really 56 different programs — one for each state, the District of Columbia, and the territories — each with different eligibility rules, covered benefits, and provider payment rates.
6	A key early expansion came in 1972, when Congress created the Supplemental Security Income (SSI) program.
7	A series of laws in the 1980s extended coverage to low-income pregnant women and children regardless of welfare status, transforming Medicaid into a broad health-financing program rather than a welfare appendage.
8	The next major milestone came in 1997, when the Balanced Budget Act created the Children's Health Insurance Program (CHIP), originally known as SCHIP.
9	CHIP was designed to cover children in families that earned too much to qualify for Medicaid but could not afford private insurance — generally children in families below roughly 200 percent of the federal poverty level.
10	CHIP was enacted as part of the Balanced Budget Act of 1997, which provided roughly $40 billion in federal funding through fiscal year 2007 to cover low-income, uninsured children not eligible for Medicaid.
11	The Balanced Budget Act of 1997
12	The ACA and the Biggest Expansion in 50 Years
13	The Affordable Care Act (ACA), signed in 2010, called for the single largest expansion in Medicaid's history.
14	Starting January 1, 2014, the ACA extended Medicaid eligibility to nearly all non-elderly adults with incomes up to 138 percent of the federal poverty level — including, for the first time, low-income adults without dependent children, who had historically been excluded in most states.
15	But in 2012, the Supreme Court changed the plan.
16	Sebelius, decided June 28, 2012, the Court ruled that the federal government could not force states to expand Medicaid by threatening to withhold all of their existing Medicaid funding.

79	In states that adopted the ACA expansion, adults qualify if their MAGI is at or below 138 percent of the federal poverty level (FPL).
80	This single number — 138 percent — is one of the most cited figures in Medicaid policy, but there is a subtle wrinkle behind it.

## medicaid-1115-waivers
206	Section 1115 Waivers: Laboratories of Democracy
207	But the law also contains a powerful escape hatch: Section 1115.
208	In practice, Section 1115 lets a state ask the federal government for permission to run its Medicaid program differently than the statute would otherwise allow — covering populations or services that are not normally eligible, testing new payment models, or restructuring delivery.
209	The breadth of the authority is striking: under Section 1115, the Secretary can waive almost any of the state plan requirements in Section 1902 of the Act to the extent necessary to carry out the demonstration.
210	That is why 1115 waivers can reshape a state's program in ways that look very different from "textbook" Medicaid.
211	A Section 1115 demonstration waiver lets the HHS Secretary approve state Medicaid projects that depart from normal federal rules, as long as they are found likely to promote the objectives of Medicaid.
212	The flexibility of Section 1115 is not unlimited.
213	Budget neutrality is the core guardrail of Section 1115: federal spending under the waiver cannot exceed what it would have been without the waiver, usually measured on a per-enrollee basis.
214	Getting an 1115 waiver is a deliberate, transparent process.
215	Before a state even submits its application to CMS, federal rules require it to provide public notice and hold a state-level public comment period of at least 30 days, publishing the application, objectives, and estimated impact on enrollment and spending.
216	Once the state submits a complete application, CMS opens its own 30-day federal public comment period, posting the application and collecting comments online.

251	Together, the 1915(c) waiver authority and the Olmstead decision drove a dramatic "rebalancing" of Medicaid long-term care away from institutions and toward the community.
252	In 1999, when Olmstead was decided, about 27 percent of Medicaid LTSS spending went to home- and community-based services and roughly 73 percent went to institutional care.
253	By 2020, those proportions had essentially flipped: 62.5 percent of LTSS spending went to HCBS and 37.5 percent to institutions.
254	This reversal represents one of the most significant structural changes in the history of American long-term care — moving from a system that assumed institutional placement to one in which most Medicaid long-term care dollars now support people living in their own homes and communities.
