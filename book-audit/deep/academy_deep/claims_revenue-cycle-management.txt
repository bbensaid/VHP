## ten-steps-revenue-cycle
19	The 10 Steps of the Revenue Cycle
20	A note on numbering: different sources count the revenue cycle in anywhere from 7 to 13 steps, depending on how finely they slice the work.
21	This lesson uses a clean 10-step framework that captures the essential flow.
22	Sources count 7-13 steps; what matters is the flow and the dependencies — early errors propagate downstream and become expensive denials.
23	Steps 1-3: Getting the Patient in the Door (Front-End)
24	Step 1 is scheduling and pre-registration.
25	Step 2 is registration, where that information is confirmed and completed at check-in.
26	Step 3 is eligibility verification and prior authorization.
27	Steps 4-6: Turning Care Into a Bill (Mid-Cycle)
28	Step 4 is clinical documentation: the provider records what was assessed and done during the visit.
29	Step 5 is medical coding, where certified coders translate the documented diagnoses and services into standardized codes (ICD-10 for diagnoses, CPT and HCPCS for procedures and services).
30	Step 6 is charge capture: the coded services are recorded as charges in the billing system, ensuring the provider bills for everything that was actually performed.
31	Translate documentation into ICD-10, CPT, and HCPCS codes.
32	Steps 7-10: Getting Paid (Back-End)
33	Step 7 is claim submission.
34	Step 8 is payment posting and remittance.
35	Step 9 is denial management and appeals: analyzing why claims were denied, correcting and resubmitting them, and appealing when the denial is wrong.
36	Step 10 is accounts receivable follow-up and patient collections: chasing unpaid balances still sitting with insurers (third-party follow-up) and billing patients for whatever portion they owe.
37	The power of viewing RCM as a 10-step chain is that it reveals where revenue actually leaks.
38	A denial at step 9 is almost never really a step-9 problem — it usually traces back to a missed verification at step 3, weak documentation at step 4, or a coding error at step 5.
39	Keep this 10-step map in mind as the backbone; everything else hangs off it.
40	The revenue cycle is a sequential chain from scheduling to final collection; sources count 7-13 steps, but the flow and dependencies matter more than the exact number.

43	Back-end steps (7-10): claim submission, payment posting, denial management and appeals, and AR follow-up plus patient collections.

92	How Verification Works: The 270/271 Transaction
93	The provider sends the payer an electronic eligibility inquiry — a HIPAA "270" transaction — and the payer returns an electronic response, the "271" transaction.

96	The 271 response is rich: it can confirm whether coverage is active and its effective dates, list benefit details by service type, report copay, coinsurance, deductible, and out-of-pocket maximum status, flag whether prior authorization is required, and confirm network participation.

## icd10-cpt-hcpcs
164	ICD-10, CPT, and HCPCS Coding Fundamentals
165	ICD-10 codes answer "why" — the diagnosis or reason for care.
166	Three code sets carry a claim: ICD-10 (the diagnosis — "why"), CPT (the procedure or service — "what"), and HCPCS Level II (supplies, drugs, and equipment — "what else").
167	ICD-10: The Diagnosis Codes
168	The U.S. uses a clinical modification called ICD-10-CM for diagnoses — the codes physicians use to report why a patient was seen, submitted on claims to explain why care was provided.
169	ICD-10-CM in the U.S. is maintained by the CDC's National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS).
170	There is also a separate, distinct code set — ICD-10-PCS — used specifically for inpatient hospital procedures; its codes are seven characters, with each character an "axis" specifying details of the procedure.
171	Do not confuse the two: ICD-10-CM is diagnoses (everywhere), ICD-10-PCS is inpatient procedures (hospitals).
172	The single most important role of ICD-10 codes in billing is establishing medical necessity.
173	7-character codes, each an axis of classification
174	Where ICD-10 codes describe the "why," CPT codes describe the "what." CPT is maintained and copyrighted by the American Medical Association (AMA), whose CPT Editorial Panel decides additions, deletions, and revisions.
175	For example, CPT 99204 is a Level 4 evaluation-and-management visit for a new patient, requiring moderate medical decision-making or 45 minutes of total time.
176	It is also where some of the most consequential errors and compliance risks live — selecting a higher-level code than the documentation supports ("upcoding") or a lower one ("downcoding") — topics the documentation-compliance lesson takes up next.
177	Alphanumeric, up to 7 characters
178	5 characters, numeric, starts with a number
179	The diagnosis (ICD-10) must support the procedure (CPT) it is paired with, demonstrating medical necessity.
180	The diagnosis (ICD-10) must justify the procedure (CPT), and supplies/drugs (HCPCS) must fit both.
181	ICD-10-CM codes report the diagnosis ("why"), are maintained by the CDC/NCHS, and establish medical necessity for procedures.
182	ICD-10-PCS is a separate 7-character code set for inpatient hospital procedures — distinct from ICD-10-CM diagnoses and from CPT.
183	CPT codes describe the procedure or service ("what"), are maintained by the AMA, are five characters starting with a number, and include E/M codes like 99204.
184	CPT codes describe what was done and largely drive reimbursement; ICD-10-CM diagnosis codes describe why and establish medical necessity.
185	Payers require that a procedure be justified by an appropriate diagnosis — a CPT code not supported by a relevant ICD-10 code is denied as not medically necessary.
186	On a claim, what is the primary billing role of the ICD-10-CM diagnosis code relative to the CPT procedure code?

## charge-capture
193	Missed charges are one of the most common and most invisible forms of revenue loss, because nothing alerts you to a charge that was never entered.
194	Certified coders assign ICD-10, CPT, and HCPCS codes.
195	This makes missed charges one of the most insidious revenue leaks.
196	The Healthcare Financial Management Association reports that up to 1 percent of a health system's net charges are lost to charge-capture leakage, and broader estimates put annual losses in the range of 1 to 3 percent of net revenue.
197	One percent may sound small until you apply it to a large organization's revenue — for a health system, it can mean millions of dollars vanishing every year, invisibly, into uncaptured charges.
198	Organizations that implement robust charge-capture systems commonly recover a meaningful share of previously lost revenue in the first year — money that was always earned but never collected.
199	When supplies and implants used in the case are not captured from the usage log onto the claim, the organization loses real money on high-cost items — which is why supply-heavy departments get the most intensive charge reconciliation and audit attention.
