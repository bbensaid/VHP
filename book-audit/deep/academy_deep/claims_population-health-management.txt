
## what-is-population-health
0	The foundational definition comes from a 2003 paper by David Kindig and Greg Stoddart in the American Journal of Public Health.
1	Population health (Kindig & Stoddart, 2003) is "the health outcomes of a group of individuals, including the distribution of such outcomes within the group." The unit of concern is the group, and the focus on distribution makes equity intrinsic.
2	This is the most important and counterintuitive idea in population health: most of what determines a group's health happens outside the doctor's office.
3	In 2008, Don Berwick, Tom Nolan, and John Whittington of the Institute for Healthcare Improvement (IHI) described three goals to pursue simultaneously: improving the patient experience of care, improving the health of populations, and reducing the per capita cost of care.
4	The biggest mental shift in population health is attending to the whole defined panel — especially the high-risk patients who are not coming in for care.
5	Population health (Kindig & Stoddart, 2003) is the health outcomes of a group AND the distribution of those outcomes — making equity intrinsic to the concept.
6	The Triple Aim (Berwick et al., IHI, 2008) made population health a co-equal goal alongside patient experience and per capita cost.

## determinants-of-health
15	The most widely cited framework for the determinants of health is the County Health Rankings model, developed by the University of Wisconsin Population Health Institute with the Robert Wood Johnson Foundation.
16	The model's weights are: social and economic factors about 40 percent, health behaviors about 30 percent, clinical care about 20 percent, and the physical environment about 10 percent.
17	The popular shorthand is "80/20" — roughly 80 percent of health outcomes are driven by factors other than medical care, and only about 20 percent by clinical care itself.
18	The federal Healthy People 2030 framework organizes them into five place-based domains, which give a practical map of where to look.
19	The Five SDOH Domains (Healthy People 2030)
20	The tool for this is a specific set of ICD-10 diagnosis codes, the "Z codes" in the range Z55 to Z65, which document social factors like housing instability, food insecurity, and lack of transportation.
21	Studies have found SDOH Z codes recorded on only around 1.9 percent of hospital admissions.
22	The social needs exist; they are simply not being captured in coded form, which means population health programs are often flying blind on the very factors that drive the most outcomes.

## risk-stratification-models
54	Risk stratification is the discipline of sorting a population by risk so that effort and resources go where they will do the most good.
55	In the United States, roughly 5 percent of the population accounts for nearly half of all health expenditures, and about 20 percent accounts for 80 percent of total spending.
56	Adults with five or more chronic conditions spend about 14 times more on health care than adults with none, and make up 12 percent of adults but 41 percent of spending (RAND, 2017).
57	You do not have to fix everyone's cost; if you can keep the highest-risk 5 percent healthier and out of the hospital, you move the majority of the spending.
58	Risk stratification is what lets you find that 5 percent — and the "rising risk" patients who are about to join them.
59	Top 5% of population ~ nearly 50% of expenditures (US data)
60	Top 20% ~ 80% of total health spending (US data)
61	Spending for adults with 5+ chronic conditions vs none (RAND, 2017)
62	The most prominent in U.S. value-based care is the Hierarchical Condition Categories (HCC) model, which calculates a Risk Adjustment Factor (RAF) score from a patient's documented diagnoses; a higher RAF means a more complex, higher-cost patient.
63	Other validated tools include the Adjusted Clinical Groups (ACG) system (which often shows the strongest statistical performance for predicting utilization), the Charlson Comorbidity Index (a weighted count of conditions predicting mortality and future use), and condition-specific tools like the LACE score for post-discharge readmission risk (Length of stay, Acuity, Comorbidities, ED visits; a score of 10 or higher flags high readmission risk).
64	Readmission prevention (score >=10 = high)
65	4-factor post-discharge readmission risk
66	Healthcare spending is extremely concentrated: ~5% of people drive nearly half of spending, ~20% drive 80%, and adults with 5+ chronic conditions spend ~14x those with none (RAND).
67	The "rising risk" tier offers the biggest opportunity to change trajectory and avoid costly progression, so smart programs invest there, not only at the apex.

## predictive-analytics-population-health
75	Here is the most important and frequently missed idea in predictive population health: predicting who is high-risk is NOT the same as predicting who you can actually help.
76	Predictive models are only as fair as their design, and the most famous lesson in healthcare AI is a population health algorithm gone wrong.
77	In a 2019 study published in Science, Ziad Obermeyer and colleagues dissected a widely used commercial algorithm — affecting millions of patients — that helped decide who got extra care-management resources.
78	Researchers calculated that fixing the proxy would have raised the share of Black patients flagged for additional help from about 17.7 percent to 46.5 percent.
79	Reformulating the model to predict actual health need (e.g., active chronic conditions) instead of cost would have increased the proportion of Black patients receiving extra help from ~17.7% to ~46.5% — a stark demonstration that proxy choice is an equity decision.
80	(2019): Cost as a Biased Proxy
81	The most effective programs are built so that clinical reasoning informs how models are constructed, and the models in turn inform care strategy — a two-way loop, not a static tool bolted onto a workflow.

## heart-failure-hypertension-management
126	About 120 million U.S. adults — roughly 48 percent — have high blood pressure.
127	Yet control is poor: only about 1 in 4 adults with hypertension have it controlled.
128	Heart failure is less common but far more acute and expensive: about 6.2 million U.S. adults have it, at an estimated national cost of $30.7 billion a year.
129	Its defining population health challenge is the hospital readmission — heart-failure patients have roughly a 25 percent 30-day readmission rate nationally, and each readmission is costly and often preventable.
130	Hypertension (~120M U.S. adults, only ~1 in 4 controlled) is a silent chronic-control problem; heart failure (~6.2M, ~$30.7B/year, ~25% 30-day readmission) is an acute decompensation-and-readmission problem.
131	The most effective hypertension programs share two features.
132	The first is self-measured blood pressure (SMBP) monitoring — having patients measure their own pressure at home and report it, which gives far more data than occasional office readings and engages patients in their own control.
133	In a 2024 retrospective study of 6,595 patients with uncontrolled hypertension enrolled in a remote monitoring program (cellular-connected home cuffs plus registered-nurse care navigators), the share still uncontrolled fell from 66 percent to 40 percent after an average of about nine and a half months.
134	In a remote monitoring program studied in 2024 (Smith et al., Healthcare), patients with uncontrolled hypertension used cellular-connected blood-pressure cuffs that transmitted home readings, while registered-nurse care navigators reviewed the data and coordinated medication changes with clinicians between visits.
135	Among 6,595 patients uncontrolled at baseline, the share still uncontrolled fell from 66.3% to 40.2% after an average of 289 days — by combining home monitoring, team-based follow-up, and continuous data rather than relying on sporadic office visits.
136	Heart-failure patients decompensate — fluid builds up, symptoms worsen — and end up back in the hospital, often within 30 days of a prior discharge.
137	With a national 30-day readmission rate around 25 percent and each readmission costing many thousands of dollars, preventing readmissions is where the clinical and financial stakes concentrate.
138	In 2024 UMass Memorial Health–Harrington Hospital reported that its remote monitoring program cut 30-day heart-failure readmissions by 50 percent, though no peer-reviewed study accompanied the announcement, and results vary across programs.

## behavioral-health-integration-population
153	The gold-standard approach to behavioral health integration is the Collaborative Care Model (CoCM), developed at the University of Washington in the 1990s.
154	It is, by a wide margin, the most evidence-backed integration model: more than 90 randomized controlled trials, beginning with the landmark IMPACT trial published in 2002, have shown it more effective than usual care for depression, anxiety, and other conditions.
155	Rather than treating depression by impression, CoCM uses a validated, quantitative measure to track each patient and treat toward a specific goal, just as a diabetes program treats toward an A1c target.
156	The standard tool for depression is the PHQ-9, a nine-item questionnaire scored 0 to 27.
157	The care manager administers it at regular intervals, and the team treats "to target" — for example, a 50 percent reduction in the PHQ-9 score (response) and ultimately a score below 5 (remission).
158	The behavioral health care manager administers the PHQ-9 at baseline (score 18, moderately severe) and re-measures every few weeks.
159	The team tracks the PHQ-9 down toward the target (at least a 50% drop, aiming for remission below 5) — actively escalating rather than waiting, which is exactly why measurement-based collaborative care beats usual care.
160	PHQ-9 Treat-to-Target in Action
161	The CoCM codes — CPT 99492 (initial month, ~70 minutes), 99493 (subsequent month), and 99494 (additional time), plus G2214 — let a practice bill for the time the behavioral health care manager and psychiatric consultant spend managing a patient in coordination with the billing provider.
162	A real-world note: a single complex patient — say, with type 2 diabetes, hypertension, and depression — may be served by several of these programs at once (collaborative care for the depression, chronic care management and remote monitoring for the physical conditions), all coordinated in one care plan.
163	The Collaborative Care Model (CoCM), developed at UW with 90+ RCTs (starting with IMPACT, 2002), is the most evidence-backed integration model and beats usual care.
164	Measurement-based care is central: validated tools like the PHQ-9 track patients and the team treats "to target" (e.g., a 50% PHQ-9 reduction, aiming for remission below 5), escalating when patients don't improve.
165	CMS billing codes (99492/99493/99494, G2214) reimburse the team-based, between-visit CoCM work, making integration financially sustainable.

## care-manager-roles-models
172	The first half of this course built the analytic machinery of population health — data, risk stratification, prediction, care gaps, and disease-specific programs.
173	The most common is the registered nurse (RN) care manager, who handles the clinical work — medication management, disease education, symptom monitoring, coordinating with physicians.
174	Medicare reimburses it: CPT 99490 covers 20 minutes of clinical staff time per month for care planning and oversight.
