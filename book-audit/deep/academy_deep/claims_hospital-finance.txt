
## hospital-business-model
0	Net patient revenue — the money a hospital actually collects for treating patients, after discounts and adjustments — accounts for an average of roughly 90.9% of total hospital revenue.
1	It is the engine of the hospital business model — averaging about 90.9% of total hospital revenue.
2	Total compensation and related expenses account for roughly 56–60% of total hospital costs — the largest single expense category (Kaufman Hall / AHA)
3	A hospital’s payer mix — the proportion of its revenue coming from each type of payer — is therefore one of the most consequential numbers in its finances.
4	In Definitive Healthcare's 2024 analysis of more than 5,700 hospitals, commercial, private, self-pay, and other payers account for about 70.7% of net patient revenue, Medicare about 14.4%, and Medicaid about 14.8% — even though, by the AHA's count, Medicare and Medicaid pay for more than 70% of hospital inpatient days.
5	In 2023, Medicare paid hospitals roughly 83 cents for every dollar of cost, and Medicare and Medicaid underpayments together totaled about $130 billion, according to the AHA.
6	Largest share of net patient revenue (~70.7% with self-pay and other)
7	Medicare ~14.4%, Medicaid ~14.8% of revenue, but over 70% of inpatient days
8	Medicare paid ~83¢ per dollar of cost in 2023
9	This dynamic produces one of the most important and least understood phenomena in hospital finance: the cross-subsidy.
10	Total compensation and related expenses account for roughly 56% to 60% of total hospital costs.
11	In 2024, hospitals saw a meaningful financial recovery — Kaufman Hall's national operating margin index, a median across U.S. hospitals, reached about 5% for the calendar year, and Fitch noted nonprofit margins flipped positive (a median around 1.2% for early-fiscal-year reporters, up from -0.5% the prior year).
12	Even so, a 1–5% operating margin is razor-thin for an organization with massive fixed labor and facility costs.
13	In Q2 2024, HCA posted roughly a 12.8% operating margin and Tenet roughly 14.9% — multiples of the nonprofit median.
14	They are exempt from federal income, state, and local property taxes, an exemption KFF valued at about $28.1 billion in 2020 ($14.4 billion federal and $13.7 billion state and local) in foregone revenue.
15	In exchange, they must satisfy the community benefit standard: under IRS Revenue Ruling 69-545 and the Affordable Care Act’s IRC Section 501(r), a nonprofit hospital must demonstrate that it benefits the community rather than private individuals — through charity care, unreimbursed Medicaid, community health programs, education, research, and subsidized services.
16	The federal revenue loss from the exemption is projected at roughly $260 billion over 2025–2034.
17	Net patient revenue — money collected for patient care after discounts and adjustments — averages about 90.9% of total hospital revenue, making the hospital fundamentally a patient-care business supplemented by investment income and philanthropy.
18	A hospital has many payers paying different prices for the same service; by net patient revenue, payer mix is roughly 70.7% commercial/private/self-pay/other, 14.4% Medicare, and 14.8% Medicaid (Definitive Healthcare, 2024), though Medicare and Medicaid pay for over 70% of inpatient days.
19	Government payers often pay below cost — Medicare paid roughly 83 cents per dollar of cost in 2023, and Medicare plus Medicaid underpayments reached about $130 billion — forcing hospitals to rely on a cross-subsidy from higher commercial payments.
20	Expenses are dominated by labor: total compensation accounts for roughly 56–60% of hospital costs and is largely fixed in the short term, making margins highly sensitive to volume and payer mix.
21	Operating margins are typically thin; even in the recovered year of 2024, medians ran from about 1.2% for nonprofits (Fitch early reporters) to about 5% across all hospitals (Kaufman Hall), versus 12–15% for large for-profit systems like HCA and Tenet.

## reading-hospital-financial-statements
31	The most important structural feature of a hospital income statement is the split between operating and non-operating items.
32	Salaries and benefits (the largest line), supplies, drugs, depreciation, and overhead.
33	Following FASB ASU 2016-14 (effective 2018), net assets are reported in two categories: net assets without donor restrictions (formerly "unrestricted") and net assets with donor restrictions (formerly "restricted").
34	Imagine two hospitals that both report a 3% operating margin this year — identical on the income statement.
35	Cash from operating activities shows whether day-to-day care generates real cash — the most important section for judging sustainability.
36	The First Numbers an Analyst Looks For
37	The most famous is days cash on hand (DCOH) — how many days the hospital could keep operating using only its available cash if all revenue stopped.
38	Strong liquidity is generally considered to be roughly 150–205 days of cash on hand, with the exact target varying by hospital size, location, and ownership.
39	A hospital with 200 days of cash has a deep cushion; one with 30 days is in danger.
40	Alongside DCOH, analysts check the current ratio (current assets divided by current liabilities; 1.0 or higher means short-term obligations are covered) and days in accounts receivable (how long, on average, it takes to collect payment — where a rising number means cash is stuck in unpaid claims).
41	The balance sheet rests on assets = liabilities + net assets; for nonprofits, FASB ASU 2016-14 splits net assets into "without donor restrictions" (formerly unrestricted) and "with donor restrictions" (formerly restricted).
42	Days cash on hand (DCOH) measures how long a hospital could operate on existing cash; strong levels run roughly 150–205 days, drawing on both the balance sheet and income statement.

## hospital-financial-ratios
50	A hospital reporting $50 million in profit sounds healthy — until you learn it has $5 billion in revenue, making that "huge" profit a razor-thin 1% margin.
51	Financial ratios solve this by expressing each number as a relationship — profit relative to revenue, cash relative to expenses, debt relative to capital — so that a 25-bed rural hospital and a 1,200-bed academic system can be compared on the same scale.
52	Recent national hospital operating-margin medians ran roughly 1–2% in 2024–2025 — profitable but very thin (HFMA / Fitch)
53	Median days cash on hand ran roughly 215–220 days in fiscal 2024, a favorable liquidity cushion versus history (Fitch)
54	Recent medians put hospital operating margins around 1–2%, and Fitch reported its rated-credit median operating margin rising from roughly 0.2% in 2022 to about 1.1% in 2024 and 1.5% in 2025.
55	A 1–2% operating margin means that a modest shift in payer mix, a wage spike, or a soft quarter of volume can tip a hospital from surplus to loss.
56	The most watched is days cash on hand (DCOH) — how many days the hospital could keep operating on existing cash if revenue stopped — calculated from cash and investments divided by average daily operating expenses (excluding depreciation).
57	Recent medians around 215–220 days represent a strong cushion; a hospital below 50 days is in genuine danger.
58	The current ratio (current assets divided by current liabilities) tests whether short-term obligations are covered; a value of 1.0 or higher means current assets are sufficient to pay current liabilities.
59	Strong is roughly 150–220+ days.
60	Are short-term assets enough to cover short-term debts? 1.0 or higher is the floor.
61	Recent medians around 170–178% indicate flexibility.
62	Debt service coverage measures whether the hospital generates enough cash to cover its debt payments (principal, interest, and leases); a ratio below 1.0 means it is not generating enough to meet obligations — a serious red flag for lenders.
63	A strong analyst reads the set: an operating margin around 1–2%, days cash on hand near 200, cash-to-debt above 170%, debt service coverage comfortably above 1.0, and an average age of plant that is not creeping upward.
64	A current ratio of 1.5 means little until you know peers run at 2.0.
65	A days-cash-on-hand figure of 120 is reassuring if it rose from 80, and alarming if it fell from 200.
66	Recent operating-margin medians run roughly 1–2% (Fitch rated-credit median ~1.1% in 2024 and ~1.5% in 2025), leaving little cushion against shocks.
67	Key liquidity ratios are days cash on hand (recent medians ~215–220 days), the current ratio (≥1.0 floor), days in accounts receivable (lower is better), and cash-to-debt (~170–178%).
68	Capital structure ratios — equity financing and debt service coverage (a ratio below 1.0 means the hospital cannot cover its debt payments) — measure financing sustainability and borrowing capacity.

## payer-mix-revenue
75	The Most Important Number You’ve Never Heard Of
76	This lesson unpacks how payer mix works, why the gap between payers is so large, how it produces the cross-subsidy that holds American hospital finance together, and why a single policy change — like the long-scheduled Medicaid DSH cuts — can threaten the survival of the hospitals serving the most vulnerable communities.
77	In 2022, private insurers paid hospitals on average 254% of what Medicare would pay for the same services — up from 224% in 2020 (RAND, 2024)
78	MedPAC put hospitals' overall fee-for-service Medicare margin at -13% in 2023 and projects about -13% for 2025 — paid below the cost of care (MedPAC, March 2025)
79	Medicare FFS Margin (2023)
80	The most revealing fact about payer mix is that patient volume and revenue do not line up.
81	Medicare and Medicaid together accounted for about two-thirds (67%) of all hospital discharges in 2023 — Medicare nearly half (46%) and Medicaid more than a fifth (21%) — while private insurance covered just 24% of discharges and self-pay 4%.
82	Yet when you look at revenue, the picture inverts: commercial, private, self-pay, and other payers together generate roughly 70.7% of hospital net patient revenue, while Medicare contributes about 14.4% and Medicaid about 14.8% (Definitive Healthcare, 2024).
83	Medicare ~46% of discharges
84	Medicaid ~21% of discharges
85	Private insurance ~24% of discharges
86	Self-pay ~4% of discharges
87	Government payers fill ~67% of beds
88	Commercial/private/self-pay/other ~70.7%
89	Government payers provide <50% of revenue
90	The RAND Corporation, analyzing billions of dollars in claims across thousands of hospitals, found that in 2022 employers and private insurers paid on average 254% of what Medicare would have paid for the same services at the same facilities — a figure that has climbed from 224% in 2020 and 247% in 2018.
91	In some states, commercial prices averaged above 300% of Medicare.
92	Meanwhile, MedPAC put hospitals' fee-for-service Medicare margin at -13% in 2023, and Medicaid often pays even less.
93	Inpatient hospital prices paid by private insurers rose about 50% from mid-2014 to late 2024 — twice the 25% growth in Medicare inpatient prices and more than twice the 19% growth in Medicaid prices over the same span.
94	Medicaid DSH payment cuts, enacted in the ACA and delayed repeatedly for more than a decade, were scheduled to take effect on October 1, 2025, at $8 billion a year for FY 2026–2028.
95	Congress delayed them again in a November 2025 continuing resolution, and the funding package signed on February 3, 2026 eliminated the FY 2026 and FY 2027 reductions — leaving one year, about $8 billion, still scheduled for FY 2028.
96	Volume and revenue diverge sharply: Medicare and Medicaid filled about 67% of discharges in 2023 but provided under half of revenue, while commercial/private/self-pay generated roughly 70.7% of net patient revenue from only ~28% of discharges.
97	Government payers reimburse below cost (MedPAC put hospitals' Medicare fee-for-service margin at -13% in 2023), while commercial payers pay well above it — RAND found private plans paid 254% of Medicare rates in 2022.
98	The payment gap is widening: private inpatient prices rose ~50% from 2014–2024 versus 25% for Medicare and 19% for Medicaid, deepening the cross-subsidy in which commercial payments cover below-cost government care.
99	Medicaid DSH cuts of $8 billion a year were scheduled to begin October 1, 2025; after short-term delays, a February 2026 funding law eliminated the FY 2026 and FY 2027 cuts, leaving about $8 billion scheduled for FY 2028 — just as tightened eligibility threatens to raise uncompensated care.

## chargemaster-gross-net-revenue
106	The $5,000 MRI That Nobody Pays $5,000 For
107	A hospital lists an MRI at $5,000.
108	A commercial insurer pays $1,200 for it.
109	Medicare pays perhaps $500.
110	A cash-paying patient might be quoted $3,200.
111	The uninsured patient who never negotiates gets billed the full $5,000.
112	The average hospital’s charges are about 3.4 times the actual cost of providing care, with some exceeding a 10x charge-to-cost ratio (Health Affairs / NIH studies)
113	Hospitals’ chargemaster list prices are on average about 164% higher than negotiated prices for the same services (Health Affairs)
114	Using the earlier example: a $5,000 chargemaster MRI with an insurer’s $1,200 negotiated rate generates a $3,800 contractual allowance and just $1,200 in net revenue.
115	The $5,000 gross charge is essentially a fiction; the $1,200 is the financial reality.
116	The full chargemaster list price for every item billed — e.g., $5,000 for an MRI.
117	What the hospital actually expects to collect — the only revenue figure that reflects financial reality.
118	Between 1996 and 2017, chargemaster markups increased by an average of 155% across more than 3,400 acute-care hospitals.
119	The average hospital’s charges run about 3.4 times the actual cost of care, and the average charge-to-cost ratio across patient-care departments has been measured anywhere from 1.8 to 28.5, with for-profit hospitals carrying notably higher ratios than government hospitals.
120	Researchers have found that both cash prices and commercial negotiated rates are typically set as a consistent percentage discount off the chargemaster — cash prices averaging around 64% and commercial negotiated rates around 58% of chargemaster prices — which is why the inflated list price still influences what real payers pay.
121	The sticker says $50,000; the dealer expects to negotiate down to $38,000; a savvy buyer pays $35,000; and only the customer who walks in and pays the sticker without haggling gets soaked.
122	Starting in July 2024, hospitals must use a standardized CMS template in a non-proprietary format (CSV or JSON), making the data far more usable.
123	Enforcement carries teeth: CMS can levy fines of up to $5,500 per day for non-compliance at hospitals with 30 or more beds.
124	As of late 2024, roughly 70% of hospitals were substantially compliant with all five required data elements, about 15% posted partial files (often missing payer-specific negotiated rates), and about 15% remained non-compliant.
125	A 2024 HHS Office of Inspector General audit of 100 hospitals found 37 failed to meet key price-transparency requirements, with 34 not complying with the machine-readable file rules — yet by September 2024 CMS had issued only 14 civil monetary penalties nationwide, totaling about $4 million.
126	In February 2025, a federal executive order directed agencies to ramp up enforcement of price transparency within 90 days, signaling that the era of lax enforcement may be ending.
127	Transparency Meets Enforcement (2024–2025)
128	Chargemaster prices are extremely inflated — averaging about 3.4 times actual cost and roughly 164% above negotiated prices — because of a self-reinforcing cycle of list-price inflation and insurer discounting, not because of cost.
129	Federal price transparency rules now require hospitals to publish five standard charges (gross, cash, payer-specific negotiated, and de-identified min/max) in a standardized CMS machine-readable template (CSV/JSON) as of July 2024.
130	Enforcement is tightening: fines reach up to $5,500/day for larger non-compliant hospitals; ~70% were substantially compliant by late 2024, and a February 2025 executive order directed stronger enforcement.

## contractual-adjustments-charity
137	Confusing these three is one of the most common errors in hospital finance, and it leads to badly wrong conclusions.
138	U.S. hospitals provided almost $745 billion in uncompensated care between 2000 and 2020 (American Hospital Association)
139	Uncompensated Care, 2000–2020
140	Charity care plus bad debt, measured at cost, in a single year (American Hospital Association, 2022 fact sheet)
141	Uncompensated Care in 2020
142	If a hospital bills $5,000 for a procedure and the insurer’s contract says it will pay $1,200, the $3,800 difference is a contractual adjustment.
143	By far the largest portion of the gap between gross and net revenue is contractual adjustments — and it represents a planned discount, not a loss or a charitable act.
144	The same unpaid $2,000 hospital bill is charity care if the patient qualified for and received financial assistance, but bad debt if the patient simply never paid and never applied.
145	Why the Distinction Has Legal Teeth: Section 501(r)
146	Under Internal Revenue Code Section 501(r), enacted as part of the Affordable Care Act, tax-exempt hospitals must establish a written financial assistance policy (FAP) and a separate written policy for emergency medical care.
147	Section 501(r) goes further, imposing concrete patient protections.
148	The dollars written off are similar, but the meaning is opposite: one is a failure of process that harms a vulnerable patient and inflates bad debt; the other fulfills the hospital’s charitable mission and its 501(r) obligations.
149	Uncompensated care has been an enormous and growing burden: U.S. hospitals provided almost $745 billion in uncompensated care between 2000 and 2020, including $42.7 billion in 2020 alone (AHA).
150	And it moves with coverage: when pandemic-era Medicaid continuous-enrollment protections unwound in 2023 and more patients lost insurance, hospitals faced more uninsured patients and more unpaid bills.
151	Rural and safety-net hospitals, which serve disproportionate numbers of uninsured and low-income patients, absorb the most uncompensated care while having the fewest commercial patients to offset it.
152	When you see that a hospital "wrote off" a large sum, the first question is always: which kind of write-off?
153	IRC Section 501(r) requires tax-exempt hospitals to maintain a written financial assistance policy (FAP), limit charges to FAP-eligible patients, and restrict extraordinary collection actions until they determine assistance eligibility.
154	Screening patients for assistance ("presumptive eligibility") can reclassify bad debt as charity care — fulfilling the charitable mission and 501(r) obligations rather than subjecting vulnerable patients to aggressive collections.
155	Uncompensated care is large and rising — almost $745 billion from 2000–2020, and $42.7 billion in 2020 alone — and it falls hardest on rural and safety-net hospitals.

## direct-indirect-costs
162	Direct-versus-indirect is not the only way costs are classified.
163	Fixed costs stay roughly constant regardless of volume over the short term — the mortgage on the building, the depreciation on the MRI machine, and much of the administrative salary base do not change whether the hospital treats 100 or 1,000 patients this month.
164	The most common allocation technique is the step-down method, which distributes overhead sequentially — from the higher-level cost centers down to lower ones in a ranked order — so that, for example, administration’s costs are spread first, then housekeeping’s, and so on, until all overhead lands on the revenue-producing departments.

## health-system-integration
309	Nearly 70% of U.S. hospitals are affiliated with a health system, and the trend toward consolidation continues.
310	This lesson examines the financial logic behind that transformation — the pursuit of "systemness" — and the uncomfortable gap between what consolidation promises and what the evidence shows it delivers.
311	These are real potential advantages, and they explain why consolidation has been one of the most powerful forces reshaping hospital finance.
