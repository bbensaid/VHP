8	Parts A and B existed from the start in 1965 and together are called "Original" or "Traditional" Medicare.
9	Part C (Medicare Advantage) was created by the Balanced Budget Act of 1997 — originally called Medicare+Choice — letting beneficiaries get their benefits through private plans.
10	Part D, the outpatient prescription drug benefit, was created by the Medicare Modernization Act of 2003 and took effect on January 1, 2006.
11	Most beneficiaries pay no Part A premium because they (or a spouse) paid Medicare payroll taxes for at least 40 quarters — ten years — of work.
12	Medicare eligibility is not purely about turning 65.
13	U.S. citizens and lawful permanent residents who have worked 40 quarters (ten years) in Medicare-covered employment are entitled to premium-free Part A at 65 and can enroll in Part B for a monthly premium.
14	People who reach 65 without enough work quarters can still buy into Part A by paying a premium.
15	The disability pathway covers people under 65 who have received Social Security Disability Insurance (SSDI) for 24 months; they are then automatically enrolled in Parts A and B.
16	Two conditions bypass the standard 24-month wait: people with End-Stage Renal Disease (ESRD) generally qualify about three months after starting regular dialysis or after a kidney transplant, and people with ALS (amyotrophic lateral sclerosis) qualify immediately upon receiving SSDI.

21	About 12 million Americans are "dual eligibles" enrolled in both at once — Medicare pays first, Medicaid fills gaps.
22	Medicare is one of the largest payers in the world.

24	Medicare made up roughly 13.5 percent of the federal budget in 2024, Medicare benefit payments are estimated at about $1.1 trillion in 2025, and Medicare accounted for about 21 percent of all national health expenditures in 2023.

## post-acute-care-payment
158	This is post-acute care (PAC), and it is one of the most consequential and most scrutinized areas of Medicare spending.
159	Each post-acute setting has its own prospective payment system, all descended from the same 1983 IPPS logic: pay a predetermined amount per episode or period rather than per service.
160	For two decades that rate was set by the Resource Utilization Groups system — most recently version IV (RUG-IV, from 2010) — which based payment primarily on the volume of therapy minutes a patient received — more therapy meant more money, largely independent of the patient’s actual clinical needs.
161	On October 1, 2019, CMS replaced RUG-IV with the Patient-Driven Payment Model (PDPM).
162	PDPM ties payment to clinically relevant patient characteristics — using ICD-10 diagnoses to sort residents into one of ten clinical categories — rather than to the sheer quantity of therapy provided.
163	ICD-10 diagnosis sorts patients into clinical categories
164	Home Health: From 60-Day Episodes to PDGM
165	Home health agencies were historically paid for 60-day episodes of care, with payment heavily influenced by therapy thresholds — the more therapy visits delivered, the higher the payment tier.
166	The Bipartisan Budget Act of 2018 mandated reform, and on January 1, 2020, CMS implemented the Patient-Driven Groupings Model (PDGM).
167	PDGM made two major changes: it cut the unit of payment from a 60-day episode to a 30-day period, and it eliminated therapy thresholds entirely, shifting payment toward clinical characteristics drawn from the OASIS assessment and claims data.
168	PDGM classifies each 30-day period into one of 432 case-mix groups (home health resource groups).
169	It also refined the Low-Utilization Payment Adjustment (LUPA): instead of a single visit threshold, each of the 432 groups has its own threshold below which the period is paid per-visit rather than as a full case.
170	Payment unit shrank from a 60-day episode to a 30-day period of care.

## bundled-payments-and-innovation
301	Instead of paying separately for the surgeon, the hospital stay, the rehab facility, and every follow-up visit, a bundle holds providers accountable for the total cost of a defined clinical episode — for example, a hip replacement and everything that happens in the 90 days after it.
302	A bundled payment sets a single target price for a whole episode of care (often 90 days).
303	It was created by Section 3021 of the Affordable Care Act in 2010 with a specific mission — to test new payment and delivery models that could reduce spending while preserving or improving quality.
304	Bundled Payments for Care Improvement Advanced (BPCI Advanced), launched in 2018, let participants take accountability for 90-day episodes across a menu of medical and surgical conditions, with target prices based partly on their own historical spending.
305	It was the first episode model to include an outpatient surgical condition (spine surgery).
306	The clearest test of bundled payment was the Comprehensive Care for Joint Replacement (CJR) model, which ran from April 2016 through December 2024.
307	A rigorous evaluation found CJR reduced institutional spending per joint-replacement episode by roughly $812 (about a 3 percent relative decrease), driven largely by a meaningful drop in patients being discharged to costly post-acute facilities instead of home.
308	The lessons from CJR fed directly into its successor: the Transforming Episode Accountability Model (TEAM), a mandatory episode-based payment model that launched in January 2026.
309	In roughly 14 years it has launched more than 50 models, but a 2023 Congressional Budget Office analysis found that only six of the 49 models it examined produced statistically significant savings, and that over 2011–2020 CMMI increased federal direct spending by about $5.4 billion — roughly 0.1 percent of net Medicare spending — once its operating costs were counted.
310	To date, only the Diabetes Prevention Program and the Pioneer ACO model met the statutory criteria, and even the Pioneer ACO model was never fully folded into permanent Medicare.
311	A bundled payment sets a single target price for an entire episode of care (often 90 days), reconciling fee-for-service spending against that target at the end.
312	The CMS Innovation Center (CMMI), created by ACA Section 3021 in 2010, tests payment models and can expand successful ones nationally without new legislation.
313	BPCI Advanced (2018) used voluntary 90-day episodes across many conditions; CJR (2016-2024) tested mandatory bundles for hip and knee replacement.
314	CJR reduced spending by about $812 per episode, mainly by shifting patients away from costly post-acute facilities, with no measured harm to quality.
315	The TEAM model, launched January 2026, makes episode payment mandatory across five surgical categories and adds safety-net hospital protections.
316	CMMI’s overall record is mixed: of 50+ models, a CBO analysis found only six achieved statistically significant savings, and only the Diabetes Prevention Program and Pioneer ACO met the bar for expansion.
