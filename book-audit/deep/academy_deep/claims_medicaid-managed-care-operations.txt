
## why-states-move-to-managed-care
0	Medicaid is the largest health insurance program in the United States, covering tens of millions of low-income children, adults, seniors, and people with disabilities.
1	As of July 2024, over 66 million Medicaid enrollees — about 78% of all enrollees — received their care through risk-based MCOs.
2	This shift from traditional fee-for-service Medicaid to managed care is one of the most consequential and least understood changes in American health policy.
3	Over 66 million enrollees — about 78% of all Medicaid enrollees — received care through risk-based MCOs as of July 2024 (KFF)
4	Medicaid Enrollees in MCOs (2024)
5	Managed care enrollment grew from ~10% of Medicaid beneficiaries in 1991 to nearly 40% by 1996 (NIH/PMC)
6	Medicaid began in 1965 as a fee-for-service program: providers delivered care and billed the state, which paid them for each service.
7	By the late 1980s and early 1990s, Medicaid costs were rising sharply, and states began experimenting with more cost-effective ways to deliver care.
8	In 1991, about 2.7 million beneficiaries were enrolled in some form of Medicaid managed care — roughly 10% of the program; by 1996, nearly 40% of Medicaid beneficiaries were enrolled in managed care plans.
9	A key legal turning point came with the Balanced Budget Act of 1997, which eliminated the requirement that states obtain a federal waiver before launching mandatory Medicaid managed care programs.
10	Before 1997, and still today for certain populations and program designs, states needed federal permission to depart from traditional Medicaid rules — typically through Section 1915 or Section 1115 waivers, which grant exemptions from standard Medicaid guidelines.
11	1965: Fee-for-service Medicaid begins
12	Late 1980s–early 1990s: Cost pressure
13	Managed care enrollment climbs from ~10% to nearly 40% of beneficiaries.
14	Eliminates the waiver requirement for mandatory managed care, accelerating adoption toward today’s ~78%.
15	1997: Balanced Budget Act
16	Medicaid is one of the largest items in every state budget, and states (unlike the federal government) must generally balance their budgets each year.
17	While states have reliably achieved more predictability in their Medicaid budgets, only a very small number of studies show that managed care has actually helped states lower costs or improve quality.
18	The most confident claim that can be made is about predictability — not savings, and not better outcomes.
19	Research has found that managed care use has been concentrated among the enrollees with the most stable spending — historically healthier children and families — which means the budget predictability gains were smaller than hoped, because the unpredictable, high-cost populations (the aged and people with disabilities) were often kept in fee-for-service.
20	As of 2024, thirty MCO states covered at least 75% of their Medicaid beneficiaries through MCOs.
21	The populations enrolled tell an important story: children and ACA expansion adults are the most likely to be in comprehensive MCOs (around 90% and 86% respectively), while people eligible through a disability pathway and adults 65 and older have historically been less likely to be enrolled — though states are steadily moving these higher-need groups into managed care too.
22	It is now the dominant model: over 66 million enrollees (~78%) received care through risk-based MCOs as of July 2024, and 30 states cover at least 75% of beneficiaries in MCOs.
23	Managed care grew rapidly in the 1990s (from ~10% of beneficiaries in 1991 to nearly 40% by 1996), accelerated by the Balanced Budget Act of 1997, which eliminated the waiver requirement for mandatory managed care.
24	The two most-cited rationales are increasing budget predictability and improving care coordination, with predictability the most powerful in practice because it converts volatile fee-for-service costs into a fixed per-member payment.
25	The evidence is mixed: states reliably gained predictability, but few studies show managed care lowered costs or improved quality, and predictability gains were limited because managed care first covered the most stable-spending populations.

## federal-framework-438
44	Federal Framework: 42 CFR Part 438 and CHIP Rules
45	For managed care, the master rulebook has a name worth memorizing: 42 CFR Part 438.
46	Understanding Part 438 is essential because it is the framework within which everything else in this course operates.
47	When a state writes an MCO contract, sets capitation rates, defines network standards, or oversees plan performance, it is implementing requirements that ultimately trace back to Part 438.
48	The companion rules for the Children’s Health Insurance Program (CHIP) live at 42 CFR Part 457, applying parallel managed care protections to CHIP enrollees.
49	In 2024, CMS substantially updated it through a major final rule, raising the bar on access, finance, and quality.
50	This lesson covers both the enduring structure of Part 438 and the significant 2024 changes that now shape how states must manage their managed care programs.
51	Key Concept: 42 CFR Part 438 is the federal regulation governing Medicaid managed care, setting the rules states must follow for network adequacy, finance (including MLR), quality, and beneficiary protections.
52	CHIP managed care is governed by the parallel 42 CFR Part 457.
53	If a state sets a minimum MLR for its managed care plans under Part 438, it must be equal to or higher than 85 percent (42 CFR 438.8)
54	The 2024 rule requires appointment within 15 business days for primary care and OB/GYN, and 10 business days for outpatient behavioral health (CMS 2024 final rule)
55	Part 438 is organized around the state’s responsibilities as the entity contracting with private plans.
56	Part 438 provides that if a state elects to mandate a minimum MLR for its plans, that minimum must be equal to or higher than 85 percent, and the MLR must be calculated and reported each year by the plan.
57	The MLR — covered in depth in its own lesson — ensures that at least 85 cents of every capitation dollar goes toward medical care and quality rather than profit and administration.
58	States have been required to submit summary MLR reports to CMS since rating periods beginning July 1, 2017.
59	Part 438 also imposes detailed network adequacy requirements at section 438.68.
60	The 2024 Final Rule: Access, Finance, and Quality
61	In 2024, CMS issued one of the most significant updates to the managed care framework in years.
62	The "Medicaid and CHIP Managed Care Access, Finance, and Quality" final rule (CMS-2439-F) was published May 10, 2024, and became effective July 9, 2024.
63	It advances CMS’s efforts to improve access to care, quality and health outcomes, and health equity for Medicaid and CHIP managed care enrollees — and it applies to both Part 438 (Medicaid) and Part 457 (CHIP).
64	The most concrete and consequential changes are on access.
65	For the first time, the rule sets specific, enforceable appointment wait time standards: enrollees must be able to get a routine appointment within 15 business days for primary care (adult and pediatric) and for OB/GYN services, and within 10 business days for outpatient mental health and substance use services.
66	New appointment wait-time standards (15 days primary/OB-GYN, 10 days behavioral health) enforced through secret shopper surveys.
67	The 2024 Final Rule’s Three Pillars
68	A standard means nothing without enforcement, and the 2024 rule pairs its access requirements with a specific monitoring tool: the secret shopper survey.
69	It is easy to dismiss regulatory citations like "42 CFR Part 438" as bureaucratic detail, but the framework is where the public interest in Medicaid managed care is actually protected.
70	Part 438 and its CHIP counterpart are the legal counterweight — the binding rules that require plans to maintain adequate networks, spend most of their capitation on care, meet quality standards, and treat beneficiaries fairly.
71	If a Medicaid MCO contract is the lease on an apartment, 42 CFR Part 438 is the housing code behind it.
72	The 2024 rule is like the code being updated to add a new requirement that the heat actually reach a measured temperature, with inspectors (secret shoppers) sent to verify it.
73	The federal government sets the floor through Part 438; states build on top of it through their own contracts, which can be more demanding but not less.
74	This is why two states’ Medicaid managed care programs can look quite different while both complying with the same federal rules — and why a major federal rule change, like the 2024 update, ripples through every state’s program at once, forcing contract amendments, new monitoring, and new reporting nationwide.
75	For the rest of this course, Part 438 is the connective tissue.
76	42 CFR Part 438 is the federal regulation governing Medicaid managed care, setting the rules states must follow for network adequacy, finance (including MLR), quality, and beneficiary protections; CHIP managed care is governed by the parallel 42 CFR Part 457.
77	Part 438 requires actuarially sound capitation rates and provides that any state-mandated minimum medical loss ratio must be at least 85 percent, reported annually by each plan.
78	Network adequacy requirements (section 438.68) require states to set quantitative standards for specified provider types, including LTSS provider types when LTSS is covered.
79	The 2024 "Access, Finance, and Quality" final rule (CMS-2439-F, effective July 9, 2024) significantly updated the framework across three pillars — access, finance, and quality — for both Medicaid and CHIP.
80	The rule established first-of-their-kind appointment wait-time standards: 15 business days for primary care and OB/GYN, and 10 business days for outpatient mental health and substance use services.

## rfp-process-mco-selection
87	Getting the procurement right is one of the most consequential things a state Medicaid agency does.
88	The RFP translates Part 438’s rules and the state’s own priorities into specific demands that bidding plans must meet, and the evaluation determines which plans best satisfy them.
89	A full Medicaid managed care procurement — preplanning, bidding, and implementation — typically lasts 18 to 24 months (MACPAC)

92	In Hawaii’s 2021 procurement, for example, answers were rated on a zero-to-five scale — zero being "Very Poor" and five "Excellent" — with each RFP question worth a set number of points.
93	The 2024–2025 period offered vivid examples across the country.
94	In Rhode Island, bidders protested a roughly $15 billion award — pointing out, among other things, that the review team was composed entirely of agency employees in violation of state procurement rules — prompting the state to reevaluate proposals, cancel the solicitation, extend incumbent contracts, and prepare a revised RFP.

## contract-requirements
105	It converts the federal requirements of 42 CFR Part 438 and the state’s own priorities into specific, enforceable obligations the plan must meet.
106	The first pillar is network adequacy: the contract must require the plan to maintain a provider network sufficient to deliver the covered services.
107	Under 42 CFR 438.68, states must develop and publish a quantitative network adequacy standard (other than appointment wait times — often time-and-distance or provider-to-enrollee ratios) for a defined set of provider types — primary care (adult and pediatric), OB/GYN, behavioral health, adult and pediatric specialists, hospitals, pharmacy, and pediatric dental — plus additional types as CMS determines.
108	The 2024 final rule layered appointment wait-time standards on top of these, as the federal-framework lesson described, so adequacy is now judged by both the state’s quantitative standards and timeliness.
109	Each state contracting with managed care plans must draft and implement a written quality strategy for assessing and improving the quality of care (42 CFR 438.340).
110	The centerpiece is the requirement under 42 CFR 438.66 that each state submit to CMS, no later than 180 days after each contract year, a Managed Care Program Annual Report (MCPAR) on each program.
111	Annual MCPAR (within 180 days), encounter data, appeals/grievances reporting, backed by sanctions and liquidated damages.
112	The MCO contract converts federal rules (42 CFR Part 438) and state priorities into binding, enforceable obligations, organized around three core pillars: network adequacy, quality assurance, and reporting.
113	Network adequacy requirements (438.68) include quantitative standards (such as time-and-distance or provider ratios) for specified provider types plus 2024 appointment wait-time standards, and must consider LEP communication, disability accommodation, and telehealth.
114	Quality assurance requires a state quality strategy (438.340), a plan QAPI program, external quality review by an independent organization, and accreditation/measure reporting — a system that exists to counter the skimping incentive of capitation.

## monitoring-oversight-eqro
122	The federal parameters that states must follow when conducting EQR are set out at 42 CFR §§ 438.350, 438.354, 438.358, 438.360, and 438.364, making EQR a federally required, structured process rather than an ad hoc review.
123	EQR is built from a defined set of EQR-related activities — the work, set out at 42 CFR § 438.358, that produces the data the EQRO analyzes.
124	A fourth, added by the 2016 rule, is validation of each plan’s network adequacy against the state’s §438.68 standards.
125	Beyond the mandatory activities, federal rules also define optional activities states may add — such as validating encounter data, administering or validating consumer and provider surveys, and conducting focused quality studies — and the 2024 final rule added EQRO help with the new managed care quality ratings.
126	Validate each plan’s network against the state’s §438.68 standards (mandatory); states may add optional reviews such as encounter data validation, consumer surveys, or focused quality studies.
127	CMS reinforces this with required reports — the Managed Care Program Annual Report (MCPAR) under 42 CFR § 438.66(e), the medical loss ratio summary reports under § 438.74, and the network adequacy assurance under § 438.207(d) — that compel states to compile and submit evidence of their oversight to the federal government.
128	EQR is the independent analysis of aggregated information on the quality, timeliness, and access to care a plan furnishes, governed by federal parameters at 42 CFR §§ 438.350–438.364.
129	Mandatory EQR activities (438.358) include a compliance review once every three years, validation of reported performance measures, validation of performance improvement projects (PIPs), and validation of network adequacy; states may add optional activities such as encounter data validation or consumer surveys.
130	Surrounding the EQRO, states maintain a broad monitoring system and submit federally required reports (the Managed Care Program Annual Report under 438.66(e), MLR summary reports under 438.74, and network adequacy assurances under 438.207(d)), creating a layered structure that lets states capture managed care’s benefits while guarding against its risks.

## capitation-rate-setting
137	The governing rule is 42 CFR 438.4, the federal regulation on actuarial soundness, supported by detailed annual CMS rate development guides.
138	Federal law (42 CFR 438.4) requires rates to be "actuarially sound" — projected to cover all reasonable, appropriate, and attainable costs for the population and services, developed by qualified actuaries and certified to CMS.
139	When a state certifies a range of rates per cell, the upper bound cannot exceed the lower bound multiplied by 1.05 — a 5% maximum range (CMS final rule)
140	A state may move up to 1.5% in either direction from the certified rate without notifying CMS — roughly the typical risk margin in rate-setting (CMS)
141	The process, outlined in federal rules (42 CFR 438.5(b)), follows a logical sequence.
142	But the range is bounded: the upper bound of a rate range cannot exceed the lower bound multiplied by 1.05 — a maximum 5% range.
143	Separately, the rules permit a state to move up to 1.5% in either direction from the certified rate without notifying CMS, a figure CMS chose because it is generally not more than the risk margin in a typical rate-setting process.
144	The certified rates must target an MLR of at least 85%, meaning the rate is built so that at least 85 cents of every dollar is expected to go to medical care and quality rather than administration and profit; states may target a higher MLR if they choose.
145	The 85% MLR target ties to the MLR lesson that follows.
146	And the actuarial soundness requirement is itself a Part 438 mandate from the federal-framework lesson.
147	A Medicaid capitation rate is the fixed per-member-per-month (PMPM) payment to an MCO, and federal law (42 CFR 438.4) requires it to be "actuarially sound" — projected to cover all reasonable, appropriate, and attainable costs for the population and services.
148	States may certify a range of rates per cell, but the upper bound cannot exceed 1.05× the lower bound (a 5% maximum range), and a state may move ±1.5% from the certified rate without notifying CMS (roughly the typical risk margin).
149	Certified rates must target a medical loss ratio of at least 85%, linking rate-setting to the MLR requirement, with states free to target a higher MLR.

## risk-adjustment-medicaid
156	This makes risk adjustment one of the most important safeguards in all of Medicaid managed care.
157	Of the 38 Medicaid programs that risk-adjust MCO payments, 33 use the Chronic Illness and Disability Payment System (CDPS) or a related model (Medical Care, 2024 — UC San Diego and the Institute for Medicaid Innovation)
158	CDPS uses ICD-10-CM diagnosis codes grouped into condition categories to measure each member’s illness burden (UC San Diego / Find-A-Code)
159	A member with no significant conditions might have a risk score near 1.0 (average); a member with multiple chronic illnesses might score 3.0 or higher, signaling expected costs three times the average.
160	A plan with a population risk score of 1.2 enrolls members who are 20% costlier than average and is paid more to reflect that.
161	CDPS is a diagnosis-based model: it uses ICD-10-CM diagnosis codes, grouped into categories for different diseases and conditions (such as psychiatric or cancer categories), to measure each member’s illness burden.

## provider-credentialing-mco
241	This is the gatekeeping that determines who is allowed to deliver care, and it is both a patient-safety safeguard and one of the most notorious sources of administrative burden in all of health care.
242	Key Concept: Credentialing verifies a provider’s qualifications (license, education, history) through primary source verification, while Medicaid enrollment screening (42 CFR Part 455) vets providers for fraud/abuse risk.

244	Recent NCQA updates have tightened these: the PSV window was reduced (to as little as 90–120 days depending on the program), so verifications like license checks, malpractice review, and board confirmation must be completed within tighter timeframes before the committee decides.

303	About 19 states have set required minimum saturation targets, and a classic example is Arizona, where the required proportion of MCO revenues in value-based arrangements started at 5% in 2013, rose to 10% in 2014, and climbed to 20% in 2015 and 2016.
304	To solve this, many states adopt the Health Care Payment Learning & Action Network (HCP-LAN) framework, a widely used classification that sorts payment arrangements into categories by how much they tie payment to value and risk — from pure fee-for-service (Category 1), to fee-for-service linked to quality (Category 2), to alternative payment models built on fee-for-service architecture (Category 3), to population-based payment (Category 4).
305	About 17 states specifically reference the HCP-LAN framework in defining acceptable VBP arrangements, and of those, 10 specify the categories MCOs should use — usually HCP-LAN Category 2C or higher, meaning at least fee-for-service tied to quality and ideally more advanced models.
306	When a state requires its MCOs to move providers to "Category 3 or higher," it is requiring them to move providers up the risk continuum into shared savings and beyond.
307	The most consequential and fastest-growing tool for VBP in Medicaid managed care is the state directed payment (SDP).
308	Created when CMS updated the managed care regulations in 2016, SDPs allow a state to direct how its MCOs pay providers — overriding the normal rule that plans set their own provider rates.
309	Between February 2023 and August 2024, CMS approved 302 state directed payment arrangements across 40 states and Puerto Rico, and the arrangements approved as of August 2024 are projected to spend $110.2 billion a year — nearly a 60% increase over the $69.3 billion projected just eighteen months earlier.
310	In 2024, CMS released a managed care rule with additional updates intended to improve the oversight and transparency of directed payments — a response to concerns that SDPs were growing so fast, and channeling so much money, that they needed tighter guardrails.
311	State directed payments let states reach through MCOs to shape provider payment and advance value-based goals, but their explosive growth to $110+ billion a year prompted CMS to tighten oversight and transparency in 2024.
312	A state can mandate that 50% of MCO payments be "value-based" and still see little change if the underlying arrangements are weak (mere pay-for-performance bonuses rather than real risk) or if providers lack the infrastructure to respond.
313	The next lesson, on Section 1115 waivers, examines the federal authority that lets states innovate beyond standard rules — including many of the boldest VBP and delivery-reform experiments.
314	About 26 states define required VBP model types and 19 set minimum saturation targets, frequently ramped over time — Arizona moved from 5% of MCO revenues in value-based arrangements in 2013 to 20% by 2015–2016.
315	Many states use the HCP-LAN framework to define "value-based," with ~17 referencing it and 10 specifying categories (usually Category 2C or higher); the framework maps the same risk continuum from fee-for-service to population-based payment.
316	State directed payments (SDPs), created by CMS in 2016, let states direct how MCOs pay providers — including requiring VBP participation — and have grown explosively to a projected $110.2 billion a year as of August 2024 (302 arrangements in 40 states), up ~60% in 18 months.
317	The rapid SDP growth prompted CMS to tighten oversight and transparency in 2024 and to require evaluation measures (readmissions, preventive care, chronic-disease outcomes), since SDPs can either advance value or simply inflate payments.

## section-1115-waivers
324	Section 1115 Waivers and Managed Care Innovation
325	The answer is the Section 1115 demonstration waiver — the single most powerful tool states have to reinvent their Medicaid programs, and the engine behind most major Medicaid managed care innovation.
326	Section 1115 of the Social Security Act gives the Secretary of Health and Human Services authority to approve experimental, pilot, or demonstration projects likely to promote the objectives of the Medicaid program.
327	What makes Section 1115 uniquely powerful is its breadth: it combines extensive waiver authority with a broadly defined purpose.
328	In effect, Section 1115 is a license to experiment with the program’s core rules.
329	For Medicaid managed care, 1115 waivers are foundational.
330	Many states’ entire managed care programs operate under 1115 authority, and the boldest delivery and payment innovations — from value-based reforms to addressing social needs — are tested through these demonstrations.
331	Understanding Section 1115 is understanding how Medicaid changes and how the most ambitious managed care experiments become possible.
332	Key Concept: A Section 1115 demonstration waiver lets the HHS Secretary waive most Medicaid rules and authorize federal funding for normally non-matchable costs, so states can test experimental approaches.
333	Section 1115 lets the Secretary waive almost any Medicaid state plan requirement under §1902 to carry out a demonstration (MACPAC / KFF)
334	Section 1115 waivers are generally approved for an initial five-year period and renewable for three-to-five-year periods (KFF)
335	A Section 1115 demonstration is, formally, a research and demonstration project: states must have an approved, publicly available evaluation strategy, because the authority is meant to test and evaluate new policy approaches, not simply to expand programs.
336	The breadth is remarkable — under Section 1115, the Secretary can waive almost any state plan requirement under §1902 (with narrow exceptions like citizenship rules), to the extent necessary to carry out the demonstration.
337	Equally important is the "expenditure authority" that comes with 1115: the Secretary can permit federal financial participation for costs not otherwise matchable (CNOM) — federal dollars for services and populations not included in the regular Medicaid state plan.
338	There is a federal 30-day public comment period, and CMS must consider all comments.
339	CMS reviews for alignment with Medicaid objectives, runs a 30-day federal comment period, and negotiates terms (often 6 months–2 years).
340	Approved for ~5 years with budget-neutrality limits and a required, public evaluation; renewable for 3–5 year periods.
341	The most important limit on Section 1115 is budget neutrality: a demonstration must not cost the federal government more than it would have spent without the waiver.
342	This single rule shapes nearly every 1115 design, because it means a state cannot simply ask the federal government to pay for new things — it must show that, on net, the demonstration stays within the federal spending it would have incurred anyway.
343	Budget neutrality means an 1115 demonstration cannot simply add federal cost — new services and populations must generally be financed from savings the demonstration itself generates against the without-waiver baseline.
344	This is why 1115 innovations are framed as investments expected to reduce other spending, not as straightforward expansions.
345	Section 1115 has become the primary vehicle for the most ambitious Medicaid innovations, and recent years have seen a wave of demonstrations addressing health-related social needs (HRSN) — the social factors like housing and food that drive health outcomes.
346	In 2022 CMS announced a demonstration opportunity to expand states’ tools to address enrollees’ health-related social needs, and by February 2024 had approved eight states under the HRSN framework — Arizona, Arkansas, California, Massachusetts, New Jersey, New York, Oregon, and Washington.
347	New York’s January 2024 waiver amendment included up to $3.673 billion to build HRSN infrastructure, including new "Social Care Networks," and to reimburse for HRSN services through Medicaid.
348	A separate 1115 opportunity addressed reentry for justice-involved individuals — partially waiving the Medicaid "inmate exclusion" so states can provide pre-release services — with the Biden administration approving 19 state reentry waivers.
349	These innovations are politically contingent, which is itself a defining feature of 1115.
350	In March 2025, the Trump administration rescinded the Biden administration’s HRSN guidance; CMS indicated this does not nullify existing HRSN approvals, but that it will consider HRSN and social-determinant requests on a case-by-case basis going forward.
351	This illustrates a core truth about 1115: it is a powerful tool for innovation precisely because it is discretionary, which also means the innovations it enables can be expanded or curtailed as federal priorities change.
352	Why 1115 Is the Engine of Medicaid Innovation
353	Section 1115 occupies a unique place in Medicaid: it is the mechanism through which the program evolves.
354	Almost every major delivery and payment innovation — including the all-payer and global-budget models discussed in the value-based-care course, comprehensive managed care expansions, and the social-needs demonstrations above — runs through 1115 authority.
355	When a state wants to do something genuinely new with Medicaid, the path almost always leads to a Section 1115 waiver.
356	Section 1115 is like a sanctioned "sandbox" or pilot program within a heavily regulated system.
357	The standard Medicaid rules are the building code everyone must follow; an 1115 waiver is special permission to construct an experimental building that breaks some of those rules — but only if you can prove it won’t cost the city more than a normal building (budget neutrality), you document what you’re testing (evaluation), and you get the regulator’s sign-off (case-by-case approval).
358	How Section 1115 enables Medicaid innovation
359	The tension at the heart of 1115 is between flexibility and accountability.
360	The required evaluations are meant to provide accountability, but the political nature of approval means 1115 is as much a policy instrument as a research tool.
361	The next and final lesson looks at where Medicaid managed care is heading — toward social determinants of health and whole-person care — and Section 1115 is precisely the authority making that direction possible.
362	The HRSN demonstrations covered here are the leading edge of that future.
363	Understanding 1115 means understanding not just a regulatory mechanism, but the very channel through which Medicaid managed care reaches beyond medical care toward the social factors that shape health, and through which the program will continue to transform in the years ahead.
364	A Section 1115 demonstration waiver lets the HHS Secretary waive almost any Medicaid state plan requirement (§1902) and authorize federal funding for normally non-matchable costs, so states can test experimental approaches — the most powerful tool for Medicaid innovation.
365	Section 1115 is uniquely broad, combining extensive waiver authority with a broadly defined purpose, and many states run their entire managed care programs and boldest reforms under 1115 authority.
366	1115 demonstrations are formally research projects requiring a public evaluation strategy, and expenditure authority (costs not otherwise matchable, CNOM) is what lets states fund services and populations outside the regular state plan.
367	The approval process is rigorous and slow — a detailed application, a 30-day federal comment period, and case-by-case CMS review — typically taking six months to two years, longer for comprehensive waivers.
368	A wave of recent 1115s address health-related social needs (HRSN): eight states (AZ, AR, CA, MA, NJ, NY, OR, WA) approved by February 2024 for housing and nutrition services, including New York’s $3.673 billion Social Care Networks; a separate opportunity approved 19 state reentry waivers partially waiving the inmate-exclusion policy.
369	1115 innovations are politically contingent — in March 2025 the Trump administration rescinded the Biden HRSN guidance (without nullifying existing approvals), shifting to case-by-case review — reflecting that discretionary approval enables both expansion and curtailment.
370	Section 1115 is the engine through which Medicaid evolves, including the all-payer/global-budget and social-needs innovations, balancing flexibility (enabling innovation) against accountability (evaluation requirements amid political discretion).

380	Projected Expansion Enrollment Loss (2028)
381	The mechanisms are ones this course has already covered: Section 1115 demonstrations (the HRSN waivers), "in lieu of services and settings" (ILOS) authority from the 2024 final rule, and value-based arrangements that reward keeping people healthy.
382	Use 1115 HRSN waivers, ILOS authority, and VBP to fund housing, nutrition, and employment supports.
383	Recent federal law (H.R.1, also discussed as OBBBA in the hospital-finance course) is projected to reduce federal Medicaid spending by roughly $911 billion — about 14% — over a decade, and to increase the number of uninsured people by around 7.5 million, with impacts varying widely by state (spending cuts ranging from about 4% to nearly a fifth of federal Medicaid spending in some states).
384	Since H.R.1 was enacted, the five largest Medicaid insurers have already lost a net 1.4 million members (3.8%), and the Urban Institute projects that expansion-adult enrollment in 2028 could be 4.9 to 10.1 million (27–55%) lower than otherwise expected, depending on how much states mitigate coverage losses.
385	The drivers include work reporting requirements and more frequent (six-month) eligibility redeterminations beginning January 1, 2027, which research consistently shows cause eligible people to lose coverage through administrative churn, not just genuine ineligibility.
386	The most pressing concerns for Medicaid managed care include enrollment oversight (ensuring that eligible people are not wrongly disenrolled amid redeterminations and work requirements), network adequacy (keeping real access as financing tightens), benefit design (deciding what to cover when budgets shrink), and MCO accountability (holding plans to their obligations even under fiscal stress).
387	The 2024 final rule’s quality rating system, the strengthened network adequacy and secret-shopper requirements, the tightened MLR and directed-payment oversight — all of these accountability tools take on heightened significance in a contracting environment.
388	States moved to managed care chiefly for budget predictability; they choose among delivery models that transfer different amounts of risk; they operate within the federal framework of 42 CFR Part 438; they select plans through procurement, pay them through actuarially sound risk-adjusted capitation bounded by medical loss ratios, require adequate networks and credentialed providers, measure quality through HEDIS, oversee performance through external review, and increasingly push value-based payment and whole-person care.
389	Plans address social needs through Section 1115 HRSN waivers, "in lieu of services" (ILOS) authority, value-based arrangements, community-based partnerships, and emerging SDOH-informed risk adjustment.
