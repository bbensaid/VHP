
## dna-genes-variants
0	In humans, the genome is about 3.2 billion nucleotide "letters" (the bases A, T, G, and C), arranged as a long double helix — the famous twisted ladder.
1	Humans have 23 pairs of chromosomes (24 distinct types): 22 numbered "autosomes" plus the two sex chromosomes, X and Y.
2	The human genome is ~3.2 billion DNA base pairs (A, T, G, C) packaged into 23 pairs of chromosomes (22 autosomes + X/Y).
3	Within the genome's 3.2 billion letters are genes — the functional units of heredity.
4	Humans have roughly 20,000 to 25,000 genes — strikingly few, given our complexity.
5	And here is a fact that surprises many: only about 2 percent of the human genome actually consists of protein-coding genes.
6	The other ~98 percent is non-coding DNA, much of which has regulatory and other functions we are still mapping.
7	Think of the genome as a vast instruction manual of 3.2 billion characters.
8	Genes are the specific recipes within it — only about 2% of the pages are actual recipes, and there are roughly 20,000-25,000 of them.
9	Any two people share about 99.9 percent of their DNA; the differences — the ~0.1 percent — are what make us individuals, and they are what clinical genomics is mostly about.
10	The most common is the single nucleotide variant or SNP — a single base swapped for another (e.g., an A where the reference has a G).
11	Many variants are harmless (most of that 0.1 percent is just normal human diversity).
12	A gene present in 1 or 3 copies vs 2
13	Germline variants are the realm of hereditary disease and inherited risk: a BRCA1 variant raising breast and ovarian cancer risk, a cystic fibrosis variant, a pharmacogenomic variant affecting drug metabolism — all inherited, all present from birth, all potentially relevant to relatives.
14	To bring consistency, the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG), with the Association for Molecular Pathology, established guidelines in 2015 for classifying germline variants into five categories: Pathogenic, Likely Pathogenic, Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS), Likely Benign, and Benign.
15	The system weighs many lines of evidence — population frequency, computational predictions, functional studies, how the variant segregates in families, whether it arose new ("de novo") — across 28 criteria, to reach a classification.
16	The "Likely" categories convey roughly 90 percent certainty.
17	The category that causes the most clinical difficulty is the Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS) — a variant where the evidence is genuinely insufficient to call it harmful or harmless.

## human-genome-project-to-clinical

30	The HGP was an international effort, launched in 1990, to determine the complete sequence of the roughly 3.2 billion base pairs of human DNA and to map all the genes — the first reading of "nature's complete genetic blueprint for a human." It was the world's largest collaborative biological project, involving researchers across the US, UK, France, Japan, China, and others.
31	It was declared complete in April 2003 — actually ahead of schedule — at a total cost of about $2.7 billion.

## pharmacogenomics-drug-therapy
80	Pharmacogenomics is the study of how a person's genetic variations affect their response to medications, and it is one of the most immediately practical applications of genomics in everyday care.
81	Many important drugs are processed by enzymes in the cytochrome P450 family — particularly CYP2D6 and CYP2C19 — and genetic variants change how active those enzymes are.
82	For CYP2D6, this is quantified with an "activity score": 0 is a poor metabolizer, 0.25 to 1 intermediate, 1.25 to 2.25 normal, and above 2.25 ultrarapid (the 2020 CPIC consensus).
83	This prodrug reversal is the source of some of the most dramatic pharmacogenomic events.
84	Clopidogrel (Plavix), an antiplatelet drug given after cardiac stenting, is a prodrug activated by CYP2C19.
85	Patients who are CYP2C19 poor metabolizers (around 3 percent of Europeans and 15-20 percent of East Asians), and to a lesser degree intermediate metabolizers, activate it poorly, get reduced protection, and face higher rates of adverse cardiac events and stent thrombosis — so genotype guides whether to use clopidogrel or an alternative.
86	Warfarin, the blood thinner with a notoriously narrow therapeutic window, is dosed partly by CYP2C9 and VKORC1 genotype — its FDA label includes a genotype-based dosing table.
87	Codeine is a prodrug activated by CYP2D6 to morphine; ultrarapid metabolizers can generate dangerously high morphine levels, and reports of harm to breastfed neonates of ultrarapid-metabolizer mothers led to major safety warnings.
88	Some of the most striking examples are HLA-related severe reactions.
89	Abacavir (an HIV drug) causes life-threatening hypersensitivity in patients carrying HLA-B*5701 — so testing is required before prescribing, and it is contraindicated if positive.
90	Carbamazepine (an anticonvulsant) can cause Stevens-Johnson syndrome and toxic epidermal necrolysis in patients of East Asian ancestry carrying HLA-B*1502, so the FDA label recommends screening first.
91	Researchers discovered the reaction is strongly tied to carrying the HLA-B*5701 allele.
92	Now HLA-B*5701 testing is standard before prescribing abacavir, and the drug is contraindicated (with a black-box warning) in those who test positive — a single genetic test that essentially eliminates a life-threatening reaction.
93	Knowing a patient is a CYP2C19 poor metabolizer is useless unless the clinician knows what to DO about it.
94	CPIC guidelines cover the major actionable pairs — CYP2C19 and clopidogrel, CYP2C9/VKORC1 and warfarin, SLCO1B1 and statins (muscle-toxicity risk), CYP2D6/CYP2C19 and antidepressants, and many more.
95	Because a person's germline pharmacogenes never change, preemptive testing is increasingly favored: sequence the relevant genes once, and the information is available for a lifetime of prescribing decisions — drug and dose selection, avoiding adverse events, before the first prescription is even written.
96	Pharmacogenomics is arguably the most "ready for primetime" application of clinical genomics: the science is solid for many drug-gene pairs, CPIC provides actionable guidance, the FDA annotates labels, and the testing is cheap.
97	Drug-metabolizing enzymes (especially CYP2D6 and CYP2C19) vary genetically, classifying patients as poor, intermediate, normal, or ultrarapid metabolizers.
98	Classic examples: clopidogrel-CYP2C19 (reduced effect in poor metabolizers), warfarin-CYP2C9/VKORC1 (genotype-guided dosing), codeine-CYP2D6 (UM toxicity risk), abacavir-HLA-B*5701 and carbamazepine-HLA-B*1502 (severe-reaction risk).
99	Preemptive testing (test once, consult for a lifetime) is increasingly favored; pharmacogenomics is the most "ready for primetime" clinical genomics application, with implementation now the main barrier.

101	(This is the reverse of an active drug, where a poor metabolizer would accumulate it.) CPIC guidelines therefore recommend considering an alternative antiplatelet for CYP2C19 poor metabolizers — a concrete example of genotype guiding prescribing.

## hereditary-conditions
127	More than 50 hereditary cancer syndromes have been described, but two dominate clinical practice.
128	Hereditary Breast and Ovarian Cancer (HBOC) syndrome is caused by pathogenic variants in BRCA1 or BRCA2.
129	It is inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern, and carriers face dramatically elevated cancer risk — women with BRCA variants have roughly a 65-74 percent lifetime risk of breast cancer and a substantially elevated risk of ovarian cancer.
130	HBOC's prevalence varies by population: the BRCA carrier frequency is roughly 1 in 500 in the general population but about 1 in 40 in people of Ashkenazi Jewish ancestry — a reminder that genetic risk is unevenly distributed and that ancestry matters in risk assessment.
131	The other major syndrome is Lynch syndrome (hereditary nonpolyposis colorectal cancer), caused by defects in DNA mismatch-repair genes (MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2, EPCAM).
132	It is highly penetrant and autosomal dominant, and it accounts for roughly 1-3 percent of all colorectal cancer cases, plus elevated risk of endometrial and other cancers.
133	General population (~1 in 40 in Ashkenazi Jewish) (ACOG/research)
134	Penetrance is rarely 100 percent; a BRCA variant confers high but not certain risk.
135	The most powerful — and most underused — feature of hereditary genetics is cascade testing: when a person (the "proband") is found to carry a pathogenic variant, that information cascades to their at-risk relatives, who can then be tested.
136	Because these conditions are autosomal dominant, each first-degree relative (parent, sibling, child) has a 50 percent chance of carrying the same variant.
137	The CDC recognizes this by designating cascade testing for Hereditary Breast and Ovarian Cancer, Lynch syndrome, and familial hypercholesterolemia as "Tier 1" genomic applications — meaning the evidence for population-level benefit is strongest for these.
138	Because hereditary cancer syndromes are autosomal dominant, each first-degree relative of a carrier has a 50% chance of carrying the same variant.
139	Cascade testing is CDC Tier 1 but badly underutilized.
