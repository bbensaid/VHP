
## why-quality-measurement-matters
0	For most of the 20th century, American medicine assumed that well-trained doctors working in good hospitals delivered good care, and there was little systematic effort to check.
1	That assumption shattered at the turn of the century, when a pair of landmark reports forced the country to confront how often care fell short.
2	In November 1999, the Institute of Medicine (IOM) released "To Err Is Human: Building a Safer Health System." Its central finding stunned the public and the profession: between 44,000 and 98,000 Americans were dying each year as a result of preventable medical errors in hospitals.
3	The report estimated that medication errors alone accounted for about 7,000 deaths annually, and that the economic cost of preventable adverse events ran to tens of billions of dollars.

Where the first report focused on safety, this one widened the lens to the entire quality gap — the chasm between the care we know how to deliver and the care patients actually get.
9	The Six Aims of Quality (IOM, 2001)
10	The IOM reports made the case conceptually; a landmark study soon quantified it.
11	In 2003, Elizabeth McGlynn and colleagues published a study in the New England Journal of Medicine examining whether adults actually received recommended care.
12	The researchers reviewed the medical records of nearly 7,000 adults across 12 metropolitan areas, scoring care against 439 clinical indicators for 30 conditions plus preventive care.
13	The headline result: on average, participants received just 54.9 percent of recommended care — barely more than a coin flip.

## ecqms-explained
207	A blood-pressure reading or an A1c result often never appears on a claim, so claims measures miss clinically important detail.

## ehr-data-quality
225	The most sophisticated measure in the world is worthless if the underlying EHR data is wrong, missing, or inconsistent.
226	Other frameworks add dimensions like plausibility (is the value believable — a blood pressure of 900 is not), uniqueness (no duplicate records), and representativeness.
227	Notably, there is still no single standard taxonomy for EHR data quality, which is itself a challenge — but completeness is consistently the most-assessed dimension, because missing data is the most common and most damaging problem.
228	A required A1c result is missing
229	Missing data is the headline issue: studies have found missing-data rates exceeding 80 percent for some variables, meaning the fact a measure needs simply is not in the record for most patients.
230	Copy-paste prevalence has been measured at around 82 percent in residents' progress notes, and more than half of the text in some electronic records is duplicated.
231	EHR-related medication errors have been found to comprise about 34 percent of all medication errors in ICUs, with a substantial share carrying life-threatening potential.

## risk-adjustment-equity
262	But when the "risk" in question is a social factor — poverty, housing instability, race — risk adjustment becomes one of the most contested questions in all of quality measurement.
263	A patient's outcome — say, whether they are readmitted within 30 days — depends heavily on factors outside the hospital's control: whether they can afford medications, have stable housing, can get to follow-up appointments, or have social support at home.
264	Without social risk adjustment, that hospital looks worse and gets penalized — which can drain resources from exactly the institutions caring for the most vulnerable, worsening inequity.
265	Systematic review evidence shows this narrowed the penalty gap between hospitals serving more versus fewer socially disadvantaged patients — concrete proof that social risk adjustment materially shifts who gets penalized.
266	A 2020 report to Congress from HHS's Office of the Assistant Secretary for Planning and Evaluation (ASPE) recommended against using social risk adjustment for performance measures that reflect a provider's quality of care — precisely because doing so could obscure quality differences that ought to be surfaced and fixed.

## statistical-process-control
299	This is the central question of improvement, and answering it wrong is one of the most common and costly mistakes in quality work.
300	SPC was invented by the physicist Walter Shewhart in the 1920s for manufacturing and later championed in health care through the Deming lineage that also gave us the Model for Improvement.
301	The Run Chart: The First-Step Tool
302	A "shift" is an unusually long run of consecutive points all above or all below the median (for example, with about 20 points, a run longer than 7 signals a shift).
303	An unusually long run of points all on one side of the median (e.g., >7 of ~20).
304	Those control limits are typically set at ±3 standard deviations from the mean — which is why they are called "3-sigma limits." They represent the boundaries of expected common cause variation.
305	There is a trade-off, though — the 3-sigma rule is actually less sensitive to small shifts (up to about 2 standard deviations) than the run chart's shift and crossings rules, so the tools are complementary, not strictly ranked.
306	UCL/LCL typically set at 3 standard deviations from the mean
307	A control chart adds a mean center line and statistically calculated control limits (typically ±3 sigma), and can detect out-of-control conditions and process stability.
308	The 3-sigma rule is less sensitive to small shifts than run-chart rules, so run charts and control charts are complementary tools.
309	SPC uses time-ordered charts and rules — such as a run-chart shift (a long run above the median) or a control-chart point beyond the ±3-sigma limits — to determine whether a genuine special cause (the intervention) shifted the process, rather than reacting to one data point.
