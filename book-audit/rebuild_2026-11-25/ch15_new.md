# Chapter 15: Delivering Vermont's Reform — The Institutions, the Workstreams, and the People Who Carry Them

> *Written for AHS and GMCB leaders, hospital executives, Blueprint and designated-agency leaders, legislators on the health committees, VITL, and anyone in another state who has to turn a reform statute into results.*

Oliver Wyman told Vermont what was wrong: nine of fourteen hospitals losing money in 2023, thirteen projected to by 2028, a five-year system deficit of $700 million to $2.4 billion, and premiums up 108% in six years. Act 68 told Vermont what to do about it. This chapter is about the part neither document could settle: who, inside Vermont's institutions, actually carries each piece of the reform, what each piece depends on, and where execution is most likely to fail. It is organized around the five workstreams that will decide whether Act 68 works — hospital transformation, price and budget regulation, the Blueprint for Health, clinical and claims data integration, and the Rural Health Transformation Program — and around the people and institutions that carry them.

## 15.2 **Who Holds Which Lever**

| Institution | What the law gives it | What it cannot do alone | Statute |
|---|---|---|---|
| AHS, Office of Health Care Reform | The Director of Health Care Reform coordinates reform across the executive branch and with GMCB; AHS writes the Strategic Plan, runs the RHT program, supervises hospital collaboration, and reports monthly | Set hospital prices or budgets | 3 V.S.A. § 3027; Act 68 |
| GMCB | Sets hospital budgets, reference-based prices, and global budgets; certifies ACOs; administers VHCURES | Direct how AHS runs programs | 18 V.S.A. ch. 220; Act 68 |
| Blueprint for Health (in AHS) | Runs the statewide PCMH and Community Health Team network; calls the Steering Committee for Comprehensive Primary Health Care | Secure its own long-term funding | 18 V.S.A. ch. 13; Act 68, Sec. 9 |
| DVHA (in AHS) | Runs Medicaid and its data warehouse; owns the statewide Health Information Technology Plan | Integrate data in the UHDS without a Steering Committee vote | 18 V.S.A. § 9351 |
| HIE Steering Committee | Decides, by majority of all voting members, whether and for which uses clinical, claims, and social-needs data are integrated | Fund or operate anything | 18 V.S.A. § 9353 |
| VITL | Designated operator of "the exclusive statewide health information exchange network" | Set its terms outside the State's contract | 18 V.S.A. § 9352 |
| Agency of Digital Services | Reviews and approves all executive-branch IT activities, supervises IT staff, requires independent review of IT projects of $1 million or more | Set health policy | 3 V.S.A. §§ 3301–3303 |
| Health Care Delivery Advisory Committee | Eighteen members, chaired by the Secretary of Human Services; develops the Strategic Plan with AHS | Bind any agency | 18 V.S.A. § 9403a |
| Legislature | Health Reform Oversight and Joint Fiscal Committees receive AHS's monthly reports; health committees receive the annual updates | Manage the work | Act 68 |

*Figure 15.2 — Decision rights in Vermont's reform. Sources: statutes as cited; Act 62 of 2025.*

Two features of this map matter most. First, **the price regulator and the transformation agency are different bodies**: GMCB decides what hospitals may charge and spend, while AHS decides how the system is redesigned and where the transformation money goes. Every hospital feels both at once, and they are coordinated only by the § 3027 Director of Health Care Reform and by goodwill. Second, **since Act 62 of 2025, the GMCB no longer approves the statewide HIT Plan, provider connectivity criteria, or VITL's budget**, so oversight of the data infrastructure now runs through DVHA, the Steering Committee, and the State's contract with VITL.

## 15.3 **Workstream 1 — Hospital Transformation and Finances**

This is where Vermont's crisis is concentrated and where Act 68 asks AHS to do something new.

**The finances.** Oliver Wyman projected thirteen of fourteen hospitals in loss by 2028 without structural change. The GMCB's August 2026 staff analysis projected a system operating margin of −1.25% for FY2026 across the thirteen reporting hospitals. For FY2027, the Board cut commercial reimbursement rates 2.9% statewide and commercial net patient revenue 1.2%, with UVM Medical Center's commercial rates falling 4.4% — while UVM Health's CEO told the Board the medical center expects to lose roughly $75 million this year and at least as much next year.

**The plans.** All fourteen hospitals submitted final transformation plans in spring 2026. The $2 million Act 68 grant program drew applications from all thirteen eligible hospitals; by June 1, 2026, eleven had fully executed agreements. AHS's own summary of the plans is blunt: they reflect current initiatives, and AHS expects hospitals to add to them over time. In May 2026 the agency told VTDigger it could not yet total what the plans would save.

**The new job.** Act 68 requires AHS to facilitate and supervise hospital collaboration so that it qualifies for state-action immunity under federal and state antitrust law, to report each hospital decision's effect on system costs and premiums, and to attribute savings to each inefficiency reduced. Those are the duties of a system manager, not a grantor. They require three capabilities AHS is still building: a standing legal-supervisory process for collaboration agreements; financial analysts who can trace a service change at one hospital to its effect on neighbouring hospitals and on premiums; and a regional convening presence in each Hospital Service Area (Chapter 16).

**Where execution fails.** The most likely failure is not a bad plan. It is plans that each make sense for one hospital and add up to no system: duplicated services preserved, regional shifts never negotiated, and savings that cannot be counted. The defence is the Sec. 11b reporting itself — but only if AHS uses it as a management tool, not a compliance exercise.

## 15.4 **Workstream 2 — Price and Budget Regulation**

The GMCB has moved from case-by-case budget review toward rule-based price setting. Its February 2026 report to the Legislature documented commercial prices averaging roughly 250–300% of Medicare against a break-even near 136%, with outpatient imaging reaching 715–944% at some hospitals. In FY2026, its regulatory actions removed $230.65 million of commercial revenue — $104.30 million from the Act 55 drug cap, $94.58 million from budget orders, and $31.76 million from enforcement. Reference-based prices take effect in hospital FY2028, and global budgets follow as resources allow.

The execution risk sits at the boundary between the two agencies. A hospital that loses price must cut cost, and the cuts it chooses are transformation decisions that AHS is supposed to shape. Unless GMCB's budget orders and AHS's transformation plans are read side by side — hospital by hospital, in the same year — Vermont will regulate price in one building and redesign care in another, and hospitals will absorb the contradiction. The Medicaid hospital global budget that launched in January 2026 and was halted in July 2026, after CMS found it unapproved under the Global Commitment demonstration, is a reminder that payment reform also depends on a third party: federal approval.

## 15.5 **Workstream 3 — The Blueprint for Health**

The Blueprint is Vermont's primary care chassis, and every downstream goal — fewer avoidable ED visits, behavioral health integrated into primary care, global budgets that reward prevention — runs through it. As of November 15, 2025, 119 practices, 93% of the state's patient-centered medical homes, had signed on for the second year of the Mental Health Integration pilot, and 62.2 FTE of community health workers and mental-health and substance-use staff were providing care with that funding. The Blueprint's own evaluations have estimated returns of about $5.80 per $1 (2008–2013 data) and $3.00–$3.80 per $1 (2016).

The execution problem is funding, not evidence. Medicare's share of Blueprint payments ended with OneCare in December 2025, and the State proposed $10.8 million in interim Global Commitment funding to cover it for calendar 2026; the Mental Health Integration funding was extended for one year past the pilot. A primary care network financed one year at a time cannot recruit the staff the Strategic Plan will count on. Act 68's Steering Committee for Comprehensive Primary Health Care — called by the Blueprint's Executive Director, with a first meeting due by September 1, 2025 — is the statutory forum for fixing that, and the Strategic Plan is the place to commit to a permanent financing source.

## 15.6 **Workstream 4 — Clinical and Claims Data Integration**

Act 68 (Sec. 10) directed AHS to develop a Unified Health Data Space with the HIE Steering Committee. AHS's January 2026 report to the Legislature sets out its plan in three phases:

1. **Medicaid first.** The Medicaid Data Warehouse and Analytics Solution went live in early January 2026 with linked Medicaid claims, clinical, and social-needs data — a working pilot.
2. **Multi-payer by 2028–2029.** Add Medicare claims, statewide clinical data, and other statewide datasets.
3. **Commercial claims by 2029–2030.**

That sequence creates the most important timing gap in Vermont's reform. GMCB's reference-based prices and the first global budgets take effect in hospital FY2028 — and both are about *commercial* prices and *hospital* budgets. Under AHS's own plan, commercial claims will not join the integrated dataset until 2029–2030. Vermont will therefore set and police commercial prices before its integrated data covers commercial patients. GMCB has its own all-payer claims database, VHCURES, to fall back on, but VHCURES holds only about 60% of commercial claims (Chapter 4) and is not linked to clinical data. This is exactly the Technology-before-Economics problem of Chapter 1, now on a calendar.

Two further facts shape what is possible. The integration cannot begin before January 1, 2027, and only on a majority vote of all voting members of the HIE Steering Committee for specific approved uses — five of nine voting members under the committee's 2022 charter. And AHS's report justified the Medicare phase by the AHEAD model's requirements; with Vermont's July 2026 withdrawal from AHEAD, that rationale has gone, and the phasing needs to be re-decided on its merits.

**What to do.** Bring the commercial phase forward for the specific uses that price and budget regulation need. Prepare each § 9353 use proposal now — purpose, owner, privacy assessment, data-use agreement — so the Steering Committee can decide deliberately in early 2027. And publish the performance measures AHS's report itself proposed: match rates, data-quality scores, latency, number of approved uses, and reduction in provider reporting burden.

## 15.7 **Workstream 5 — The Rural Health Transformation Program**

The RHT Program is the capital behind the redesign: $195.1 million for federal FY2026 and $190.0 million for FY2027, each to be spent within about two years. By the State's October 30, 2026 list, Vermont had executed 242 grant and contract agreements worth $182.2 million across its five initiatives:

| Initiative | Executed agreements | Amount |
|---|---|---|
| Regionalization and Innovative Care Strategies | 103 | $87.5M |
| Clinically Integrated Network of Shared Services | 59 | $33.9M |
| Strengthening Primary Care | 55 | $29.8M |
| Health Care Workforce Development | 21 | $26.7M |
| Price Transparency and Insurance Competition | 2 | $3.1M |

*Figure 15.3 — Vermont RHT Year 1 executed agreements by initiative. Source: State of Vermont RHT recipient list (October 30, 2026); HTR RHT Year 1 Review.*

Specific purchases include AI-scribe grants ($5.2 million, 26 agreements), remote patient monitoring ($1.8 million), provider telehealth ($9.7 million), e-Consult expansion ($5.6 million), community paramedicine ($5.2 million), shared electronic health record adoption ($8.0 million), and a $15 million transformation-analytics contract. CMS counted $28.4 million as put to work by July 31, 2026.

The execution risk is that the money is spent on schedule and the system does not change. Two hundred forty-two agreements are 242 relationships to manage, and federal rules limit direct payments to providers to 15% of each award. The test of the RHT program is not obligation; it is whether each purchase is tied to a hospital transformation plan, a Blueprint priority, or a Strategic Plan goal — and whether anyone is measuring the result. The fund's last awards are for FY2030, while most of H.R. 1's Medicaid reductions come after it ends; whatever the RHT dollars build must be able to run without them.

## 15.8 **The Health Information Exchange: Living With a Designated Operator**

Every data ambition above runs partly through the Vermont Health Information Exchange, which the law reserves to one operator.

**What the law says.** Section 9352 requires that VITL "be designated in the Health Information Technology Plan to operate the exclusive statewide health information exchange network for this State," while adding that nothing in the chapter "shall impede local community providers from the exchange of electronic medical data." VITL operates under a sole-source contract administered by AHS, and its State matching funds come chiefly from the Health IT Fund — 0.199 of 1% of health insurance claims, about $3.5–4 million a year — which the Legislature has historically extended one or two years at a time. Since CMS certified the exchange in May 2022, the federal government pays 72% of its maintenance and operations.

**Vermont's own turnaround.** In 2016 the State Auditor reported that Vermont had paid VITL more than $38 million since 2005 without being able to assess its performance. The evaluation the Legislature ordered in Act 73 of 2017 found that only 19% of stakeholders thought the exchange met their needs, only 21% thought it critical that VITL manage it, and — because consent was opt-in — fewer than one in five Vermonters' records were accessible. The State then took ownership: the HIT Plan moved under DVHA with a State-led Steering Committee, and Act 53 of 2019 switched to an opt-out model from March 2020. By February 2025, VITL reported 98.9% of Vermonters sharing their data and 241 organizations contributing or accessing it. That turnaround came from governance and law, not software.

**Where the risks are.** A single designated operator is a single point of failure. Its designation limits the State's leverage at each contract renewal. And the participation rate hides coverage gaps: in February 2025 the designated mental-health and specialized service agencies contributed no data feeds (twelve accessed data), nor did nursing homes or commercial payers. For behavioral health and long-term care — where population-health management most needs data — the exchange is still thin.

**What a mature owner does.** Write data-completeness, timeliness, and matching-accuracy targets into the contract, by provider type, and publish them; guarantee that the data and patient index can transfer to a successor operator; build the second, State-controlled ingestion path Chapter 4 recommends; and fund the designated agencies' connections as data contributors, within 42 CFR Part 2's consent rules — a natural use of RHT funds.

## 15.9 **The People Who Carry It**

AHS's Act 119 review — over 325 participants plus about 950 comments and survey responses — described an agency of six departments, roughly 4,000 staff, and a multibillion-dollar budget, and named its own constraints: unclear decision-making authority and accountability; chronic staffing shortages and workload pressure; fragmented technology; and the reality that "only a limited number of issues can reach the executive level at any time." Act 68 added a statewide plan, monthly reporting, antitrust supervision, a grant program, and the data space to that agency, while the RHT program added 242 agreements to manage.

Four practices follow, none of which needs new law:

- **One named owner per workstream.** Hospital transformation, the Blueprint's financing, the data space, and RHT each need one accountable executive with authority to decide — the gap Act 119's participants named first.
- **Read regulation and transformation together.** A joint AHS–GMCB review, hospital by hospital, once a year, of budget orders against transformation plans.
- **Use the regional workforce Vermont already has.** The Blueprint's quality-improvement facilitators and Community Health Team leads already work in every Hospital Service Area; they are the natural base for the regional convening role Chapter 16 describes.
- **Protect executive attention.** A written escalation rule for which decisions must reach the Secretary, and how fast.

## 15.10 **A Vermont Delivery Compact**

Vermont already publishes most of what a delivery system needs: AHS's monthly Act 68 reports, GMCB's budget decisions, the RHT recipient list, the Blueprint's annual report, VITL's annual report, and ADS's monthly IT project inventory. What it lacks is one agreement that joins them. HTR proposes a **Vermont Delivery Compact** among AHS, GMCB, the Blueprint, and VITL, renewed with each Strategic Plan:

1. **One integrated roadmap** showing the dates in Figure 15.1 and the work that serves them.
2. **One public dashboard** joining hospital finances, transformation-plan progress, RHT spending against results, Blueprint financing, and data-space milestones.
3. **A decision-rights charter** naming who decides, who is consulted, and how long each step may take.
4. **A published catalogue of § 9353 data uses**, with each Steering Committee decision.
5. **Published exchange performance and portability terms.**

Each item builds on an obligation already in Vermont law. The pattern travels: any state can map decision rights from its own statutes, join its regulator's data with its transformation agency's, and make its data infrastructure's performance a matter of public contract.

## 15.12 **Implications for You**

**If you lead at AHS:** Name one owner for each of the five workstreams this quarter, and use the Sec. 11b reports to manage, not just to comply.

**If you lead at the GMCB:** Ask for each hospital's transformation plan beside its budget order, and say which uses of integrated commercial data your price regulation needs before 2029.

**If you sit on the HIE Steering Committee:** Ask for every § 9353 data-use proposal in writing, with an owner and a privacy assessment, before January 1, 2027.

**If you lead a hospital or designated agency:** Tie every RHT dollar you hold to a line in your transformation plan, and ask what it would take to send data to the exchange, not only to receive it.

**If you are a legislator:** Ask for one dashboard, and ask who owns each workstream by name.

## 15.13 **Key Concepts in This Chapter**

**Execution calendar.** The statutory and federal dates that govern Vermont's reform through 2030.

**State-action supervision.** AHS's Act 68 duty to supervise hospital collaboration so that it qualifies for antitrust immunity.

**§ 9353 data gate.** Unified Health Data Space integration may begin no earlier than January 1, 2027, on a majority vote of all voting HIE Steering Committee members, for approved uses only.

**Designated exchange operator.** VITL's statutory status as operator of the exclusive statewide health information exchange network.

**Vermont Delivery Compact.** HTR's proposed AHS–GMCB–Blueprint–VITL agreement joining deadlines, decision rights, and public reporting.
