# Chapter 15: The Execution Machinery — How Vermont's State Government Actually Delivers Health Reform

> *Written for AHS and ADS leaders, legislators on the health and technology committees, VITL and GMCB, hospital executives, and anyone in another state who has to turn a reform statute into working systems.*

Every chapter before this one asked what Vermont should do. This one asks who, inside state government, actually does it — and through which statutes, contracts, approvals, budgets, and people. The answer is less glamorous than a pillar framework and far more decisive. Reform statutes do not execute themselves. They are carried out by a small number of institutions with specific legal powers: the Agency of Human Services (AHS) and its Department of Vermont Health Access (DVHA); the Agency of Digital Services (ADS), which by law controls every information technology activity in the executive branch; the Green Mountain Care Board (GMCB); Vermont Information Technology Leaders (VITL), the nonprofit the law designates to run the state's health information exchange; a handful of legislative committees with oversight power; and two federal funders whose approval gates nearly every dollar. Whether Act 68 succeeds will be decided by how well these institutions work together between now and 2028. This chapter is a field guide to that machinery.

## 15.1 **The 2026–2028 Execution Crunch**

Vermont has never had to execute this much, this fast, through this few people. The obligations below are not aspirations. Each one carries a statutory or federal date, and several land in the same twelve months.

| Date | Obligation | Who carries it | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Monthly, Aug 2025 – Jan 2027 | Report outcome measures, savings attributed to each inefficiency, and transformation spending to the Health Reform Oversight Committee and Joint Fiscal Committee | AHS | Act 68, Sec. 11b |
| Jan 1, 2027 | Medicaid work requirements and six-month redeterminations for expansion adults | AHS / DVHA, on the legacy eligibility systems | H.R. 1; CMS interim final rule |
| Jan 1, 2027 (earliest) | Unified Health Data Space may begin integrating clinical, claims, and social-needs data — only on a majority vote of all voting members of the HIE Steering Committee, and only for the uses they approve | AHS + HIE Steering Committee | 18 V.S.A. § 9353 |
| Q1 2026 – 2028 (planned) | Replace Vermont Health Connect and the ACCESS eligibility system with VT-IES | AHS / DVHA + ADS | ADS 2025 Annual Report |
| Dec 31, 2027 | Global Commitment to Health demonstration expires; renewal must be negotiated | AHS | Chapter 16 |
| Jan 15, 2028 | Statewide Health Care Delivery Strategic Plan due to the Legislature | AHS + Health Care Delivery Advisory Committee | 18 V.S.A. § 9403 |
| Hospital FY2028 | Reference-based prices take effect; first global budgets as resources allow | GMCB | Act 68 |
| Annually, Feb 15 | AHS update on the Strategic Plan, with an evidence-based evaluation framework | AHS | 3 V.S.A. § 3027(b) |
| Annually, Jan 15 | VITL report on health IT progress; AHS report on the Unified Health Data Space (from 2027) | VITL; AHS | 18 V.S.A. §§ 9352(e), 9353(d) |

*Figure 15.1 — The execution calendar. Sources: Act 68 of 2025; 18 V.S.A. §§ 9352, 9353, 9403; 3 V.S.A. § 3027; CMS-2454-IFC; ADS 2025 Annual Report.*

Read the column labelled "who carries it." Almost every row runs through one agency — AHS — and the most consequential technology rows run through a second agency, ADS, that AHS does not control. That is the organizational fact this chapter is built around.

## 15.2 **Who Holds Which Lever**

Vermont splits authority over health reform across institutions that were designed at different times for different purposes. The map below is drawn from the statutes themselves.

| Institution | What the law gives it | What it cannot do alone | Statute |
|---|---|---|---|
| AHS, Office of Health Care Reform | The Director of Health Care Reform coordinates reform across all executive-branch agencies and with GMCB; AHS writes the Strategic Plan, supervises hospital collaboration, reports monthly | Approve its own IT projects; set hospital prices | 3 V.S.A. § 3027; Act 68 Secs. 8, 11a–11c |
| DVHA (inside AHS) | Runs Medicaid; owns the statewide Health Information Technology Plan, which must be revised annually and comprehensively every five years, in consultation with the HIE Steering Committee | Integrate data in the Unified Health Data Space without a Steering Committee vote | 18 V.S.A. § 9351 |
| HIE Steering Committee | Advises on the HIT Plan; decides by majority of all voting members whether, and for which uses, clinical, claims, and social-needs data may be integrated | Fund or operate anything | 18 V.S.A. §§ 9351, 9353 |
| VITL | Designated in the HIT Plan to operate "the exclusive statewide health information exchange network"; sets provider connectivity criteria; reports annually | Set its own budget free of the State's contract terms | 18 V.S.A. § 9352 |
| ADS | Reviews and approves all executive-branch IT activities; its Secretary is the State CIO and supervises all IT employees and contractors; requires independent expert review of new IT projects of $1 million or more | Set health policy or choose which programs to fund | 3 V.S.A. §§ 3301–3306 |
| GMCB | Sets hospital budgets and reference-based prices; certifies ACOs. Since July 1, 2025 it no longer approves the HIT Plan, provider connectivity criteria, or VITL's budget | Direct AHS's operations | Act 68; Act 62 of 2025 |
| Health Care Delivery Advisory Committee | Eighteen members chaired by the Secretary of Human Services; develops and maintains the Strategic Plan with AHS | Bind any agency | 18 V.S.A. § 9403a |
| Legislature | Health Reform Oversight Committee and Joint Fiscal Committee receive AHS's monthly reports; the Joint Information Technology Oversight Committee (six members, three per chamber) oversees state IT deployment and cybersecurity; JFO's IT consultant reviews large projects before funds are released | Manage projects | Act 68; 2 V.S.A. § 614 |
| Federal partners | CMS and FNS approve eligibility-system contracts and fund most Medicaid IT; CMS certification sets the HIE's federal match | Waive their own review cycles | Federal funding rules |

*Figure 15.2 — Decision rights in Vermont's health-reform execution system. Sources: statutes as cited; Act 62 of 2025 summary; JFO IT Consultant Update (April 22, 2026).*

Three features of this map shape everything that follows.

**The policy agency does not control its own technology.** Executive Order 06-17 created ADS in April 2017 and moved the executive branch's IT positions under it, while leaving most IT staff physically embedded in their agencies; Act 49 of 2019 put the arrangement in statute. AHS decides what it needs, but ADS approves the activity, supervises the people building it, and commissions the independent review. That is a deliberate check, and a good one, and it comes with transparency: ADS must publish a monthly inventory showing whether every major IT project is on time and on budget. But it means every health-reform deadline that depends on technology is a two-agency deadline.

**The data gate is a committee vote.** Act 68 did not let AHS integrate claims, clinical, and social-needs data on its own authority. Section 9353 requires a majority of all voting members of the HIE Steering Committee — not a majority of those present — and limits use of any integrated data to the specific purposes the committee approves. In the committee's 2022 charter that meant five of nine voting members. Whoever sits on that committee, and how well AHS prepares each proposed use, will decide whether the Unified Health Data Space becomes Vermont's analytic backbone or remains a plan.

**Oversight is layered, and each layer adds time.** A large health IT project passes through ADS's independent review, the federal agencies' review, the Legislature's JFO review, and JITOC before money is released. Section 15.4 shows the history that makes that care worth its cost.

## 15.3 **How a Health IT Investment Actually Moves Through Vermont Government**

The clearest way to understand the machinery is to follow one project through it. The Vermont Integrated Eligibility and Enrollment System (VT-IES) is the largest information technology project in Vermont state government: an approved implementation budget of $112.1 million and an estimated $119.6 million to operate over five years, $231.7 million in total, of which $80.0 million is state money. Human Services projects account for 56% of the estimated cost of all active state IT projects. VT-IES will replace Vermont Health Connect and every eligibility program on ACCESS — Medicaid, 3SquaresVT, Reach Up, Fuel Assistance, General and Emergency Assistance, and qualified health plan enrollment — in one system.

Its path so far:

1. **Planning and federal partnership (2022–2024).** Three requests for information to vendors (2022–2023), a 2023 cost-benefit analysis, and peer exchange with other states. The design is modular — customer portal, case management, rules engine — because federal funders now expect modularity.
2. **Federal review of the RFP.** Three federal reviews spanning nearly twelve months before the RFP could be posted in October 2024.
3. **Procurement.** Proposals evaluated from January 2025. AHS describes the full procurement phase, from RFP posting to contract execution, as spanning 24 months.
4. **Contract approval.** By April 2026 a preferred vendor had been selected; two federal agencies had to approve the contract before funding could flow, with three to four months of federal contract review anticipated.
5. **State oversight before execution.** The project must then undergo the CIO's independent review under 3 V.S.A. § 3303(d) — acquisition cost, architecture, implementation plan, cost-benefit model, alternatives, operating-cost impact, and security — followed by a Joint Fiscal Office IT review. As of April 2026, JFO had expected a request for state match funds that had not been submitted.

*Sources: ADS 2025 Annual Report; AHS, "VT-IES Modernization" (JITOC, November 12, 2025); JFO IT Consultant Update (April 22, 2026).*

Three practical lessons come out of this sequence.

**Federal time is the long pole, and it is predictable.** Twelve months of RFP review and several months of contract review are not delays to be complained about; they are fixed costs to be scheduled. A Vermont reform plan that assumes a new system in less time than the federal cycle allows is not ambitious. It is wrong.

**The reform calendar and the technology calendar collide.** VT-IES was planned to execute from early 2026 into 2028. The H.R. 1 work requirement and six-month redeterminations begin January 1, 2027 — in the middle of that window. Vermont will therefore implement the largest change to Medicaid eligibility in a generation on the legacy systems it is in the process of replacing. That is not a criticism of anyone; it is the situation. It means the state's most important near-term technology decision is not about the new system at all. It is about how much change the old one can safely absorb, and how many frontline eligibility staff will be needed to carry the rest by hand. (CMS's November 2026 offer of an optional six-month hold-harmless period, through June 30, 2027, is relief on enforcement, not on the build.)

**Knowledge is a balance-sheet risk.** AHS's own description of ACCESS is unusually candid: a 45-year-old system that fails CMS Medicaid IT standards, with complex programming that hinders timely updates, whose expert staff are retiring and whose history lacks sufficient documentation to ensure continuity. When the people who understand a system leave before it is replaced, the state loses the ability to change it at exactly the moment it most needs to. No budget line captures that risk, and every state with a decades-old eligibility system carries it.

## 15.4 **What Vermont's Own Record Teaches**

Vermont does not need case studies from other states to learn how health IT succeeds and fails. It has its own, and they explain why the oversight machinery looks the way it does.

**Vermont Health Connect (2013–2014): the cost of a weak owner.** The state's ACA exchange was built under a contract with CGI worth about $83 million. In August 2014 the State and CGI ended the relationship by mutual agreement, with neither assuming fault, after the State had paid about $57 million and agreed to pay $9.7 million more for completed work; Optum took over the remaining work. A state technical assessment at the time found that a project-specific cost and budget management plan did not exist. The lesson is that the State must own the business case, the budget, and the schedule rather than delegate them to a vendor, and those obligations are now written into law: ADS must keep a business case, life-cycle costs, risk plans, and status reports for every project its Enterprise Project Management Office manages, and publish a monthly inventory showing whether each project is on time and on budget.

**The health information exchange (2016–2020): governance, not software, was the problem.** In 2016 the State Auditor reported that Vermont had paid VITL more than $38 million since 2005 and was unable to adequately assess VITL's performance or demonstrate the value of the exchange. The evaluation the Legislature then ordered in Act 73 of 2017 found:

- 91% of stakeholders said the exchange was critical, but only 19% said it met their organization's needs, and only 21% said it was critical that VITL manage it;
- only 9% said the State had provided guidance and planning;
- because Vermont used an opt-in consent model, only 19.5% of Vermonters had even been asked for consent, so fewer than one in five patients' records were accessible — even though 96% of those asked said yes.

Vermont fixed this mainly with governance and law, not with new technology. The HIT Plan process moved to DVHA with a State-led HIE Steering Committee; the State's contract became the instrument for holding VITL accountable; and Act 53 of 2019 switched the exchange to an opt-out model, effective March 1, 2020. By February 2025, VITL reported that 98.9% of Vermonters were sharing their data, and that 241 organizations contributed or accessed data through the exchange. In May 2022 CMS certified the exchange, one of the first states to achieve that status, which raised the federal share of its operating costs from 25% to 72%.

**The Medicaid Data Warehouse and Analytics Solution: evidence that it can be done.** MDWAS, a $66.3 million implementation that gives Vermont its first centralized Medicaid data repository, reported green on scope, schedule, and budget in ADS's 2025 report; its data lake went live in October 2024 and the solution followed in early 2026.

The pattern across all three is the same. Vermont's health IT failures were failures of ownership, governance, and policy design; its successes came when the State owned the plan, wrote accountability into contracts and statute, and changed the policy (consent) that the technology could not fix.

## 15.5 **The Health Information Exchange: Living With a Designated Operator**

No part of Vermont's execution machinery is more misunderstood than the health information exchange, and no part matters more to the Unified Health Data Space.

**What the law actually says.** Section 9352 requires that VITL "be designated in the Health Information Technology Plan to operate the exclusive statewide health information exchange network for this State." The same subsection adds that nothing in the chapter "shall impede local community providers from the exchange of electronic medical data." The exclusivity is therefore narrower than it sounds: VITL is the only *statewide* exchange network, and providers remain free to exchange data directly with each other. VITL operates under a sole-source contract administered by AHS, and since Act 62 of 2025 the GMCB no longer reviews its budget; the State's leverage now runs almost entirely through the HIT Plan and that contract.

**How it is paid for.** Under CMS certification, the federal government pays 72% of the exchange's maintenance and operations and 86–90% of development; the State's share comes chiefly from the Health IT Fund — 0.199 of 1% of health insurance claims, about $3.5–4 million a year — a revenue source the Legislature has historically extended one or two years at a time. ADS projects the exchange's five-year maintenance and operations at about $11.2 million.

**What a designated operator does well.** One operator means one longitudinal record per Vermonter, one patient-matching index, one consent model, and one set of feeds to public health, the Blueprint, and the Chronic Care Initiative. The 98.9% participation figure is possible partly because there is only one exchange to participate in.

**Where the risks are.**

- *Concentration.* A single designated operator is a single point of failure — operational, financial, and cyber. The 2020 ransomware attack on UVM Health Network (Chapter 4) showed what a statewide data outage costs.
- *Bargaining power.* When the statute requires the State to designate one operator, the State's ability to walk away is limited to rewriting the statute. That shifts leverage from the buyer to the supplier at every contract renewal.
- *Coverage gaps hiding behind a high participation rate.* In February 2025, the designated mental-health and specialized service agencies contributed no data feeds to the exchange (twelve accessed data), nursing homes contributed none, and no commercial payer contributed data. "98.9% of Vermonters sharing" describes consent, not completeness. For behavioral health — where federal 42 CFR Part 2 rules restrict substance-use treatment records — the gap is precisely where population-health management most needs data.
- *Dependence for the State's flagship data project.* The Unified Health Data Space is being built on top of the exchange. If the State's core analytic asset depends on one external intermediary, the State's transformation timeline depends on that intermediary's capacity.

**What a mature State does about it.** None of these risks requires ending the designation. Each requires the State to act like the owner it now is:

1. **Write performance into the contract, not the statute.** Data-completeness targets by provider type (with behavioral health and long-term care named), timeliness standards, matching-accuracy measures, and uptime — reported publicly, with payment tied to them, as the 2017 evaluation recommended.
2. **Guarantee portability.** Contract terms that make the data, the master patient index, and the interfaces transferable to a successor operator, so that the designation can change without the data having to be rebuilt.
3. **Build the second ingestion path.** As Chapter 4 recommends, the State should be able to receive core data feeds directly, so that its own analytic pipeline does not depend on a single intermediary.
4. **Prepare the Steering Committee decisions now.** Each proposed use of integrated data under § 9353 should arrive with a written use case, a privacy assessment, a data-use agreement, and a named owner, so that the committee can say yes deliberately rather than no by default.
5. **Fund the behavioral health connection.** Bringing the designated agencies into the exchange as data contributors — within Part 2's consent rules — is the single most valuable completeness gain available, and a candidate for Rural Health Transformation funds.

## 15.6 **The Human Side: What the Machinery Asks of People**

Statutes and systems are carried by people, and AHS says plainly that its people are stretched. The Act 119 review — 325 participants in interviews, focus groups, and discussions, plus about 950 comments and survey responses — described an agency of six departments, roughly 4,000 staff, and a multibillion-dollar budget, and named its problems directly: a need for clearer decision-making authority and accountability; chronic staffing shortages, workload pressures, and limited professional development; fragmented and outdated technology; and leadership constraints, since "only a limited number of issues can reach the executive level at any time."

Act 68 added to that load without adding a matching organization. AHS must now write a statewide plan, report monthly to two legislative committees, supervise hospital collaboration closely enough that it qualifies for state-action immunity from antitrust law, run a grant program, retain OneCare's data capabilities, and develop the Unified Health Data Space — while preparing for H.R. 1 and replacing its eligibility systems. Several of those tasks require skills that a benefits agency has never needed at scale:

| New competence | Why Act 68 requires it | Where it sits today |
|---|---|---|
| Antitrust-grade supervision | Sec. 11a: hospital collaboration must be "facilitated and supervised" by AHS to qualify for state-action immunity | No dedicated unit; carried by the Office of Health Care Reform |
| Savings attribution and evaluation | Sec. 11b: savings "attributable to each inefficiency reduced"; § 3027: evidence-based evaluation framework | Depends on the analytics contract and MDWAS |
| Data governance | § 9353: use-by-use approval, privacy assessment, data-use agreements | HIE Steering Committee, staffed by AHS's HIE unit |
| Vendor and contract management | VITL sole-source contract; VT-IES prime vendor; analytics vendor | DVHA and AHS Central Office, with ADS |
| Federal negotiation | Global Commitment renewal by Dec 31, 2027; CMS and FNS approvals | AHS leadership |
| Change management for frontline staff | Six-month redeterminations and work requirements on legacy systems | Economic Services and DVHA eligibility staff |

*Figure 15.3 — Competences Act 68 asks AHS to have. Sources: Act 68 of 2025, Secs. 9–12, 15; 18 V.S.A. § 9353; AHS Act 119 Report (Nov. 1, 2025); JFO IT Update (April 2026).*

Four people-centred practices follow, none of which requires new legislation.

**Name a single accountable executive for each statutory deliverable.** The Act 119 finding that decision rights are unclear is the most important sentence in the report. Every row of Figure 15.1 should have one named owner whose job description says so.

**Treat institutional knowledge as an asset to be inventoried.** For ACCESS, Vermont Health Connect, and the Medicaid systems, the State should know which people hold which critical knowledge, document it while they are still employed, and pair successors with them before they retire.

**Protect executive attention.** If only a few issues can reach the Secretary at once, the reform program needs a written escalation rule: which decisions must go up, which are delegated, and how quickly. Most stalled projects are waiting for a decision, not for a deliverable.

**Bring frontline eligibility staff into design.** The people who will carry H.R. 1's paperwork through 2027 know which manual workarounds the legacy systems require. Their knowledge is the cheapest risk reduction available.

## 15.7 **A Vermont Delivery Compact**

Vermont already has the raw materials for something few states have assembled: a statutory decision-rights map, a public monthly inventory of every major IT project (3 V.S.A. § 3303(e)), monthly legislative reporting on transformation outcomes (Act 68, Sec. 11b), an inventory of automated decision systems (3 V.S.A. § 3305), and a single statewide health information exchange. What it lacks is the agreement that binds them into one delivery system. HTR proposes a **Vermont Delivery Compact** — an operating agreement among AHS, ADS, GMCB, and VITL, renewed with each Strategic Plan:

1. **One integrated roadmap.** A single schedule that shows the policy deadlines in Figure 15.1 and the technology milestones that serve them on the same page, with the dependencies drawn.
2. **One public delivery dashboard.** Built from data the State already publishes — ADS's project inventory and AHS's Sec. 11b reports — so that legislators and the public see policy progress and system progress together.
3. **A decision-rights charter.** For each deliverable: who decides, who must be consulted, who approves the technology, and how long each step is allowed to take.
4. **A data-use catalogue for § 9353.** Every proposed use of integrated data, with its owner, purpose, privacy assessment, and the Steering Committee's decision, published.
5. **Exchange performance and portability terms.** The contract standards in Section 15.5, published with the results.
6. **A knowledge-continuity register.** Critical systems, the people who understand them, and the plan to transfer that knowledge.
7. **An automated-decision register for health.** The § 3305 inventory, applied to every algorithm used in eligibility, risk stratification, or care management — including whether it has been independently tested for bias.

None of these items requires new money on the scale of the reforms it would protect, and each builds on an obligation already in Vermont law. Other states can adopt the same pattern with their own statutes: map decision rights from the law itself, publish one dashboard that joins policy and technology, and make the health information exchange's performance a matter of public contract rather than private trust.

## 15.9 **Implications for You**

**If you lead at AHS or ADS:** Put one name next to every row of Figure 15.1 this quarter, and publish the integrated roadmap.

**If you sit on the HIE Steering Committee:** Ask for every § 9353 data-use proposal in writing, with a privacy assessment and a named owner, well before January 1, 2027.

**If you are a legislator:** Ask for the State's delivery dashboard in one document, not in separate AHS, ADS, and VITL reports, and ask what the contingency is if the eligibility build and H.R. 1 collide.

**If you lead a hospital or designated agency:** The exchange is only as complete as its contributors. Ask what it would take to send data, not only to receive it.

**If you work in another state:** Map your own decision rights from your statutes before you design anything else.

## 15.10 **Key Concepts in This Chapter**

**Agency of Digital Services (ADS).** The executive-branch agency that reviews and approves all state IT activities, supervises IT staff, and requires independent review of new IT projects of $1 million or more (3 V.S.A. §§ 3301–3303).

**Independent expert review.** The § 3303(d) review of acquisition cost, architecture, implementation plan, cost-benefit model, alternatives, operating impact, and security.

**Designated exchange operator.** VITL's statutory status as operator of the exclusive statewide health information exchange network (18 V.S.A. § 9352).

**§ 9353 data gate.** The rule that Unified Health Data Space integration may begin no earlier than January 1, 2027, and only on a majority vote of all voting members of the HIE Steering Committee for approved uses.

**State-action supervision.** AHS's Act 68 duty to facilitate and supervise hospital collaboration so that it qualifies for state-action immunity under antitrust law.

**Vermont Delivery Compact.** HTR's proposed AHS–ADS–GMCB–VITL operating agreement joining policy deadlines, technology milestones, decision rights, and public reporting.
