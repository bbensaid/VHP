## 15.8 **The Critical-Path Component: The Unified Health Data Space**

Run the sequencing test of Section 15.7 on Vermont's portfolio and one component sits upstream of almost everything else: a unified view of each person's care. That view joins what was paid for (claims), what happened clinically (clinical records), the social conditions that shape health (social determinants), and the mental-health and substance-use care that so often drives cost (behavioral health). Reference-based prices, global budgets, the Blueprint's primary care payments, the Rural Health Transformation investments, and the Strategic Plan's affordability benchmarks all depend on it. Act 68 gave this component a name, the Unified Health Data Space (UHDS), and a statute, 18 V.S.A. § 9353. Vermont's experience shows why unified data belongs at the head of the critical path, and why the institutions and laws around it can hold back a reform as surely as any shortage of money.

### 15.8.1 **Why value-based care cannot be managed on claims or clinical data alone**

Every form of value-based payment makes the same promise: pay for outcomes and total cost rather than for units of service. Keeping that promise requires evidence that no single data source holds. Each source answers a different question, and value-based care needs every answer for the same person.

- **Claims** record what was billed and paid: who was seen, where, for what diagnosis, at what price, across every setting a payer covers. They are the basis for total cost of care, patient attribution, and utilization trends. But a claim records that a blood-pressure check happened, not what the blood pressure was.
- **Clinical data** from electronic health records, laboratories, and registries records what happened to the patient: the readings, the results, the medications. It is the basis for outcome measures. But each record sees only the care delivered inside one organization, and it carries no price.
- **Social-determinants data** (housing, food security, transportation) explains why two patients with the same diagnosis have very different costs and outcomes. Without it, risk adjustment penalizes the providers who serve the hardest-pressed communities.
- **Behavioral-health data** from designated agencies, substance-use treatment, and mental-health providers covers the conditions that most often drive avoidable emergency visits and readmissions. Much of it is protected by separate consent rules, and much of it sits outside both claims and hospital records.

Value-based care needs all four, unified at the level of the individual person:

| What a value-based arrangement must do | Claims | Clinical | Social and behavioral | Without unified data |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Attribute patients and measure total cost of care | Every paid service, every setting | Severity and acuity | Drivers of avoidable cost | Cost is measured, but not what it bought |
| Pay on quality (blood-pressure control, diabetes control, screening) | Defines the eligible population | Supplies the result | Explains gaps in reach | Measures are built by hand from charts, late and incomplete |
| Adjust for risk fairly | Diagnoses over time | Results and severity | Social and behavioral risk | Providers serving sicker, poorer patients look worse than they are |
| Detect stinting under a fixed budget | Care that stopped | Health that declined | Who lost access | A global budget can save money by doing less, and no one can tell |
| Attribute savings to a specific change | Before-and-after cost | Before-and-after outcomes | Whether the right people benefited | Savings are claimed, not demonstrated |

*Figure 15.6 — Why value-based care requires unified claims, clinical, social, and behavioral-health data. Sources: AHS, Advantages and Disadvantages of Health Care Data Integration (Jan. 14, 2026); Act 68 of 2025.*

The gap is measurable. A study cited in AHS's January 2026 report found that half of Medicaid members' cholesterol, diabetes, and kidney screenings, and their influenza vaccinations, were documented in clinical records but missing from claims. A program paid on claims-based quality measures would have scored those providers as failing at care they had delivered. The reverse is equally true: a program measured on one hospital's clinical records cannot see the emergency visit at another hospital the following week. AHS's report also notes that health care organizations reporting "hybrid" quality measures, which require claims and clinical data together, combine them today by manual chart abstraction.

The stinting row matters most for Vermont. Act 68 moves hospitals to global budgets, a fixed sum regardless of volume. That design removes the incentive to over-treat and creates the opposite risk. The only safeguard is the ability to see, quickly and across every setting, whether access and outcomes held up after the budget was set. Claims alone would show that spending fell; clinical records alone would show one organization's patients; neither would show whether Vermonters as a whole were worse off. Only unified data can.

### 15.8.2 **What Vermont has built, and when the pieces arrive**

The mandate for unified data is older than Act 68. Act 167 of 2022 directed the HIE Steering Committee to "create one health record for each person" that integrates claims, clinical, mental-health and substance-use, and social-determinants data, and to plan a merger of VHCURES claims with the exchange's clinical data. Act 68 then set the rules in § 9353: AHS develops the UHDS with the HIE Steering Committee; integration may not begin before January 1, 2027; it requires a favorable vote of a majority of all voting committee members; and unified data may be used only for the uses that vote approves.

Some foundations exist. Opt-out consent since 2020 means 98.9% of Vermonters' data is shared through the health information exchange (February 2025). A rebuilt patient-matching index raised record matching from about 65% to more than 95%, the precondition for joining any two sources about the same person. Since January 2026 the State's Medicaid data warehouse (MDWAS) has held linked Medicaid claims, clinical, and social-needs data: the first working slice of unified data in Vermont.

The calendar is the problem. AHS's own plan adds Medicare and statewide clinical data in 2028–2029 and commercial claims in 2029–2030. The GMCB's reference-based prices take effect in hospital FY2028. Vermont will regulate commercial hospital prices for one to two years before its unified data can see a commercially insured patient. In portfolio terms, downstream components are scheduled ahead of the gate they depend on. That is the error the sequencing test exists to catch, and it is the dependency behind risk R-01 in the portfolio risk register (Figure 15.8).

### 15.8.3 **Where the law itself stands in the way**

Three features of the statute, not of its execution, slow the unification the reform needs.

**The insurer mandate was dropped.** The version of Act 68's data section that passed the Senate required health insurers to "provide clinical and claims data to the Agency of Human Services as directed by the Agency." The enacted law does not contain that sentence. AHS therefore has no power to require commercial claims for the UHDS. It can only negotiate access to VHCURES, the GMCB's all-payer claims database, which operates under its own use restrictions and captures only about 60% of commercial claims because self-insured employer plans cannot be compelled to report (Chapter 4). The phase price regulation needs most is the phase the law makes hardest.

**The money stops at Medicaid.** Federal match pays roughly 90% of Medicaid data development. Work serving commercial and Medicare populations earns none, and § 9353 names no State source for it. The multi-payer phases, the ones that turn a Medicaid dataset into unified data for the whole state, are unfunded by design.

**The gate rewards inaction.** Requiring a majority of all voting members, rather than of those present, is a sound privacy protection. It also means that every vacant or absent seat counts as "no." And the statute sets no priority among uses, so nothing guarantees that the uses the reform depends on (price and global-budget monitoring, quality payment, behavioral-health integration) are the first to be approved.

### 15.8.4 **The designated monopoly**

The most consequential structural choice in Vermont's health data system predates Act 68 by two decades. Section 9352 requires that VITL "be designated in the Health Information Technology Plan to operate the exclusive statewide health information exchange network for this State." The operative word is exclusive. Vermont did not procure an exchange operator; it legislated one. Every phase of the UHDS that draws clinical data, and every attempt to unify clinical data with claims, social, and behavioral-health data, runs through that monopoly.

Monopolies behave like monopolies, and the public record shows it.

- **Accountability came late.** In 2016 the State Auditor reported that Vermont had paid VITL more than $38 million since 2005 without being able to assess its performance. The evaluation ordered by Act 73 of 2017 found that only 19% of stakeholders thought the exchange met their needs.
- **Progress, when it came, was driven from outside.** The turnaround of 2019–2022 followed State intervention: State ownership of the Health Information Technology Plan, a State-led Steering Committee, and the Legislature's switch to opt-out consent. It did not come from competitive pressure, because there is none.
- **Modern capability lags.** The HIE Strategic Plan update of November 2024 rated the goal "Implement FHIR APIs," the open interfaces on which modern applications, analytics, and patient access are built, at 40% complete and only partially met. Meanwhile Vermont is funding e-Consult platforms, closed-loop referral systems, a shared electronic health record, and transformation analytics through the RHT program as separate purchases, where a modern exchange would let each of them build on one unified data foundation.
- **Reported coverage overstates what flows.** The same 2024 update reported the goal of connecting all designated mental-health agencies as complete. In February 2025, VITL's own figures showed those agencies contributing no data feeds: twelve accessed data; none sent it. Nursing homes and commercial payers sent none either. These are exactly the behavioral-health, long-term-care, and claims data that unified, whole-person measurement needs.
- **Oversight has narrowed.** Until 2025 the GMCB reviewed VITL's budget. Act 62 of 2025 removed that review, leaving the contracting department as the main check on a sole-source vendor funded by a tax on health insurance claims and by federal match.

None of this requires bad faith by anyone. It is what a statutory monopoly produces: no competitor to set a benchmark for price or performance, no alternative at contract renewal to give the State leverage, and an incentive to protect the franchise rather than to open the data for others to build on. Innovation in health data now happens mostly at the application layer (analytics, care coordination, referral, patient access), and an exclusive operator that is also the gatekeeper to the data decides how much of that layer anyone else is allowed to build.

Vermont does not need to replace its exchange to fix this. It needs to change the terms on which the exchange operates:

| Problem | Structural fix |
|---|---|
| Exclusive designation in statute | Amend § 9352 so that the State owns the core data assets (patient index, consent registry, clinical repository) and contracts for their operation on fixed terms, competitively at each renewal |
| No benchmark for price or performance | Contract targets for completeness, timeliness, and matching accuracy by provider type, published quarterly, with financial consequences |
| A closed data layer | Open, standards-based (FHIR) access for any certified application, so that e-Consult, referral, analytics, and patient tools compete on one shared, unified foundation |
| Behavioral-health and long-term-care data missing | Funded contributor connections for designated agencies and nursing homes, within 42 CFR Part 2 consent rules |
| Lock-in at renewal | Guaranteed transfer of data, patient index, and consent records to any successor operator |
| Single point of failure | A second, State-controlled ingestion path, starting with the clinical data already flowing into MDWAS (Chapter 4) |
| Narrowed oversight | Independent annual review of the operator's budget and performance by a body other than the contracting department |

*Figure 15.7 — The exchange designation: structural problems and fixes. Sources: 18 V.S.A. §§ 9351–9353; Vermont State Auditor (2016); Evaluation of Vermont Health Information Technology Activities (2018); HIE Strategic Plan 2024 Update (Nov. 18, 2024); VITL (Feb. 4, 2025); Act 62 of 2025.*

### 15.8.5 **What it takes to deliver it**

A unified data utility is not a project with an end date; it is permanent infrastructure, and the portfolio must treat it that way. It needs one accountable owner with authority over the whole data stack, from the exchange contract and the unified dataset to governance and funding. It needs data stewards responsible for the completeness of each source (claims, clinical, social, behavioral), privacy and legal staff who can bring every proposed use to the Steering Committee in writing with a privacy assessment, and a contract manager with the standing to enforce performance terms on the exchange operator. The specialized build work can be contracted. Ownership cannot be: the State's own staff must be able to run the utility, and hold its vendors to account, by the time the build contracts end.

### 15.8.6 **What the portfolio should do now**

Applied to the UHDS, the sequencing test gives four instructions:

1. **Treat commercial data as the critical path.** A VHCURES data-access agreement between AHS and the GMCB for approved UHDS uses before FY2028 prices take effect, and restoration of the insurer-reporting requirement the Senate originally passed.
2. **Fund the phases that make the data unified.** A named State source for the multi-payer and commercial phases, and use of the time-limited RHT funds for the one-time work federal Medicaid match will not pay for, beginning with behavioral-health and long-term-care contributor connections.
3. **Set the first uses by reform priority.** Before the first § 9353 vote in 2027, publish the priority uses: price and global-budget monitoring, quality payment, behavioral-health integration, and RHT outcome measurement.
4. **Open the exchange.** Begin the statutory and contractual changes in Figure 15.7, so that the clinical data the UHDS depends on comes from an operator that competes on performance and opens its data to others.

> **BEYOND VERMONT** — Every state attempting value-based payment faces the same dependency: total cost lives in claims, outcomes live in clinical records, and the causes of both live in social and behavioral-health data that neither captures. No one source can show whether a reform is working; only unified data can. Vermont's lessons generalize: put the unification mandate, the insurer-reporting requirement, and the funding in the same statute; sequence price regulation behind the unified data that can see its effects; and never grant a single operator an exclusive franchise over the data everyone else must build on. Own the core assets publicly, contract for their operation competitively, and open the data layer to innovation.
