## Claims checked against their own source

### MISMATCHES

**M1 — Figure T.1's caption claims a range the table does not cover.** [445] captions it
"Vermont Healthcare Transformation Timeline: **2022–2035**"; the table's final row is
**FY2030 (Oct 2029)**. Nothing in the table reaches past 2030.
*Fix:* caption → "Vermont Healthcare Transformation Timeline: 2022–2030".

**M2 — RBP effective date: Figure 2.4 contradicts Figure 2.3, Figure T.1 and the prose.**
Figure 2.4's Vermont row [530] reads "Methodology set by rule FY2027, **effective FY2028**."
But Figure 2.3 [522] says "FY2027 (Oct 2026) — Reference-based pricing mandatory for all
commercial payers"; Figure T.1 [444] says "FY2027 (Oct 2026) — RBP mandatory for all Vermont
hospitals"; [527] says the act directs maximum amounts "not later than hospital fiscal year
2027"; [560] says RBP is "effective FY2027". Four places say FY2027, one says FY2028.
*Fix:* Figure 2.4 Vermont row → "Methodology set by rule during FY2026; maximum allowable
payments mandatory from FY2027." (If the FY2028 date is in fact correct in the GMCB Feb 2026
report, the other four instances are wrong and this is a five-place correction — verify
against the source before changing either side.)

**M3 — Two different effective dates for Act 68 global budgets inside the same table, both
labelled FY2028.** Figure 2.3 [522] contains both "January 2028 — Act 68 **commercial**
hospital global budgets take effect (FY2028)" and "FY2028 (Oct 2027) — Global hospital budgets
for non-Critical Access Hospitals". Figure T.1 [444] carries the same pair. FY2028 is defined
elsewhere in both tables as beginning **Oct 2027**, so "January 2028 … (FY2028)" is internally
inconsistent with the table's own fiscal-year convention. Figure 2.3 additionally lists
January 2028 **before** FY2028 (Oct 2027), breaking chronological order in an
explicitly chronological figure.
*Fix:* collapse to one row — "FY2028 (Oct 2027) — Global hospital budgets for non-Critical
Access Hospitals; commercial global budgets phase in January 2028" — and reorder so dates
ascend.

**M4 — "200% of Medicare" is attributed to statute; the chapter's own account of the statute
says the level is set by rule.** [560] (Implications for You): "RBP at 200% of Medicare is not
a theoretical target; it is a **statutory mandate** effective FY2027." But [527] says Act 68
"directs GMCB to establish, **by rule**, maximum amounts", [529] says only that "prices must be
based on a percentage of Medicare reimbursement", and [497] shows 200% as **Oliver Wyman's
recommendation** ("ideally at 200% of Medicare or less"). Figure T.1 [444] compounds this with
"≤200% Medicare target" in the FY2027 row.
*Fix:* [560] → "RBP is not a theoretical target; it is a statutory mandate effective FY2027,
and the Oliver Wyman benchmark GMCB is working from is 200% of Medicare or less." Change
Figure T.1's cell to "RBP mandatory for all Vermont hospitals; GMCB sets the maximum by rule
(Oliver Wyman benchmark: ≤200% Medicare)."

**M5 — The meeting-type breakdown does not add to the stated total, and the community-meeting
attendance alone exceeds the stated participant total.** [491]: "over 230 meetings … more than
3,100 participants from over 100 organizations. Meeting types included 57 sessions with
hospital leadership and boards, 45 with state partners, **50 public HSA-level community
meetings averaging 68 participants each**, 35 provider meetings, and 15 sessions … for diverse
populations." 57+45+50+35+15 = **202**, not 230+ (defensible — "included" is non-exhaustive).
But 50 × 68 = **3,400 participants in the community meetings alone**, which already exceeds the
"more than 3,100 participants" total that is supposed to cover all 230 meetings.
*Fix:* either drop "averaging 68 participants each", or state the total as "more than 3,400
participants", or reword to "the 50 public HSA meetings drew roughly 3,400 attendances" —
attendances rather than unique participants would reconcile it. Verify against the Oliver
Wyman report before choosing.

**M6 — Brattleboro's COE count does not match its own listed specialties.** Figure 2.2 [510]
gives Brattleboro "COE Count 6" but lists **five** specialties (acute general surgery, cancer
surgery, geriatric care, orthopedics, robotic surgery). Every other small-count row in the
table lists exactly its count (CVMC 5/5, Northwestern 4/4, NVRH 4/4, Rutland 5/5, Springfield
2/2, Copley 2/2, Mt. Ascutney 1/1); only the two large rows (UVMMC 16, SVMC 9) abbreviate.
Brattleboro is the sole small row where count ≠ items listed.
*Fix:* either correct the count to 5 or add the missing sixth specialty from the source.

**M7 — "three primary sources of direct savings" vs six sources listed.** [517]: "Reducing
exceedingly high administrative costs … was identified as **one of the three primary sources of
direct savings**." The callout [518], which itemises the same $400M direct-savings estimate,
lists **six** sources.
*Fix:* [517] → "one of the principal sources of direct savings" (or name which three are
primary and mark the other three as secondary in the callout).

**M8 — The $400M callout contradicts itself on whether the figure is five-year or annual.**
[518] opens "more than $400 million in direct savings **over five years**" and closes "$400M
… is the **recurring savings** that structural transformation is projected to unlock, **year
after year**, once implemented." These cannot both be true.
*Fix:* close with "…it is a five-year cumulative figure — roughly $80 million a year once
implemented — and it recurs only if the structural change is sustained."

**M9 — Vermont's global-budget attempts are dated outside the stated window, and the stated
common cause is contradicted by one of the four entries.** [535]: "Vermont has attempted global
budget design **four times in the past decade**: the GMCB net patient revenue cap in **2012**,
the Rutland Regional pilot proposed in 2014 (**never implemented**), the Vermont All-Payer ACO
Model from 2017 to 2025, and the current Act 167/Act 68 mandate." Two problems: (a) 2012 is
fourteen years before the present, not within the past decade; (b) "Each prior attempt
foundered on the same obstacle: voluntary participation" cannot be true of an attempt the same
sentence says was **never implemented**, nor of a regulatory revenue cap.
*Fix:* "Vermont has attempted global budget design four times since 2012 … Two of the three
prior attempts foundered on the same obstacle — voluntary participation; the Rutland pilot
never reached implementation at all."

**M10 — Figure 2.4 does not support the "all confirm this" claim made of it.** [552]: "Oregon's
RBP experience, Montana's experience, and Maryland's global budget model **all confirm** this"
— "this" being that price control without volume control is insufficient. Figure 2.4's own
Oregon row reports **success** (">$107.5M savings in first 27 months. All 24 affected hospitals
remained in-network") and says nothing about volume leakage; the Montana row attributes its
fragility to the **absence of a statutory mandate**, not to uncontrolled volume. Only the
Maryland row (fixed annual revenue with volume expectations) actually speaks to volume.
*Fix:* "Maryland's global budget model, which pairs standardized prices with volume
expectations, is the only one of these programs that closes the volume channel — Oregon's and
Montana's caps leave it open."

**M11 — The chapter's own Figure 2.1 says the primary-care shortage is a misperception, while
the surrounding prose treats workforce shortage as established fact, without ever reconciling
them.** Figure 2.1 [485]: "If primary care providers were supported to see 3 patients per hour,
there are enough for well into the future. **HRSA recognizes no Health Profession Shortage
Areas in Vermont.** The problem is productivity and practice model, not pure headcount."
Against that: [508] justifies regionalization by "population density and **physician
shortage**"; [548] lists "**workforce shortage**" among the structural problems Vermont's model
generalizes to; [544] describes an RHT goal as addressing "the conditions that drive
**workforce shortage**"; [477] cites "licensure complexity impedes provider recruitment"; [553]
concludes housing/transportation failures "will fail to achieve the **workforce stability**…".
The distinctions that would reconcile these (primary care vs specialists; productivity vs
headcount; retention vs supply) are available in the text but never drawn.
*Fix:* add one sentence after [487]: "The primary-care correction is specific — it is a
productivity and practice-model problem, not a headcount problem. The shortages this chapter
treats as structural are in specialist coverage and in **retention**, which the systems map
traces to housing and cost of living rather than to the size of the provider pool." Then make
[508] read "specialist coverage" rather than "physician shortage".

**M12 — "Act 68 is as effective as it is" asserts realized effectiveness for a statute whose
operative provisions have not taken effect.** [550]: "Vermont's Act 68 **is as effective as it
is** because it was preceded by Act 167's … diagnostic process." Per the chapter's own
timelines, RBP begins FY2027, global budgets FY2028–FY2030, and [563] says "Vermont will have
answers to these questions **by 2030**."
*Fix:* "Vermont's Act 68 is as **durable** as it is — surviving enactment with its mandates
intact — because it was preceded by …".

**M13 — Act 51 and the promised "enforcement architecture" are announced and never

*Fix:* align [442] to the chapter as built — "the diagnostic mandate, the planning mandate that
bridged them, and the operational mandate" — or add a short §2.5.3 on Act 51's transformation
planning pilots and a §2.7.4 on enforcement. The framing sentence should not promise a section
that does not exist.

**M14 — The realistic-scenario callout mislabels the assumption set.** [467] defines the

the scenario's main driver and making the two scenarios look closer than they are (5% vs 7-8%,
rather than 5% vs 10%-on-labor).
*Fix:* "Under realistic assumptions (10% labor, 7-8% other expense growth)".

## Other issues

### Internal contradictions (beyond the MISMATCH list)

**C1 — Competing "most important contribution of the Oliver Wyman report" claims.** [465] "The
financial projections … are **the most important data in the entire Vermont reform story**" vs
[476] "**Perhaps the most analytically powerful contribution** of the Oliver Wyman report is
its systems map" vs [484] "**One of Oliver Wyman's most valuable contributions** is a
systematic correction of widely held misconceptions" vs [539] "Oliver Wyman's state-level
recommendations are **the most detailed public blueprint available**". The hedges on three of
the four make this survivable, but [465] and [476] are three paragraphs apart in the same
section-cluster and read as a direct conflict.
*Fix:* keep one unhedged superlative (the financial projections are the strongest candidate,
since the chapter's argument rests on them) and reduce [476] to "The report's systems map is
its most operationally useful exhibit" or similar non-competing framing.

**C2 — "Sequencing-neutral" vs the chapter's sequencing thesis.** [442] argues "the Policy
pillar has to come first in the five-pillar sequence"; [457] calls the three imperatives "a
**sequencing-neutral** checklist"; [478] says the imperatives "are formulated precisely to

consistent with each other but sit oddly against the book's sequencing doctrine. Probably
defensible (the imperatives are a diagnostic checklist, not an implementation order) but the
word "sequencing-neutral" in a book whose central claim is about sequencing invites the
reading that sequence does not matter.
*Fix:* [457] → "an **order-independent** checklist for diagnosing whether a state's reform
agenda is structural or merely additive".

**C3 — Maryland dated twice, differently.** [441] and [446] both anchor to "Maryland's
all-payer model **in the 1970s**"; Figure 2.4 [530] lists "Maryland (**2014–present**)". Both
