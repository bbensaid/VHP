## Section A — Updated Reform Cascade Introduction (replace existing paragraph)

The sequence has a clear internal logic that unfolds across six legislative and institutional steps between 2022 and 2025. Act 167 of 2022 was a diagnostic mandate: commission a rigorous, public, community-grounded diagnosis of what is actually wrong. Act 51 of 2023 was a technology infrastructure mandate: build the data foundation — expanded VHCURES reporting, strengthened GMCB financial data collection — that price transparency, global budget attribution, and total cost of care measurement require. Act 55 of 2024 was an affordability mandate on the drug pricing front: cap pharmaceutical manufacturer price increases and establish GMCB affordability review authority, generating an estimated $135M in Vermont savings. The AHEAD Model State Agreement of January 2025 was a federal partnership mandate: Vermont and CMS signed the agreement establishing all-payer global budget accountability for Medicare FFS and cementing the federal counterpart to Act 68's state-level all-payer mandate. Act 62 of 2025 transferred Vermont's Health Information Technology Strategic Plan authority from GMCB to DVHA, aligning technology governance with AHEAD implementation authority. Act 68 of 2025 was the operational mandate: specific tools — reference-based pricing, global hospital budgets, a Statewide Health Care Delivery Strategic Plan — were authorized and given statutory deadlines with real consequences for non-compliance. Each act built on the evidence and institutional capacity developed by its predecessor. The whole sequence constitutes what policy scholars call a reform cascade: a legislatively structured process in which each intervention creates the conditions for the next. The HTR platform's Vermont Reform Cascade tracker at /vermont-act-68 provides an interactive visual of this sequence with current milestone status.

## Section B — Act 55 of 2024: Pharmaceutical Affordability

**The Drug Pricing Dimension**

Vermont's affordability crisis is driven primarily by hospital prices — the balloon squeeze dynamic described in the RBP section — but pharmaceutical costs are a compounding factor. The Oliver Wyman analysis estimated that prescription drug costs account for 20-25% of premium inflation, with a smaller but still material portion of the total premium crisis attributable to manufacturer price increases above general inflation.

Act 55 of 2024 addressed this dimension with targeted pharmaceutical pricing controls:

- **Price increase caps:** Pharmaceutical manufacturers are prohibited from increasing drug prices above the Consumer Price Index without GMCB affordability review. Manufacturers triggering the review threshold must justify the increase or negotiate a Vermont-specific rebate.
- **GMCB affordability review authority:** The Green Mountain Care Board received expanded authority to conduct affordability reviews for high-cost drug launches and price increases, with the power to require manufacturer rebates to Vermont payers.
- **Estimated savings:** GMCB estimates $135M in Vermont savings over five years — approximately $200-250 per Vermont household annually.

Act 55 is significant not primarily for its direct savings — hospital prices drive more premium inflation than drug prices — but for the institutional precedent it establishes. Vermont demonstrated that GMCB's affordability review authority can be successfully applied to pharmaceutical manufacturers, not just hospitals and insurers. The precedent matters for future regulatory design.

## Section C — AHEAD Model State Agreement: January 2025

**The Federal Counterpart**

The legislative reform cascade is the state-level story. The federal counterpart is the AHEAD Model State Agreement, signed between Vermont and CMS in January 2025 — the document that transformed Vermont's global budget aspiration from state policy into a federal contractual obligation with performance accountability.

The AHEAD (All-payer Health Equity Approaches and Development) Model is CMS's primary vehicle for testing all-payer global budget accountability in participating states. Vermont's participation, secured through a 9-year agreement covering 2025-2033, establishes:

- **Total cost of care accountability** for Medicare FFS beneficiaries attributed to Vermont's participating hospitals and primary care organizations
- **Primary care investment floor requirements** — participating hospitals must maintain or increase primary care investment as a percentage of total medical expenditure
- **Health equity benchmark targets** by Hospital Service Area, requiring measurable improvement in HEDIS disparity measures for attributed Medicare populations
- **Vermont's per-beneficiary benchmark:** CMS establishes an annual per-beneficiary target for Medicare FFS spending; Vermont earns shared savings or pays shared losses based on performance against this target
- **Medicaid alignment:** Vermont's Medicaid program is participating under a coordinated state plan, creating genuine all-payer accountability (Medicare FFS, Medicaid, and commercial under Act 68's RBP mandate)

The AHEAD State Agreement is the federal counterpart to Act 68 in a precise sense: Act 68 mandates reference-based pricing and global budgets for commercial and Medicaid payers at the state level; the AHEAD Agreement establishes Medicare FFS global budget accountability at the federal level. Together, they constitute the all-payer global budget architecture that Vermont has been attempting to build since Act 48 created GMCB in 2011.

**AHEAD and the 14-Hospital Transformation**

The AHEAD agreement structures Vermont's 14 hospitals differently based on size and current financial position. Critical Access Hospitals (CAHs) — 8 of Vermont's 14 hospitals — receive cost-based reimbursement from Medicare and are treated differently under AHEAD's attribution rules than non-CAH PPS hospitals. This creates a two-track transformation:

- **Non-CAH hospitals** (CVMC, Northwestern, Porter, Southwestern, Rutland Regional, Gifford when converted, Northwestern, and UVMMC network): Full global budget accountability under AHEAD and Act 68's mandatory budget requirement beginning FY2028
- **CAH hospitals:** Exempt from Act 68's global budget mandate (global budgets would undermine the cost-based reimbursement model), but still subject to AHEAD's total cost of care accountability and primary care investment requirements

The AHEAD framework also provides the quality measurement infrastructure that Act 68's accountability mechanisms rely on. Vermont hospitals will be assessed against AHEAD equity benchmarks, primary care investment floors, and HEDIS quality targets — creating a unified performance accountability system across federal and state programs.

## Section D — Act 62 of 2025: HIE Governance and Technology Alignment

**Aligning Technology Governance with Transformation Authority**

Act 62 of 2025 is the least visible of the five reform cascade acts — a governance transfer rather than a programmatic mandate — but its strategic significance is larger than its low profile suggests.

Prior to Act 62, responsibility for Vermont's statewide Health Information Technology Strategic Plan resided with the Green Mountain Care Board. GMCB is Vermont's all-payer rate-setting and oversight body — the appropriate home for healthcare pricing and global budget authority, but not necessarily the most operationally positioned agency to lead healthcare technology strategy.

Act 62 transferred the HIT Strategic Plan authority to the Department of Vermont Health Access (DVHA), effective July 1, 2025, with an annual revision requirement (November 1 deadline) and a five-year comprehensive update cycle. The rationale for the transfer is operational: DVHA manages Vermont's Medicaid program, administers the Blueprint for Health initiative (which runs Vermont's primary care practice transformation), and will be the primary state agency responsible for Vermont's AHEAD implementation. HIT strategy that is disconnected from these operational relationships — as GMCB-housed strategy necessarily was — cannot be effectively aligned with the actual technology needs of transformation implementation.

The practical implications for Vermont's 14-hospital transformation include:

- **AHEAD analytics alignment:** DVHA's AHEAD data reporting requirements and HIE connectivity expectations will now be designed by the same agency that owns the statewide HIT strategy — eliminating the coordination failure between GMCB rate-setting requirements and DVHA implementation requirements
- **CIN architecture decisions:** The Vermont Clinical Integration Network's technology architecture — which FHIR implementation approach, which population health platform, which analytics vendor — will be guided by DVHA's HIT Strategic Plan rather than a GMCB plan that lacks operational AHEAD context
- **VITL/VHIE connectivity mandates:** Act 68's mandatory HIE connectivity provision for Vermont hospitals (requiring all hospitals to maintain active VITL/VHIE participation) is more effectively implemented when HIT strategy is aligned with DVHA's operational enforcement authority

Act 62 is, in the language of organizational design, an obstacle removal: it eliminates an institutional seam between technology strategy and technology implementation that would otherwise create friction in AHEAD implementation.

## Section E — H.R. 1 and the Federal Dimension: The Medicaid Cliff

**The Federal Variable in Vermont's Transformation**

Vermont's reform cascade is a state-constructed legislative and regulatory architecture. But it does not operate in a federal vacuum. The most significant federal development for Vermont's transformation trajectory is H.R. 1 — the One Big Beautiful Bill Act signed by President Trump on July 4, 2025 — which combines $50 billion in Rural Health Transformation investment with $911 billion in Medicaid cuts over 10 years.

Vermont's $195 million RHT award (December 2025) is addressed separately in the Rural Health Transformation Program section. This section addresses the Medicaid cut dimension, which creates what Vermont policymakers are calling the "Post-2030 Medicaid Cliff."

**The Structure of H.R. 1's Medicaid Cuts**

H.R. 1 achieves its $911 billion in Medicaid savings over 10 years through several mechanisms:

- **Work requirements:** Medicaid adults aged 19-55 without dependent children must document 80+ hours of monthly work, community service, or vocational training. CMS estimates 10-15% of current Medicaid adults will lose coverage through administrative burden even if technically eligible.
- **Waiver accountability restrictions:** New limits on state Medicaid waiver authority restrict states' ability to use 1115 waivers to expand coverage beyond statutory eligibility categories.
- **Provider tax restrictions:** Limits on states' ability to use provider taxes to generate federal matching funds affect hospital financing in states like Vermont that rely heavily on these mechanisms.
- **Per-capita cap proposals:** While full block grant conversion did not pass, per-capita spending caps on certain Medicaid populations create budget exposure for high-cost states.

For Vermont, the direct Medicaid coverage impact is more limited than in many states — Vermont's Medicaid expansion penetration is among the highest in the country, but Vermont's Medicaid population is also heavily composed of elderly and disabled individuals who are exempt from work requirements. CBO estimates Vermont's coverage loss at 4-6% of current Medicaid enrollment.

**The Hospital Revenue Impact**

The more consequential impact for Vermont hospitals is on revenue, not just coverage. Vermont's 14 hospitals collectively receive approximately 28-32% of net revenue from Medicaid — significantly higher than the national average of 18-20%. This reflects Vermont's high Medicaid penetration, the high concentration of elderly patients in rural Vermont communities, and CAH cost-based reimbursement which includes Medicaid at its cost basis.

A 10-12% reduction in Medicaid reimbursement rates — which CBO projects as the likely revenue effect of H.R. 1's combination of work requirements (coverage reduction), provider tax limits (lower matching funds), and per-capita pressure — would reduce Vermont hospital Medicaid revenues by an estimated $85-115 million annually. Against the already-projected $2.4 billion five-year deficit baseline from Oliver Wyman, this represents a significant additional headwind.

**The Post-2030 Cliff Dynamic**

Vermont's reform cascade is timed for completion by 2030: RBP by FY2027, global budgets by FY2028-2030, Statewide Strategic Plan by December 2028. The implicit assumption in this timeline is that Vermont hospitals will be operating in a reformed, sustainable payment environment by 2030.

H.R. 1 disrupts this timeline logic in two ways. First, the RHT Program funding (Vermont's $195 million) is one-time investment capital; it expires after the program period. Second, H.R. 1's Medicaid cuts are ongoing — they do not expire but rather represent a permanent reduction in federal Medicaid support. The combination means that Vermont hospitals receive a one-time RHT investment boost (FY2026-2029) followed by a permanent Medicaid revenue reduction (post-2030) at precisely the moment when the reform structures are expected to provide financial stability.

Vermont policymakers are acutely aware of this dynamic. The explicit framing in Vermont's RHT application — "transformation infrastructure investment, not revenue replacement" — reflects the understanding that RHT funds must build capabilities that reduce hospital cost structures and improve revenue diversification by 2030, so that the Medicaid cliff is absorbed into a transformed system rather than destabilizing a system that has merely received temporary support.

**Six-Pillar Implications**

The H.R. 1 Medicaid cliff has implications across all six pillars:

- **Policy pillar:** Vermont may need to file 1115 waiver amendments to maintain coverage for populations affected by work requirements. The Policy Simulator's Medicaid expansion scenario module models these coverage impacts.
- **Economics pillar:** Hospital financial modeling through 2030 must incorporate H.R. 1 Medicaid revenue reduction assumptions. The Hospital Financial Stress Test's H.R. 1 Medicaid Cliff preset models a 12% Medicaid rate reduction with concurrent volume changes.
- **Technology pillar:** RHT-funded technology investments (AI scribe, RPM, FHIR infrastructure) must be designed to reduce labor and overhead costs sufficiently to absorb post-2030 revenue reductions.
- **Clinical pillar:** Care delivery transformation — moving care out of hospitals into lower-cost community settings — is both a clinical quality improvement and a cost reduction strategy that Vermont must accelerate to prepare for the post-2030 revenue environment.
- **Equity pillar:** Medicaid coverage losses from work requirements will fall disproportionately on working-age adults with unstable employment — a population with high chronic disease burden and significant equity concerns. Vermont's AHEAD equity benchmarks may become harder to achieve as covered population composition shifts.
- **Operations pillar:** Hospital revenue cycle management becomes significantly more complex with work requirement administrative processing, coverage loss management, and charity care policy redesign.
