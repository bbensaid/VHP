## Section 2A: The $195M Financing Architecture — Vermont's Rural Health Transformation Program

### From Planning to Capital Deployment

The Transformation Planning Sequence described above creates the strategic map — 14 hospitals, phased from data review to final implementation plans, converging on a Statewide Strategic Plan by December 2028. What moves a plan off paper and into operational reality is capital. Vermont's transformation is unusual among state reform efforts in one respect: it has identified, competed for, and won a substantial federal capital investment precisely when capital is most scarce and the implementation mandate is most urgent.

The Rural Health Transformation Program — authorized by Congress in H.R. 1 (the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, signed July 4, 2025) as a $50 billion national program, with Vermont awarded $195 million in December 2025 — is the financial bridge between Act 68's statutory mandates and their operational execution. Understanding the RHT Program's structure, investment categories, and strategic constraints is essential to understanding how Vermont's transformation will actually be financed.

### The Investment-Not-Revenue-Replacement Principle

The most important conceptual distinction in the RHT Program is embedded in its name: *transformation*, not *stabilization*. Federal program guidance and Vermont's application are both explicit: RHT capital is not designed to replace lost operating revenue. It is not a hospital bailout. It is not a mechanism for sustaining the current system configuration longer than the market and reform environment would otherwise allow.

This distinction has direct operational consequences for how hospitals and AHS should approach RHT capital allocation:

**What RHT funds can do:**
- Finance the one-time costs of care redesign — building new primary care capacity, converting inpatient beds to outpatient or REH configurations, standing up telehealth and RPM infrastructure
- Invest in workforce pipeline development — tuition assistance that builds a locally-rooted provider supply over a 5-10 year horizon
- Fund shared service infrastructure — IT systems, billing platforms, supply chain platforms — that reduce long-run operating costs
- Support EMS transformation and inter-facility transfer infrastructure that enables regionalization
- Finance FHIR/HIT connectivity to VITL/VHIE and AHEAD reporting requirements
- Pay for the AHS management and oversight capacity that transformation requires

**What RHT funds cannot do:**
- Substitute for the operating margin improvement that RBP and global budget reform must deliver
- Fund ongoing salary costs beyond the transitional period specified in program guidance
- Replace the revenue reduction from H.R. 1's $911 billion in Medicaid cuts on a permanent basis
- Sustain current service configurations that Act 68's regionalization framework has identified as misaligned

The distinction between investment and subsidy is not just a compliance matter — it is the difference between a $195M award that accelerates transformation and one that defers it. Vermont's RHT Program application, reviewed and approved by HRSA in December 2025, reflects AHS's commitment to the investment framework.

### The Six RHT Investment Categories

Vermont's $195M application organized investments into six operational domains. Each is described here with its transformation rationale — the link between the capital investment and the durable operating improvement it is designed to produce.

#### 1. Clinical Informatics and AI-Assisted Care Delivery ($38M)

AI scribe technology, clinical decision support, and automated care gap closure tools reduce the documentation burden that is consuming an estimated 30-40% of physician time in Vermont's current care environment. Vermont's hospitals operate with thin physician-to-patient ratios — the workforce shortage documented in Section 5 is already acute — and the effective capacity of the existing physician workforce is compressed by administrative requirements that AI tools can reduce.

**Transformation rationale:** Every hour of physician time recovered from documentation burden is an hour available for patient care. At Vermont's per-physician productivity values, AI scribe deployment across Vermont's 14 hospitals is estimated to recover sufficient physician capacity to defer 30-40 FTE equivalent hires during the critical 2026-2030 transformation period — deferring hiring costs at a time when the workforce market is most expensive.

**Post-RHT sustainability:** AI scribe and CDS tools are SaaS subscription products with ongoing licensing costs. The business case requires that the productivity gains per FTE produce documented time savings that translate to either reduced overtime, reduced locum costs, or increased panel capacity — each of which has a measurable revenue or cost equivalent. Vermont's RHT application requires participating hospitals to document productivity baselines before deployment.

#### 2. Remote Patient Monitoring and Telehealth Infrastructure ($42M)

RPM infrastructure investment — devices, connectivity, monitoring platforms, and care team integration — enables Vermont's primary care practices to manage high-risk chronic disease patients in their homes rather than in emergency departments. Vermont's ED visit rate for potentially avoidable conditions (32.3% of all ED visits are classified as avoidable) is the most actionable metric in the transformation outcome framework: RPM and telehealth interventions have a documented evidence base for reducing avoidable utilization in rural populations.

**Transformation rationale:** Vermont's geography creates utilization inefficiencies that are genuinely addressable through technology. A patient in the Northeast Kingdom managing COPD or heart failure with RPM monitoring and telehealth check-ins generates a fraction of the facility utilization that the same patient generates when the only care touchpoints are ED presentations and inpatient admissions. The AHEAD global budget explicitly rewards reduced total cost of care — and RPM-enabled reduction in avoidable utilization is one of the highest-confidence mechanisms for achieving that reduction.

**Post-RHT sustainability:** CMS CPT codes 99453-99458 provide billing infrastructure for RPM services in fee-for-service Medicare. Under AHEAD's global budget, reduced utilization translates directly to improved financial performance against benchmark. Vermont's RHT application includes the billing infrastructure buildout required to capture these revenue streams sustainably.

#### 3. Broadband and Connectivity Infrastructure ($28M)

Broadband investment is the infrastructure prerequisite for telehealth, RPM, and FHIR/HIT connectivity in Vermont's most rural communities. The Northeast Kingdom counties — Essex, Caledonia, Orleans — where uninsurance rates run 6-8% above statewide average and where transportation distances to the nearest RSC can exceed 90 minutes, are also the counties with the least reliable broadband connectivity. Telehealth without connectivity is not a care delivery model; it is a theoretical construct.

**Transformation rationale:** This is the clearest example of infrastructure investment — capital that creates the physical capacity for other investments to function. Broadband investment does not directly reduce a healthcare cost or improve a clinical outcome; it enables the investments that do. The business case is straightforward: the full return on AI scribe, RPM, and telehealth investments is only achievable if the underlying connectivity infrastructure is reliable.

**Post-RHT sustainability:** Broadband infrastructure, once built, has minimal ongoing operating costs relative to the utilization value it enables. Vermont's RHT broadband investments are coordinated with the state's existing ConnectVermont initiative to avoid duplication and target the highest-gap communities.

#### 4. Clinical Integration Network and Shared Services Infrastructure ($35M)

CIN development — including shared IT platforms, supply chain consolidation, administrative shared services (billing, coding, credentialing, human resources), and care coordination infrastructure — is the operational mechanism for realizing the $100M+ administrative cost savings that Oliver Wyman identified as achievable from the $1,303 per-discharge administrative cost gap.

**Transformation rationale:** Vermont's 14 hospitals each operate their own administrative infrastructure. Supply chain is not consolidated. Billing platforms are hospital-specific. HR and credentialing functions are duplicated across organizations that compete for the same limited pool of administrative talent. The shared services investment funds the platform migration and change management required to consolidate these functions — a one-time cost that produces permanent per-discharge savings once operational.

**Post-RHT sustainability:** Shared services models, once operational, are self-sustaining from the cost savings they generate. The RHT investment funds the transition; the ongoing operating model is cost-neutral relative to current fragmented operations at lower volume and self-funding at scale.

#### 5. EMS Transformation and Inter-Facility Transfer Coordination ($22M)

Oliver Wyman documented 31 separate EMS agencies that a Vermont provider may need to contact to arrange a patient transfer. This fragmentation is simultaneously a patient safety risk — delayed transfers to higher-acuity care for time-sensitive conditions — and an operational cost driver. Vermont's RHT Program investment in EMS transformation addresses both dimensions: professionalizing and regionalizing EMS agency structures, building inter-facility transfer coordination protocols, and expanding telehealth to enable remote clinical assessment that reduces unnecessary transfers.

**Transformation rationale:** Regionalization is only operationally viable if the transfer infrastructure that moves patients between Tier 2/3 facilities and Tier 1 RSCs functions reliably. The 31-agency fragmentation is the primary operational barrier to the regional hub-and-spoke model that Vermont's transformation requires. EMS investment is not an add-on to the regionalization strategy — it is the enabling infrastructure.

**Post-RHT sustainability:** EMS transformation produces sustainable operational savings through reduced duplicative agency overhead and reduced per-transfer costs. AHS's EMS consolidation roadmap, developed with RHRC, targets a 10-12 agency regional structure from the current 31 — reducing aggregate EMS operating costs while improving response capacity.

#### 6. Workforce Pipeline Development ($30M)

The workforce investment category is described in detail in Section 5 of this chapter (RHT Workforce Investment Priorities). The five investment areas — tuition assistance, clinical training infrastructure, scope-of-practice training, community health worker deployment, and IMLC participation — are each designed to produce durable workforce supply rather than temporary staffing.

**Transformation rationale:** Vermont's workforce shortage — the primary finding of Oliver Wyman's analysis — is a structural supply problem that temporary incentives cannot solve. Rural communities need providers who trained in Vermont, whose families and networks are in Vermont, and whose professional identity is embedded in the communities they serve. Tuition assistance with 5-year service obligations builds exactly that supply — at a per-provider cost that is a fraction of the lifetime economic value of a locally-rooted rural physician.

**Post-RHT sustainability:** Workforce pipeline investments produce returns over a 10-20 year horizon, well beyond the RHT program period. The obligation structures in Vermont's tuition assistance design — 5-year service commitments with graduated repayment structures for early departure — create provider retention incentives that continue after the capital investment period ends.

### The Post-2030 Medicaid Cliff: The Strategic Constraint on RHT Deployment

Vermont's RHT Program planning occurs in the shadow of a structural threat that its $195M award cannot fully offset: the permanent reduction in Medicaid funding enacted in H.R. 1. While the RHT Program provides capital to invest in transformation infrastructure through approximately 2030-2032, H.R. 1's work requirements, provider tax restrictions, and base rate adjustments produce Medicaid revenue reductions that are permanent and that intensify over time as RHT capital is exhausted.

The Post-2030 Medicaid Cliff — the scenario modeled in the Hospital Financial Stress Test's H.R. 1 preset — represents the operational planning horizon that AHS's capital allocation decisions must address. The correct framework for evaluating RHT investments is not "will this generate positive ROI by 2028?" but "will this produce sufficient durable operating improvement that Vermont's hospital system remains viable after RHT capital is exhausted and permanent Medicaid funding cuts are the baseline operating environment?"

This framing has direct implications for investment prioritization:

**High-priority investments under Post-2030 lens:** Those that reduce the operating cost structure of Vermont's hospital system permanently — administrative shared services, workforce pipeline development (reduces locum dependency), CIN/HIE infrastructure (reduces per-encounter administrative cost), RPM/telehealth (reduces avoidable utilization that drives fixed-cost facility overhead).

**Caution-warranted investments under Post-2030 lens:** Those that create ongoing SaaS or subscription dependencies without a documented path to cost savings that exceed ongoing licensing costs — particularly if those licensing costs are borne by hospitals that will simultaneously be facing reduced Medicaid revenue post-2030.

Vermont's AHEAD global budget framework creates a partial hedge: as total cost of care is reduced through the investments above, the global budget benchmark improves, and Vermont hospitals capture shared savings against a lower cost base. The AHEAD model is designed precisely so that the investment and the financial reward are aligned — but this alignment only functions if the investments are the right ones, executed in sequence, with the operational discipline to realize the savings they project.

### The RHT Program and Vermont's AHEAD Alignment

Vermont's AHEAD agreement and its RHT Program are not parallel tracks — they are the same transformation viewed from two different federal program authorities. AHEAD provides the payment architecture: the global budget structure, the EAST Fund for SDOH and behavioral health investment, the equity benchmarks and reporting requirements, the CMS partnership on Medicaid alignment. RHT provides the capital infrastructure: the one-time investment funds that build the clinical, technological, and workforce capacity to succeed under the AHEAD payment model.

This alignment is explicit in Vermont's RHT application: each of the six investment categories maps directly to an AHEAD performance requirement. AI scribe and CDS tools improve quality documentation for AHEAD's stratified HEDIS reporting requirements. RPM and telehealth reduce the avoidable utilization that the AHEAD total cost of care benchmark rewards. CIN and shared services infrastructure enables the population health management data flows that AHEAD attribution and reporting require. EMS transformation enables the regionalized care delivery model that AHEAD's primary care investment floor is designed to build. Workforce development addresses the supply constraint that would otherwise limit the reach of AHEAD's primary care expansion.

The operational lesson from other state global budget experiences — Maryland, Pennsylvania, Oregon — is that the financial architecture of global budgets does not, by itself, produce transformation. The payment reform creates the incentive; the operational investments create the capacity. Vermont's combination of AHEAD payment reform and RHT capital investment is, in that sense, the complete model. The implementation challenge is executing the capital investments quickly enough and correctly enough that they produce the operational improvements that AHEAD's financial architecture rewards — before the H.R. 1 Medicaid cliff requires Vermont's hospital system to function without the capital bridge.
