## 1. What Is HTR? The Case for a New Kind of Platform

### The Problem HTR Was Built to Solve

The United States healthcare system is the most expensive among all peer nations — spending exceeds **$14,000 per person per year**, roughly twice the OECD average — yet the U.S. ranks last or near-last on access, equity, infant mortality, and preventable death. Vermont is not immune to this structural failure. Vermont's hospital system saw 9 of its 14 hospitals operating at a loss in 2023, with 13 of 14 projected to lose money by 2028. Commercial premiums have risen 108% in six years. The 65+ population is projected to grow 57% by 2040 while the working-age population shrinks.

This is not a Vermont-specific problem. It is the U.S. healthcare system behaving exactly as its structure predicts.

The deeper problem is analytical fragmentation. Healthcare is covered by policy journalists who don't understand the actuarial implications of what they're reporting. It is analyzed by health economists who model financial flows without accounting for technology readiness. It is studied by clinical journals that examine care delivery without attending to equity consequences. It is tracked by investors who screen digital health without a policy lens.

The result: every decision-maker — whether a state Medicaid director, a hospital CFO, a GMCB board member, a legislator, or a community health researcher — is working with an incomplete map. They are making consequential decisions with fragmented information.

### What HTR Is

**Health Transformation Review (HTR) is a unified intelligence and analytics platform** built to give healthcare decision-makers the complete analytical picture. It combines:

- A **continuously updated intelligence feed** of curated analysis and reporting across all six pillars of healthcare transformation
- **19 interactive research tools** — calculators, simulators, optimizers, and modeling environments — for hands-on analytical work
- An **AI Analyst** powered by a purpose-built knowledge base of healthcare policy, clinical research, payment reform, and state-specific data (including Vermont-specific documents)
- **State-by-state performance data** for all 50 states on transformation readiness metrics
- A **structured learning system** (Academy) with courses, modules, tracks, and certifications
- **Vermont-specific analysis** — including the most comprehensive public modeling tool for Vermont's Act 167 hospital transformation and the most detailed Medicaid eligibility resource in the state

### Why Vermont Reviewers Should Care

Vermont is, by HTR's measurement, the highest-scoring state in the nation on healthcare transformation infrastructure — scoring **82 out of 100** on the Health Transformation Index (HTI) as of Q1 2026. Vermont leads the nation in policy innovation, all-payer model participation, and equity-adjusted access metrics.

But Vermont simultaneously faces an existential financial sustainability crisis — a paradox that perfectly illustrates why transformation *infrastructure* and financial *health* must be measured separately.

HTR is the only platform that holds both of these realities in view simultaneously, gives policymakers the tools to model reform pathways, and provides the evidence base to support every consequential decision Vermont faces in the next 36 months.

## 2. The Six-Pillar Framework — HTR's Organizing Logic

Everything in HTR is organized around **six analytical pillars**. Before navigating the platform, understanding this framework will help you understand *why* information is organized the way it is — and why that organization matters.

### Why Six Pillars?

Healthcare transformation is a **systemic challenge**. A policy that looks good from a legislative perspective may be economically unviable. A clinically proven intervention may be impossible to execute operationally. A technology platform that promises efficiency may amplify health disparities if deployed carelessly. Every pillar checks every other pillar.

HTR's six-pillar framework enforces this cross-disciplinary rigor on every analysis.

---

### Pillar 1 — Policy (Sky Blue)
**What it covers:** Federal and state legislation, CMS payment rules, Medicaid waiver programs (1115, 1115A, 1332), CMMI innovation models, state price transparency laws, prior authorization reform, scope of practice legislation.

**Why it matters for Vermont:** Vermont's AHEAD model is a CMMI 1115A agreement — a federal waiver that governs how Vermont's global budget is structured and enforced. Vermont's Act 167 recommendations include numerous legislative changes (CON reform, reference-based pricing, EMS transformation, APRN scope of practice expansion). Every one of these has a policy architecture that determines whether it works as intended.

**The pillar question:** *What are the rules, and are they the right ones?*

---

### Pillar 2 — Economics (Emerald Green)
**What it covers:** Value-based care contract design, alternative payment models (APMs), hospital and payer financial performance, pharmaceutical pricing, actuarial analysis, cost-effectiveness methodology.

**Why it matters for Vermont:** Vermont hospitals have a median operating margin of **-2.1%**, compared to a national rural hospital median of **+0.8%**. Commercial premium growth of 108% over six years has created one of the highest premium-to-income burden ratios in the country. The economic pillar is where HTR models what Vermont's global budget should actually be, how reference-based pricing would change hospital revenue, and what a glide path to financial sustainability looks like.

**The pillar question:** *Is it financially viable — and for whom?*

---

### Pillar 3 — Technology (Indigo)
**What it covers:** EHR systems and interoperability (FHIR, HL7), artificial intelligence in clinical and administrative settings, telehealth and remote patient monitoring, health data privacy, cybersecurity, clinical decision support systems.

**Why it matters for Vermont:** Vermont's 14 hospitals use multiple different EHR systems with inconsistent interoperability implementation. A functioning global budget requires real-time all-payer claims data — and Vermont's claims data infrastructure (VHCURES) is chronically underfunded. The technology pillar is where HTR identifies the infrastructure prerequisites for every clinical and financial reform Vermont is considering.

**The pillar question:** *Does the technology infrastructure support the reform?*

---

### Pillar 4 — Clinical (Red)
**What it covers:** Hospital operations and quality metrics, value-based care delivery models, clinical quality measurement (HEDIS, CMS Stars, MIPS), workforce supply and distribution, clinical guideline adherence, post-acute care integration.

**Why it matters for Vermont:** Vermont's behavioral health crisis — with rates of mental health conditions and substance use disorders exceeding the national average — is the highest-leverage clinical investment the state can make. Unaddressed behavioral health conditions are a primary driver of high-cost utilization. The clinical pillar is where HTR models how care delivery redesign translates into actual health outcomes.

**The pillar question:** *Does it improve care?*

---

### Pillar 5 — Equity (Amber)
**What it covers:** Racial and ethnic health disparities, social determinants of health (housing, food security, transportation, income, education), geographic access gaps (rural/urban divide), community health worker programs, equity measurement.

**Why it matters for Vermont:** Vermont's equity profile is paradoxical — one of the whitest states in the U.S. yet with significant disparities along rural/urban, income, and LGBTQ+ dimensions. Rural Vermonters face systematically worse access and higher out-of-pocket costs as a percentage of income. Any global budget design that does not include equity adjustors will allow budget savings to accrue in urban academic medical centers while high-need rural populations go underserved.

**The pillar question:** *Who does this help — and who does it leave behind?*

---

### Pillar 6 — Operations (Slate/Gray)
**What it covers:** Revenue cycle management (billing, coding, claims, denials, prior authorization), workforce operations (staffing models, credentialing pipelines, vacancy and turnover), quality and compliance infrastructure, supply chain and facilities, payer and network operations.

**Why it matters for Vermont:** Operations is the execution test that every other pillar must pass. A reform can be legally permissible, economically funded, technologically capable, clinically proven, and equitably designed — and still fail if the operational infrastructure cannot execute it. Vermont hospitals facing conversion to Rural Emergency Hospital (REH) status need a detailed operational plan covering revenue cycle adaptation, credentialing pipeline, transportation logistics, and supply chain adjustment. The operations pillar is where transformation plans either survive contact with reality or collapse.

**The pillar question:** *Can it actually be executed?*

---

### How the Pillars Interact — The Vermont Example

Vermont's AHEAD global budget model is the most ambitious healthcare reform experiment in the country. Analyzing it correctly requires all six pillars simultaneously:

| Pillar | AHEAD Model Question |
|---|---|
| **Policy** | Is the 1115A waiver structure achieving its legislative intent? |
| **Economics** | How is the global budget benchmark set — and does it create the right incentives? |
| **Technology** | Can Vermont's hospitals produce the all-payer claims data required for budget management? |
| **Clinical** | What care delivery changes are needed to bend the cost curve without reducing quality? |
| **Equity** | Does the global budget create incentives to avoid high-cost, high-need rural patients? |
| **Operations** | Do Vermont's hospitals have the revenue cycle infrastructure to function under a global budget? |

An analyst examining only the waiver language misses the actuarial problems. An economist modeling only the financial flows misses the clinical implementation barriers. HTR's platform ensures all six dimensions are always in view.

Six cards below the ticker show each pillar's sub-index score with its own trend arrow. Each card shows:

- **Trend arrow**
- **A one-line summary** of the most significant development driving the current score

### How the HTI Is Calculated

The HTI is a weighted composite using data from federal sources (CMS, AHRQ, ONC, HRSA, Census Bureau) and HTR's proprietary scoring methodology:

| Pillar | Weight | Primary Data Sources |
|---|---|---|
| Policy | 20% | CMMI participation data, KFF state health facts, CMS Medicaid data |
| Economics | 25% | HCPLAN survey, CMS Cost Reports, CMS National Health Expenditure Accounts |
| Operations | 10% | CMS cost reports, HFMA benchmark surveys, HRSA workforce data |
| Technology | 15% | ONC Health IT Dashboard, AHA IT Supplement, CMS telehealth data |
| Clinical | 20% | NCQA Quality Compass, AHRQ HCUP, CMS readmission data |
| Equity | 10% | NCQA HEQ measures, AHRQ Disparities Report, ACS |

**Important interpretive note:** A high HTI score indicates strong *transformation infrastructure* — the presence of value-based contracts, advanced payment models, interoperable technology, and equity programs. It does not measure financial sustainability. Vermont's HTI score of 82 can coexist with 13 of 14 hospitals losing money because the HTI measures whether the reform mechanisms are in place, not whether they have yet delivered financial relief.

### Methodology Link

Click the **Methodology** link on the HTI Dashboard to access the complete HTI Methodology document — a detailed technical reference explaining every component, weighting decision, data source, and normalization methodology. Reviewers with a research or policy analysis background will find this document valuable for understanding the rigor behind the scoring.

### The Medicaid Eligibility Simulator

**Direct URL:** `/medicaid-eligibility-simulator`

This is one of the most practically useful tools on the platform for Vermont reviewers focused on coverage and access.

The Eligibility Simulator is a multi-step wizard that walks through a structured set of questions and returns a **preliminary determination of which Vermont Medicaid programs a person may qualify for** — in plain language, not bureaucratic jargon.

**How to use it:**

**Step 1 — Household Composition**
Enter: number of adults in the household, number of children, ages of household members.

**Step 2 — Income**
Enter: annual household income (or monthly if that's easier). The simulator converts to the Federal Poverty Level percentage automatically and displays what % FPL your household falls at — this is the number that determines program eligibility.

**Step 3 — Circumstances**
Answer questions about: disability status, pregnancy, age (65+), immigration status, current coverage, and other qualifying factors.

**Step 4 — Results**
The simulator displays:
- Which Vermont Medicaid programs you **likely qualify for**
- Which programs you **do not qualify for** and why
- **Next steps** — specific instructions on how to apply, which office to contact, what documents to bring

**What the simulator does NOT do:** It does not make legally binding determinations. It provides preliminary guidance to help people understand their options and prepare for the official application process. Every results screen includes a prominent reminder to verify with DVHA or a certified application counselor.

**Why this matters for Vermont reviewers:** Vermont has one of the most complex Medicaid eligibility landscapes in the country, combining MAGI (Modified Adjusted Gross Income) rules for most programs with non-MAGI rules for aged/blind/disabled populations. The simulator translates this complexity into accessible guidance — potentially reducing barriers for the most vulnerable Vermonters who most need coverage.

## 9. Vermont Act 167 — The Most Important Health Policy Document in a Generation

**How to get there:** In the left sidebar, look for **Vermont Act 167** under the Vermont-specific or Tools & Resources section. Or navigate directly.

**Direct URL:** `/vermont-act-167`

### Background: What Is Act 167?

In August 2024, the Vermont Green Mountain Care Board (GMCB) commissioned Oliver Wyman / Marsh McLennan to conduct a comprehensive community engagement and analysis of Vermont's hospital system. The result — a 144-page report — is the most sweeping assessment of Vermont's healthcare transformation challenge in a generation.

The Oliver Wyman report documented:
- 9 of 14 Vermont hospitals operating at a loss in 2023
- 13 of 14 projected to lose money by 2028
- Commercial premiums up 108% over six years
- Out-of-pocket maximums up over 100% in the same period
- A structural crisis driven by demographic aging, workforce shortages, and systematic underpricing in rural care settings

Act 167 — Vermont's legislative response — creates the policy mandate for healthcare system transformation. The HTR Act 167 page is the only place in Vermont where you can find:

1. A complete, five-pillar analysis of the Oliver Wyman report's recommendations
2. HTR's independent assessment — endorsing some recommendations, modifying others, and adding new ones
3. An integrated policy simulation environment (the Simulator — covered in the next section)

### Page Structure and Navigation

The Act 167 page uses a **tabbed navigation** structure. Tabs appear as raised, browser-tab-style buttons at the top of the content area. The active tab is visually lifted. Click any tab to switch content.

**Tab 1 — Overview**
Provides the full HTR advisory analysis of Act 167, organized through the five-pillar framework:
- **Diagnostic Assessment** — Does HTR agree with the Wyman Report's problem statement? (Answer: unambiguously yes.)
- **Five-Pillar Analysis** — What each pillar says about Act 167's recommendations, where HTR agrees, where it disagrees, and what HTR adds
- **AHEAD Model Assessment** — A dedicated deep-dive on Vermont's global budget model
- **Implementation Architecture** — HTR's recommended sequencing for 2025–2028
- **HTR Recommendations Summary** — A clean table separating endorsed, modified, and new recommendations

**Tab 2 — Policy Analysis**
Detailed policy analysis covering the legislative and regulatory mechanisms behind each Act 167 recommendation — including the reference-based pricing glide path, CON reform considerations, and APRN/PA scope of practice expansion.

**Tab 3 — Economic Impact**
Financial modeling of Act 167's recommendations — projected savings, investment requirements, hospital-by-hospital impact, and the actuarial implications of the global budget redesign.

**Tab 4 — Equity & Access**
Analysis of how each Act 167 recommendation affects Vermont's most vulnerable populations — rural Vermonters, low-income households, LGBTQ+ Vermonters, and non-English-speaking communities.

**Tab 5 — Implementation**
The sequenced implementation timeline (2025 → 2028), dependencies between recommendations, "no regrets" moves that are beneficial regardless of which broader reforms are adopted, and risk factors.

**Tab 6 — Policy Simulation Lab** (links to Simulator)
Describes the simulation tool and provides a prominent "Launch Simulation Engine →" button that opens the Act 167 Simulator.

### HTR's Key Findings on Act 167

**What HTR Endorses Without Modification:**
- Housing investment (urgent, uncontroversial, cross-pillar necessity)
- EMS transformation funding
- Broadband expansion to rural areas
- APRN/PA scope of practice expansion (fastest path to primary care capacity relief)
- Uniform hospital accounting standards
- Telehealth expansion
- Behavioral health and primary care integration (HTR's highest-leverage recommendation)

**What HTR Endorses With Important Modifications:**
- **Reference-based pricing:** The Wyman Report recommends pricing at 200% of Medicare. HTR endorses the direction but recommends a **glide path**: 250% in 2026 → 220% in 2028 → 200% in 2030, tied to system-level cost savings milestones. An immediate hard cap risks precipitating the hospital closures the reform is meant to prevent.
- **CON reform:** HTR recommends site-neutral payment reform *before* simplifying Certificate of Need rules. Without site-neutral pricing, CON simplification creates incentives to build outpatient facilities that replace lost inpatient revenue — increasing total system costs by an estimated 8–12% over five years.
- **AHEAD model:** Strengthen the benchmark methodology (move from percent-of-prior-spending to cost-per-episode benchmarks), add a two-track accountability framework, and fix the equity measurement deficit.

**New Recommendations Not in the Wyman Report:**
- Mandatory All-Payer Participation: FFS Medicare must be included in the global budget for it to be a genuine budget
- Hospital Community Benefit Accountability: Link non-profit status to measurable transformation progress
- Prior Authorization Reform: Mandatory real-time PA decisions with automatic approval after 24 hours
- VHCURES Expansion: Fund and mandate all-payer claims data completeness
- FHIR API Mandate: Require all licensed Vermont providers to implement FHIR R4 interoperability
- Vermont Healthcare Retention Tax Credit: Refundable tax credit for clinicians maintaining five-year Vermont practice commitment
- LGBTQ+ Network Adequacy Requirements: Require gender-affirming care network adequacy from all licensed health plans

## 11. The AHEAD Model — Vermont's Global Budget Experiment

**Background:** The **All-payer Health Equity Approaches and Development (AHEAD)** model is Vermont's agreement with the CMS Innovation Center (CMMI) — making Vermont one of only a handful of states in the U.S. operating under a true global budget for healthcare spending.

Under AHEAD, Vermont participates in a multi-year agreement that sets a spending target for the entire Vermont healthcare system and creates accountability structures to ensure participating payers and providers stay within it. AHEAD embeds health equity requirements in its design and funds investment in primary care and behavioral health.

### HTR's Assessment of AHEAD

HTR's analysis of AHEAD — available in the Act 167 Overview page under the **AHEAD Model Assessment** section — is the most rigorous independent analysis of Vermont's global budget model available. The key findings:

**What AHEAD Gets Right:**
- The all-payer concept is structurally correct — cost containment requires all payers, not just Medicaid
- Multi-year agreement provides planning horizon for health systems
- Health equity requirements are embedded in model design
- Primary care and behavioral health infrastructure investment is funded by the model

**What AHEAD Gets Wrong (and Must Be Fixed):**

1. **The benchmark methodology problem.** Current AHEAD benchmarks are set as a percentage of prior spending. This perpetuates historical cost inefficiencies — Vermont's most expensive hospitals face the least pressure because their benchmark is anchored to their high historical cost base. A true global budget requires **cost-per-episode benchmarks**: what should it cost to manage a patient with Type 2 diabetes in Vermont? The budget should reflect that evidence-based standard, not historical spending patterns.

2. **The Medicare exclusion gap.** FFS Medicare beneficiaries represent the highest-cost, highest-utilization population in Vermont — and they are largely outside the global budget accountability structure. AHEAD covers Medicare Advantage and Medicaid but not traditional FFS Medicare. Without FFS Medicare, Vermont's "global budget" is a subset-payer model that cannot meaningfully control total system costs. Vermont's single most important federal lobbying priority for 2025–2026 should be mandatory Medicare APM participation.

3. **The accountability architecture deficit.** When hospitals exceed their global budget, the current consequence structure is insufficient to enforce compliance without threatening financial stability. Vermont needs a two-track accountability model: routine reporting for hospitals within 2% of budget, and mandatory GMCB corrective action plans for hospitals exceeding budget by more than 2%.

4. **The equity measurement gap.** The equity components of AHEAD are underspecified. "Health equity" appears as a goal without the operational measurement framework required to know whether the model is achieving it. Vermont should require annual equity reporting tied to specific SDOH and disparity metrics, with consequences for sustained equity performance failures.

## 16. Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: What does "MVP · FOR TESTING ONLY" mean? Is the platform incomplete?**
A: MVP stands for Minimum Viable Product — the first complete, functional version of the platform that is ready for external review. All analytical frameworks, Vermont-specific tools, and content are fully operational. The watermark is a signal that you are one of the first people to access the system, not that the platform is broken or unfinished. It will be removed when the platform opens to the public.

**Q: The faculty names on the Academy page show "Faculty #1," "Faculty #2," etc. Why?**
A: Faculty names are anonymized during the early-access period to protect the privacy of instructors before the platform's public launch. Role, bio, and course content remain fully visible.

**Q: Some advisory content mentions "Competitor #1," "Competitor #2," "Competitor #3." What does that mean?**
A: References to real competitor firm names have been temporarily replaced with placeholders for the early-access period for legal and strategic reasons. This affects only a small number of advisory positioning statements and does not affect any analytical content.

**Q: Is the data in the Act 167 Simulator real or synthetic?**
A: The simulator uses a combination of real data (Oliver Wyman report figures, publicly available GMCB hospital financial data, Vermont Census/ACS demographic data) and synthetic projections where actual data has not yet been fully ingested. A persistent "Beta — Synthetic Data" badge on the simulator header serves as a constant reminder. All figures are illustrative for scenario-planning purposes.

**Q: Can I share what I see in the platform?**
A: During the early-access period, please treat platform content as confidential. Do not share your access code with others. Do not publish screenshots or reports based on platform content without prior authorization from the HTR team. This restriction will be lifted at public launch.

**Q: My AI Analyst conversation disappeared. Where did it go?**
A: AI Analyst conversations are stored only in your browser's local storage — not on HTR's servers. If you clear your browser cache or switch browsers, the conversation history is lost. Use the **Download Transcript** button in the full chat interface (`/chat`) to save important conversations before clearing your browser.

**Q: Who built this platform?**
A: HTR is an independent health transformation research and advisory institution. It has no vendor relationships, no political affiliations, and no financial stake in the outcome of Vermont's healthcare reform decisions. The platform was built to serve decision-makers who need an evidence-first, complete analytical foundation — not a platform with a stake in which reforms are adopted.

**Q: How current is the data?**
A: Most federal data sources have 12–18 month reporting lags. HTR applies a currency adjustment methodology that weights more recent data more heavily. The HTI is updated quarterly. State Performance Index scores are updated semi-annually. Vermont-specific content is reviewed and updated by HTR's Vermont advisory team on a rolling basis. Data vintage is noted wherever relevant.

**Q: What happens if I find an error or a piece of content that seems wrong?**
A: This is exactly what the early-access period is for. Please contact the HTR team with specific notes — page URL, what you saw, and why you believe it may be incorrect. Your feedback directly shapes the final platform before public launch.
